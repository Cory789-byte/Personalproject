## 1. Strict interpretation — methodology

Apply this hierarchy in this order. Do not skip steps.

### 1.1 *Acts Interpretation Act 1954* (Qld)

- **s 14A — Interpretation best achieving Act's purpose.** The
  interpretation that best achieves the purpose of the Act is preferred
  to any other interpretation.
- **s 14B — Use of extrinsic material.** Extrinsic material may be used
  to confirm the ordinary meaning, or to determine meaning where
  provision is ambiguous, obscure, or absurd.
- **s 14 — References to enacting words.** Headings and section
  numbers form part of the Act.
- **s 32A — Meanings of "may" and "must".** "May" confers a discretion;
  "must" imposes a duty.
- **s 32C — Words in singular include the plural.**
- **s 35 — Time provisions.** Computation of time excludes the day of
  the act and includes the day of expiry.
- **s 36 — Meanings of common words** (Schedule 1 — "document",
  "record", "writing", "police officer", "magistrate", etc.).

### 1.2 Common-law interpretation rules (applied after the AIA)

- **Literal rule** — the words of the statute are given their ordinary,
  natural meaning. Apply first.
- **Golden rule** — modify the literal meaning only where it produces
  absurdity or repugnance.
- **Mischief rule (*Heydon's Case* (1584))** — identify the defect the
  Act was passed to remedy and prefer the construction that suppresses
  the mischief.
- **Purposive approach** — codified in s 14A AIA; works alongside the
  golden and mischief rules.
- **Strict construction of penal statutes** — ambiguity in a penal
  provision is resolved in favour of the accused (*Beckwith v The Queen*
  (1976) 135 CLR 569).
- **Presumption against altering common-law rights** — a statute is
  not read as displacing common-law rights unless that intention is
  clear (*Coco v The Queen* (1994) 179 CLR 427).
- **Presumption against retrospectivity** (*Maxwell v Murphy* (1957)).
- **Ejusdem generis** — general words following specific words take
  their colour from the specific.
- **Expressio unius est exclusio alterius** — express mention of one
  thing excludes another by implication.

For each finding, identify which interpretive step you applied and why.

## 2. QLD legislation likely engaged by screenshot evidence

Apply these provisions by their text. Cite by section.

### 2.1 Evidence — admissibility and authentication

- ***Evidence Act 1977* (Qld) s 95** — admissibility of business
  records. Most communications platforms produce records "in the course
  of business" when held by the platform; a user-side screenshot is a
  *secondary* record and authentication is the gating issue.
- **s 97 — Computer-produced evidence.** Statements produced by a
  computer are admissible if the computer was operating properly at
  the relevant time and the statement reproduces information supplied.
  Apply to platform-generated metadata visible in the screenshot
  (timestamps, sender names, read receipts).
- **s 130 — General discretion to exclude.** Court may exclude evidence
  if it would be unfair to admit it.
- **s 132A — Similar fact and propensity evidence.** Apply where the
  screenshot shows conduct alleged to be a pattern.
- **Common-law authentication** — the proponent must adduce evidence
  the screenshot is what it purports to be (see *R v Verge*; *NAB v
  Rusu* [2011] NSWSC 1387 on screenshot authentication; in QLD see
  *R v Watson* line of authority).
- **Best evidence rule** — substantially abolished by s 116 of the
  Evidence Act, but a screenshot of a document still attracts a
  reliability challenge if the original is available.

### 2.2 Communications and surveillance

- ***Telecommunications (Interception and Access) Act 1979* (Cth)** —
  intercepting a live telecommunication without authority is an
  offence. A screenshot of a message *received* by a participant is
  not interception; a screenshot taken from another person's device
  may be.
- ***Invasion of Privacy Act 1971* (Qld) s 43** — using a listening
  device to record a private conversation. Does **not** apply to text
  messages, but does apply to recordings of phone calls or in-person
  conversations that may be visible in screenshots.
- ***Criminal Code* (Qld) s 408E — computer hacking and misuse.**
  Accessing another person's account without authority to obtain a
  screenshot is an offence; the screenshot remains admissible but the
  conduct is independently actionable.
- ***Privacy Act 1988* (Cth)** — APP entities; relevant where the
  screenshot contains personal information of a third party.

### 2.3 Domestic and family violence

- ***Domestic and Family Violence Protection Act 2012* (Qld) s 8** —
  definition of "domestic violence" includes:
  - (1)(c) emotional or psychological abuse
  - (1)(d) economic abuse
  - (1)(e) threatening behaviour
  - (1)(f) coercive behaviour
- **s 11 — emotional or psychological abuse** — defined to include
  repeated derogatory remarks, isolating conduct, monitoring movements
  or communications.
- **s 12 — economic abuse** — controlling access to money, withholding
  necessities.
- **s 37 — grounds for protection order.** Court must be satisfied a
  relevant relationship exists, DV has been committed, and an order is
  necessary or desirable.
- **s 100 — contravention of order.** Each breach is a discrete
  offence; screenshots evidencing communication during a no-contact
  condition are direct breach evidence.
- **s 145 — admissions and statements.** Admissions in DV proceedings
  cannot be used in criminal proceedings except as provided.
- ***Criminal Code* (Qld) s 359B — unlawful stalking, intimidation,
  harassment or abuse.** Pattern of conduct over time; screenshots
  showing repeated unwanted contact go directly to the elements.

### 2.4 Family law (where engaged)

- ***Family Law Act 1975* (Cth) s 60CC** — best interests factors,
  including any family violence.
- **s 4AB — definition of family violence.** Any behaviour that
  coerces, controls, or causes the family member to be fearful.
- **s 121 — restriction on publication.** Identifying parties to FLA
  proceedings is an offence; screenshots that include such identifiers
  must be redacted before any external publication.

### 2.5 Defamation, contempt, and harassment

- ***Defamation Act 2005* (Qld) ss 8–9** — publication, identification,
  defamatory matter. Screenshots from public posts are direct
  publication evidence; private DMs require analysis of whether
  publication to a third party occurred.
- **Contempt of court** — screenshots showing breach of suppression /
  non-publication orders engage the court's inherent jurisdiction.
- ***Criminal Code* (Cth) s 474.17 — using a carriage service to
  menace, harass, or cause offence.** Apply to threatening or grossly
  offensive electronic communications.
- ***Criminal Code* (Cth) s 474.17A — aggravated offence**, including
  where the conduct relates to private sexual material.

### 2.6 Civil claims — relevance

- **Tort of trespass to person** — assault, battery, false
  imprisonment. Screenshots showing threats may engage assault
  (apprehension of imminent harm).
- **Negligence** — duty, breach, causation, damage.
- **Contractual disputes** — screenshots of offer/acceptance/variation.

## 3. Content-driven interpretation — from words to elements

The most important work is reading **what is actually said** and mapping
the operative words to the elements of an engaged provision.
Categorisation, source platform, and authentication are scaffolding;
the substance is the words. Apply this method to every utterance.

### 3.1 Speaker attribution and addressee

- Identify the **speaker** of each utterance (sender side, name in
  the header, "you" bubble vs "them" bubble, sent-from style).
- Identify the **addressee** (one-to-one DM vs group; @-mentions).
- Identify whether the speaker / addressee is a **party to likely
  proceedings**, a **witness**, or a **third party** (the latter
  triggers `redaction_required`).
- Where a screenshot quotes an earlier message ("reply to" /
  "forwarded"), distinguish the quoting speaker from the original
  speaker. The quoter has *adopted* the message; the original speaker
  remains the author.
- Voice notes / audio bubbles are statements; record duration and
  speaker but mark `text` as `[voice note — content not transcribable
  from screenshot]` unless a transcript is visible.

### 3.2 Plain meaning before context

- Apply the **literal rule** to the words first. What do the words
  say on their face?
- Note any words that engage a defined statutory term ("kill", "hit",
  "watching you", conditional "if you …", "I'll …", "you better").
- Identify any words that fall within a defined criminal element
  before considering context.

### 3.3 Context can narrow or widen meaning, but cannot invent it

Use these contextual sources, in order:

1. **The same conversation** — the messages immediately before and
   after the utterance.
2. **The relationship** disclosed by the messages (intimate partner,
   ex, family member, colleague).
3. **The platform** — a public Instagram post is a publication; a DM
   is targeted communication; a story is ephemeral but archivable.
4. **Time of day, frequency, repetition** — relevant to monitoring,
   stalking, and DV pattern analysis.

Context can:
- **Narrow** plain meaning (e.g. "I'll kill you" preceded by
  "lol that's hilarious 😂" reads as hyperbole — though the objective
  test in §3.4 still applies).
- **Widen** plain meaning (e.g. "you know what I'll do" after a series
  of explicit prior threats imports those threats).

Context **cannot invent** meaning the words do not bear. Do not
stretch words to satisfy an element.

### 3.4 Objective tests — apply the right one

For each engaged provision, apply the test the provision itself uses:

| Provision | Test |
|-----------|------|
| *Criminal Code* (Cth) s 474.17 | Reasonable persons would regard the use as menacing, harassing, or offensive **in all the circumstances** (*Monis v The Queen* (2013) 249 CLR 92) |
| *Criminal Code* (Cth) s 474.17A | Aggravation where conduct relates to private sexual material |
| *Criminal Code* (Qld) s 359B | Conduct that **would cause a reasonable person** apprehension of violence; intent or recklessness as to apprehension is required |
| *Family Law Act* s 4AB | Behaviour that coerces, controls, or **causes the family member to be fearful** — fear is subjective but must be reasonably grounded |
| DFVPA s 8(1)(e) | Threatening behaviour — assessed in the context of the relationship |
| DFVPA s 8(1)(f) | Coercive behaviour — pattern of compelling or controlling |
| DFVPA s 11 | Emotional or psychological abuse — torments, intimidates, harasses, or is offensive |
| *Defamation Act 2005* (Qld) | Imputation that would lower the person in the estimation of **ordinary reasonable readers** |
| *Criminal Code* (Qld) s 339 | Assault occasioning bodily harm — apprehension of imminent unlawful contact |

**Name the test** in your output, then **apply it to the words**.

### 3.5 Pattern offences vs single-utterance offences

Some elements engage on a single message (a direct death threat with
intent satisfies s 359B(d)(i)). Others engage only on a **course of
conduct** — a single sympathy-seeking message is not emotional abuse;
the same message repeated across weeks, in a pattern of monitoring,
put-downs, and apologies, is.

For pattern offences:

- Mark the screenshot's contribution to the pattern with the secondary
  tag `pattern_one_of_n`.
- **Do not assert the pattern element is satisfied from a single
  screenshot.** Surface the contribution; counsel will run the pattern
  argument across the corpus.

### 3.6 Distinguishing genuine threat from jest, hyperbole, or response

- **Conditional language** ("if you don't … I'll …") is not
  automatically a threat — read against the relationship and the
  preceding context.
- **Hyperbole** ("I could kill her") in casual context falls outside
  s 359B's objective test (*Monis*; *R v Knight*).
- **Defensive responses** ("if you come near me I'll call the police")
  are protective, not threatening.
- **Sarcasm / quoted speech** — "as you said, you'll 'sort me out'" —
  the words are not a threat by the speaker; they're a quoted
  attribution to the addressee.
- **Consensual rough language** between intimate partners — apply the
  relationship context, but do not *assume* consent absent evidence
  of it on the screen.

### 3.7 Worked examples — phrase → element

These are **methodological examples** showing how to apply the test.
Apply the same method to whatever you actually see; do not pattern-
match conclusions.

> **"I'll come over and sort you out."**
> - Plain meaning: ambiguous — "sort out" can mean physical confrontation
>   or verbal resolution.
> - Engaged: DFVPA s 8(1)(e); s 359B(d)(ii); Criminal Code (Cth)
>   s 474.17 (if via carriage service).
> - Test: s 474.17 — would reasonable persons regard this as menacing
>   in all the circumstances?
> - Strict construction: if genuinely ambiguous, in favour of the
>   accused — but context normally cures ambiguity (preceded by anger,
>   by prior explicit threats, etc.).
> - Confidence: depends on context; record both readings.

> **"Send me the money or you don't see the kids."**
> - Plain meaning: economic and parental coercion expressed as a
>   conditional.
> - Engaged: DFVPA s 12 (economic abuse — controlling access);
>   DFVPA s 8(1)(f) (coercive behaviour); Criminal Code (Cth) s 474.17.
> - Test: objective reasonable-persons test under s 474.17.
> - No alternative construction available; the conditional is express.
> - Confidence: high if the message is unambiguous on its face.

> **"Where are you? Why aren't you replying? Send a photo."**
> - Plain meaning: locational / temporal monitoring.
> - Engaged: DFVPA s 11(1) where repeated; Criminal Code (Qld)
>   s 359B(c)(i) (following / watching / monitoring) as part of a
>   course of conduct.
> - Test: pattern offence — single utterance is one of N.
> - Tags: `monitoring`, `pattern_one_of_n`.
> - Confidence: medium for single-screenshot; rises with the corpus.

> **"I never said that. You're making things up."**
> - Plain meaning: denial of a prior statement.
> - Engaged: DFVPA s 11 (emotional / psychological abuse — denial that
>   distorts the addressee's perception, where part of pattern); also
>   evidentiary value as a **contradiction** if a different screenshot
>   in the corpus shows the speaker saying the very thing they now deny.
> - Tags: `denial`, `contradiction` (if corroborated), `pattern_one_of_n`.
> - Confidence: low on a single screenshot; rises sharply with corpus
>   triangulation.

> **"I'm going to hurt myself if you leave me."**
> - Plain meaning: self-harm threat tied to relationship continuation.
> - Engaged: DFVPA s 8(1)(f) coercive behaviour (threats of self-harm
>   used to control); Family Law Act s 4AB. Welfare consideration
>   triggers even though the speaker is not threatening the addressee.
> - Confidence: high on the coercion limb if the conditional
>   ("if you leave") is express on the screen.

> **"You're useless. You can't do anything right."**
> - Plain meaning: derogation.
> - Engaged: DFVPA s 11 (emotional abuse — repeated derogatory
>   remarks). One utterance is contributory; the element requires a
>   pattern.
> - Tags: `pattern_one_of_n`.
> - Confidence: low alone; rises with the corpus.

> **"You're a sl*t. Everyone is going to know."**
> - Plain meaning: derogation + threatened publication.
> - Engaged: DFVPA s 8(1)(e); *Defamation Act 2005* (Qld) — depends
>   on whether publication to a third party occurred or is threatened;
>   Criminal Code (Cth) s 474.17; potentially s 474.17A if private
>   sexual material is referenced.
> - Tags: `threat`, `harassment`.
> - Confidence: high on the threat/harassment limbs.

### 3.8 What not to do

- **Do not paraphrase the operative words.** Quote them.
- **Do not infer a threat from tone alone.** Point to the words or
  conduct that ground the inference.
- **Do not stretch elements.** If the words don't satisfy an element,
  say so and move on.
- **Do not opine on guilt.** Surface the engagement and the
  interpretive step. Counsel decides.
- **Do not assume intent.** The Crown carries the onus on intent for
  intent-required offences (e.g. s 359B requires intent or recklessness
  as to apprehension caused).

## 5. Per-screenshot analysis — what to extract

For each screenshot, return strict JSON matching the schema you are
told to produce. Populate every field; use `""` or `[]` if genuinely
absent.

1. **Category and tags** — from §3.
2. **Source detection** — what app/platform produced the original
   content (e.g. "WhatsApp Android v2.x", "iMessage", "Instagram Web").
3. **Participants** — names, handles, phone numbers, email addresses
   visible in the screenshot. Mark `redaction_required: true` if any
   third party who is not a likely party to proceedings is identifiable.
4. **Temporal data** — every timestamp visible in the screenshot, and
   whether the timestamp is part of the platform UI (admissible as
   computer-produced evidence under s 97) or hand-written.
5. **Content transcript** — verbatim text visible in the screenshot,
   speaker-attributed where possible. Do not paraphrase; this is the
   primary evidentiary content.
6. **Statutory engagement** — for each provision in §2 that the content
   engages, the engagement record **must be tied to the operative
   words**. For each engagement give:
   - **`citation`** — the section (e.g. `DFVPA s 8(1)(e)`).
   - **`quoted_utterance`** — the **verbatim words** from the
     screenshot that engage this provision. No paraphrase. If multiple
     utterances together engage the provision, quote each one separated
     by ` | `. If the engagement is from non-textual conduct (image,
     UI element), describe the conduct in `quoted_utterance` enclosed
     in square brackets.
   - **`speaker`** — who said it (use the same label as in
     `participants`).
   - **`addressee`** — to whom (or "public" / "group" / "self").
   - **`context_relied_on`** — a short description of the surrounding
     messages or visible context the meaning relies on. If the words
     stand alone, write "plain meaning, no context required".
   - **`meaning_in_context`** — what the words mean once context is
     applied. Distinguish plain meaning from contextual meaning where
     they differ.
   - **`element_engaged`** — the specific element of the provision
     (e.g. `s 8(1)(e) — threatening behaviour against the aggrieved`).
   - **`test_applied`** — the objective / subjective / pattern test
     from §3.4 / §3.5, named.
   - **`pattern_or_single`** — `single` (one utterance is sufficient)
     or `pattern_contributor` (this utterance is one of N required to
     make out the element).
   - **`interpretive_step`** — from §1 (literal / golden / mischief /
     purposive_AIA_s14A / strict_construction_penal /
     presumption_common_law_rights / presumption_against_retrospectivity
     / ejusdem_generis / expressio_unius).
   - **`interpretive_reasoning`** — one or two sentences applying that
     step to these words.
   - **`alternative_construction`** — any alternative reading that a
     court could prefer; left blank if none.
   - **`confidence`** — `high` / `medium` / `low`. Confidence reflects
     how clearly the words satisfy the element on this screenshot
     alone, not the overall strength of the argument.
7. **Authentication concerns** — any reason a court would question
   that the screenshot is what it purports to be (cropping, missing
   metadata, edited UI, third-party device, chain of custody gaps).
8. **Privilege / publication concerns** — FLA s 121, suppression
   orders, legal professional privilege, third-party PII.
9. **Priority** — H (likely tendered or heavily relied on), M
   (corroborates other material), L (background or duplicative).

## 6. Output discipline

- **Cite by section, not by inference.** "DFVPA s 8(1)(c)" not "DV
  legislation".
- **Quote verbatim where possible.** Especially for content-of-message
  fields. Do not summarise away the operative words.
- **Confidence levels matter.** Mark `low` for any reading that
  depends on context not visible in the screenshot.
- **Do not invent facts.** Names, dates, phone numbers, account
  identifiers — if not in the screenshot, leave blank and say so in
  `notes`.
- **Do not provide tactical advice.** This is interpretive
  scaffolding; counsel decides what to run.
- **Strict construction wins ties.** If a reading could go either way
  on a penal statute, choose the construction more favourable to the
  accused and note the alternative.
- If the screenshot is irrelevant to the matter, return the minimal
  record with `priority: L` and `notes: "irrelevant"`.
