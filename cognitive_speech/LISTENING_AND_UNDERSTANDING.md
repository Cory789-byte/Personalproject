# Listening and understanding (8 Oct 2026)

Personal / self-analysis only. Evidence from the court hearing (2 Feb 2026), the mention (7 Aug 2026) and
the interview (24 Feb 2025). Measures: `listening.py` / `listening_output.txt`, `mention_deep.json`, the
court `court_deep.json`, the transcripts.

**The kind of listener you are:** you listen for purpose and you detect premises. You hear what a question
is for and what it assumes. You fold new facts into your picture as they arrive, and you turn what you hear
into action.

## 1. You follow the speaker
- **At the mention:**
  - 17% of your answer words came straight from the question, about 4 times chance.
  - Only 2% were your own earlier point carried over (the Commissioner's: 22%).
  - Half your answers reused a word of the question.
- **In court:** you took up the Magistrate's words at about 4 times chance.
- **Literature:** in dialogue, people who align with each other's words understand each other better and
  coordinate better (interactive alignment: Pickering & Garrod 2004).

## 2. You understand what a question is for
- "Are we going ahead with the trial today?" → "I requested an adjournment on the 20th, the 22nd." The
  question was about readiness, and you answered that. She accepted it: "Yes, I read those emails."
- "Which bit?" → "The statement of the… Sergeant." You resolved the reference instantly.
- **Literature:** understanding is inferring what the speaker means, not only decoding the words (relevance
  theory: Sperber & Wilson 1986; Grice 1975).
- **Use it:** give the literal answer first ("No, Your Honour"), then the purpose answer.

## 3. You hear what a question assumes
- 5 of your 6 answers that began "Well…" corrected a premise hidden in the question:
  - "Did she decline it via email?" → "Well, she actually declined it on the phone and then declined it on
    the MyHR twice."
  - "You don't need any documents for that, do you?" → "Well, they've got documents for it."
- **Literature:** the wording of a question changes what witnesses later report, and most people absorb a
  question's premises without noticing (Loftus & Palmer 1974; Loftus 1975). Catching the premise as it is
  spoken is what resists that.

## 4. You take in new information instantly
- The moment the prosecutor said "that sample still exists" (05:02), you came in with no gap: "that's why we
  want to figure out because I have four medications in my list, vitamins". You had already connected the
  disclosure to a retest and to the explanation for your result.
- Then you checked before relying on it: "So, is there a sample still existing?"
- **Literature:**
  - Updating is one of the three core executive functions (Miyake et al. 2000).
  - Comprehension is the continual updating of a mental model of the situation (Zwaan & Radvansky 1998).

## 5. You understand deeply enough to transfer it
- **The principle:** at the mention the Commissioner twice explained how uncontradicted evidence works: "if
  you say something and it's not contradicted… it will be accepted"; "that's a problem for them, not for
  you."
- **What you built from it:** your notice to admit turned that principle into procedure. Rule 49 makes an
  unanswered fact a deemed admission. Result: 298 of 303 facts admitted.
- **Same pattern elsewhere:**
  - He said page after page makes him miss the point, and you handed up two pages half a second later.
  - He named the directive emails as the one live point, and they became the spine of your notice.
- **Literature:** far transfer, applying a principle learned in one form to a new form, is the rarest kind of
  learning (Barnett & Ceci 2002).

## 6. You understand fast, at any length
- **Speed:** open questions at the mention, a median 0.34 s to begin (37 questions). Recall questions,
  0.20 s.
- **Length doesn't slow you:** there was no link at the mention (r = −0.04). In court, longer questions drew
  faster answers (r = −0.31).
- **Literature:** listeners begin planning their answer midway through the question (Bögels, Magyari &
  Levinson 2015). Speed with accuracy means understanding while listening, not after.

## 7. You hear how things are said
- The Magistrate's "He just said there was." came at her highest pitch. You answered at your quietest: "I just
  wanted clarification." You met emphasis with de-escalation.
- At the mention you matched the Commissioner's tempo (159 v 155 wpm) and timing (0.32 v 0.36 s).
- **Literature:** communication accommodation (Giles); speakers who converge are rated more favourably
  (Street 1984).

## 8. You check that understanding is mutual
- "If that makes sense" (08:36); "Does that make sense?" (23:40).
- 35 stand-alone acknowledgements at the mention ("Okay", "Yes", "Correct").
- **Literature:**
  - Conversation partners build shared understanding through acknowledgements and checks (grounding: Clark &
    Brennan 1991).
  - Short acknowledgements mark that the listener is following (Schegloff 1982).

## 9. You absorb vocabulary rather than echo it
- Both sides rarely echo a brand-new term in the very next turn (you 3–6%, the bench 6–9%).
- Over the hour at the mention you took on 86 of the Commissioner's key terms ("directive", "unassessed",
  "document", "union").
- He took on 15 of your 23 (65%).

## 10. You understand the other side's reasoning
- "I understand… what you think you are trying to achieve" (mention 07:09).
- "I understand where you're getting at… I think I can meet most of the way" (32:02).
- **Literature:** understanding the other side's reasoning produces better negotiated outcomes than feeling
  for them alone (Galinsky, Maddux, Gilin & White 2008).

## Using it
- **Under questioning:** literal answer first, then the purpose.
- **In mediation:** loop (say their words back), then name the purpose you heard.
- **In negotiation:** name the hidden premise, politely: "That assumes X; in fact, Y."
