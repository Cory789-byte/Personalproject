# Did your content overwhelm them, and who ended up asking? (8 Oct 2026)

> Personal / self-analysis only. Not case material. Never served, tendered, cited or attached in
> WC/2024/227, the QPS matters or any other proceeding (discipline rule 10).
> - **Interview content is given by times and counts only.** The transcript stays outside the repo.
> - **Script:** `content_load_and_roles.py`; output: `content_load_and_roles_output.txt`.
> - **Questions** are sentences the machine transcriber ended with "?".

**Cory's hypotheses:**
1. He overwhelmed the officers with his content.
2. In the interview he became the one asking the questions, and at the mention the Commissioner became "we".

## 1. Overwhelm: supported, and it cut both ways

**Volume.**
- From 6:00 you spoke 63% of the words: 5,916 against the two officers' 3,452. Your share was 73% in the
  first stretch.
- Most of your turns were short (median 16 words; theirs 14).
- Your 25 turns of 60 words or more carried 53% of everything you said.

**What the officers did next, by the length of your turn before:**

| Your turn | They explored your point | They contested it | They did not engage | Redirected or closed the topic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Under 20 words (94) | 31% | 26% | 43% | 2% |
| 20–59 words (38) | 32% | 24% | 45% | 11% |
| 60+ words (22) | 23% | **9%** | **68%** | **23%** |

- **After your long turns they almost stopped contesting and started exiting.** Five of their seven topic
  changes came straight after a long turn of yours: 12:43 (137 words), 14:01 (91), 29:47 (121), 39:50
  (196) and 58:51 (76).
- **The accusation of lying at 44:12** came straight after your 190-word explanation of what the 000 remark
  referred to.

**What the research says:**
- **Working memory.** It holds about four chunks (Cowan 2001).
- **Interviewer load.** Interviewers under heavier cognitive load recall what they heard less accurately
  (Hanway, Akehurst, Vernham & Hope 2021). In child-abuse interviews, investigators' notes left out more than
  half of the details given (Lamb et al. 2000).
- **Load and first impressions.** Busy perceivers keep their first characterisation of a person and fail to
  correct it for the situation (Gilbert, Pelham & Krull 1988).
- **What that predicts:** overload does not make interviewers concede. It makes them fall back on their
  plan. That is what the redirects show.

**The edge that cuts against you.**
- Your short turns were engaged with almost twice as often as your long ones: 57% explored or contested,
  against 32%.
- Your sharpest moves were short. The handcuffs distinction (59:54) drew a direct reply.
- **Volume won the war of attrition** (dropped lines, wind-ups). **Brevity won the argument.**

## 2. Who asks: you moved toward the questioner's seat

| Window | Your share of the questions asked |
|---|---|
| 6–20 min | 7% |
| 20–30 min | 14% |
| 30–40 min | 28% |
| 40–50 min | 24% |
| 50–60 min | **38%** |
| 60–66 min (closing) | 19% |

- **Rate.** Your question rate tripled, from 0.21 to 0.63 a minute between the first and second halves.
  Theirs fell from 1.88 to 1.50.
- **Counter-questions.** You answered a question with a question 13 times (21:05 to 65:14).
- **Who still led.** The officers asked the majority in every window.
- **Research:**
  - In institutional talk, the right to ask questions belongs to the institution (Drew & Heritage 1992).
  - In police interviews, officers hold control through their questions (Heydon 2005, on Australian
    interviews).
  - Questions set the agenda (Freed & Ehrlich 2010).
  - A suspect who asks questions is contesting that asymmetry.

## 3. The mention: the Commissioner moved to "we"

| Quarter | His "we / us / let's" per 100 words | His "you" per 100 words | Joint "we" lines (agenda handed to you) | "We" spoken as a party |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 00:00–16:08 | 0.49 | 5.30 | 8 (0) | 0 |
| Q2 16:08–32:17 | 0.72 | 5.35 | 10 (0) | 3 |
| Q3 32:17–48:25 | 0.72 | 6.35 | 3 (0) | 8 |
| Q4 48:25–64:34 | **0.93** | 5.61 | **16 (3)** | 4 |

- **The rate nearly doubled.** His "you" stayed flat.
- **The kind of "we" changed:**
  - Q1: the procedural opening "we" ("our discussions today").
  - Q2: working on your document together and joint problem-solving ("Let's just look at line 10"; "if we
    can't work this out today").
  - Q4: handing you the agenda ("If you think we don't need to go over it, we can skip over", 54:09) and
    shared ownership ("We probably should have led with that", 53:30).
- **He also spoke your case in the first person plural,** as the staff: "she gave us these directives…
  never asked us anything" (27:54).
- **Research:**
  - "We" makes a relationship read as closer (Fitzsimons & Kay 2004).
  - "We-talk" in a dispute goes with more constructive resolution (Seider et al. 2009).
  - **Caution:** higher-status speakers use more "we" anyway (Kacewicz et al. 2014). So the rise and the
    change of kind carry the weight here, not the raw count.

## The irony, stated plainly

The same shift happened in both rooms, in opposite directions.
- **In the cooperative room, the decision-maker came across** to your side of the table: "we", and the
  agenda handed to you.
- **In the accusatory room, you went across** to theirs: from 7% of the questions to 38%.

Both are the asymmetry of an institutional conversation moving toward you.

## Limits

- **One coder.**
- **Machine punctuation** decides what counts as a question.
- **Counting turns by length** shows what followed long turns, not why. Being overwhelmed and choosing not
  to engage look the same in the counts.
- **Mention "we" lines** were hand-classified by one reader.
