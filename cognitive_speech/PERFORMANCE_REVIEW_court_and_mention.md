**One caution about judging by outcome.** Outcomes mix performance with luck. People rate the same
decision as better when it happened to turn out well (outcome bias: Baron & Hershey 1988). Here the two
hearings run in opposite directions:
- **Court:** the outcome flatters the performance. The prosecutor's disclosure was not yours.
- **Mention:** the immediate outcome understates the performance, and the later outcome restores it.

Both are scored on both.

## 1. Brisbane Magistrates Court, 2 Feb 2026 (drug-driving trial day; stay application)

### What was at stake
A trial that morning, without the sergeant's statement, his body-worn camera footage, or the sample you
had been told was destroyed. Your aims were an adjournment or stay, disclosure, and a sample that could be
tested.

### Outcome
- **In the room:**
  - The trial did not start.
  - The matter was stood down so the prosecutor could ask the lab technician about a retest.
  - The bench put three things on the record in your favour:
    - "he was told there was no viable sample" (07:10)
    - "he was first raised this back in September in emails" (08:37)
    - on whether you refused the B sample: "Which is contested" (08:55)
- **What did not happen:**
  - The stay was not decided.
  - The schedule was declined.
  - The targeting point was redirected.
  - The NATA explanation was cut off.
- **Afterwards (the file):**
  - Your FSQ statutory complaint, 1 Mar 2026.
  - The charge was still active on 4 May 2026.
  - FSQ had given three accounts of the sample ("destroyed or consumed", "consumed", "stored but not
    viable"). That matches the contradiction that surfaced in court.
  - The final result is not on file.

### What the Magistrate's words and actions show she made of you
- **She had read your emails before the hearing** ("Yes, I read those emails") and summarised your
  material ("The body-worn camera footage reveals that he didn't refuse").
- **She adopted your point three times on the record,** and asked you directly what you wanted: "Is that
  what you want to do, is get that tested?"
- **She declined the paper** ("three pages of tiny print… I'm not that interested").
- **She contained the side issues:** she cut off the NATA explanation and reframed "targeted" as
  "complaints to police… part of the profiling".
- **Read:** credible on the point that mattered, prepared, a little over-papered, and drifting into
  grievance at the end.

### Verdict
| | Grade | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome | **B+** | An interim win: no unprepared trial, and the bench on your side of the decisive contradiction. Not a final result. |
| Substance | **A−** | The fact that turned the hearing came from you, from memory, accurately. Dates confirmed by the bench. |
| Delivery | **C+** | Inaudible first line; paper before the point; the request muddled ("a stay or adjournment and a stay") and quiet (−3.2 dB); "obviously"; grievance in the last minute; no "Your Honour". |
| **Overall** | **B** | Won on substance despite the delivery. |

**What made the outcome:** your spoken fact, plus her preparation (your emails, sent in time, which she
had read), plus his disclosure (not yours). Two of the three were yours. The short emails sent ahead did
more than the schedule handed up on the day.

### Feedback
**Keep**
- Speaking from memory, with dates.
- The steady voice.
- The fast, short answers.
- Going back to the parked point: it produced the hinge.
- Checking the disclosure quietly and letting the bench run with it.

**Fix (with the line that would have done it)**
1. **Open with the request, at full voice.** "Yes, Your Honour. I'm not ready for trial today: the
   prosecution hasn't disclosed the sergeant's statement, his body-worn camera footage, or the sample. I'm
   asking for an adjournment, and for my stay application to be heard."
2. **Answer "are we going ahead today?" with the answer.** "No, Your Honour. I asked for an adjournment by
   email on the 20th and 22nd, and the prosecution refused."
3. **Lead with the strongest ground, without "obviously".** "Partly, Your Honour. The main ground is the
   sample: I was told verbally it was destroyed, and I've asked for it in writing since September."
4. **One line for the NATA complaint, then stop.** "I've also complained to NATA about how the lab handled
   the sample."
5. **Leave the Southport matter and "targeted" out of that courtroom.** The bench had already given you
   the result.
6. **Spend your volume on the request and the decisive fact,** not on the offer of paper.

## 2. QIRC mention, 7 Aug 2026 (WC/2024/227, Commissioner Dwyer)

### What was at stake
Your Form 29 non-party disclosure application against MSH, and in practice the shape of the whole case.
The Commissioner turned it into a 65-minute walk through every pleaded stressor: "how would you prove
this, and do you already hold the documents?"

### Outcome
- **Immediate:**
  - The Form 29 was parked for four weeks, then withdrawn by you "consistent with what was discussed".
  - One point survived as live: the directive emails ("the one area… there might be something to look
    at").
  - Directions in substance:
    - identify specific documents
    - confer with Ms Matheson
    - come back with any single document refused
- **Downstream:**
  - The notices to admit served on 28 Aug answered those directions point for point.
  - Result on 8 Sep: **298 of 303 facts and 29 of 39 documents admitted**, against about 50 facts in
    February.

### What the Commissioner's words show he made of you
- **On the paper:**
  - "pretty heavy-going stuff… Is it AI-assisted?"
  - "when I'm overloaded with page after page… I switch off… That's how I fall into error"
- **On you in the room:**
  - He took your two pages 0.16 s after you offered them ("Hand it up, please").
  - He learned your filter table from you ("doesn't make any sense to me", then "So you're still pressing
    for disclosure of manager email filters A–E", then "Okay").
  - He welcomed your narrowing ("We probably should have led with that").
  - He left the door open ("I'm not close[d] to that idea if you really wish to press it").
  - He pointed you to the way through ("If you think it's in a patient safety environment, you can raise

  - He ended on agreement ("Sound like a plan?" / "Sounds good").
- **His critique:**
  - "answer my question first… Yes or no?" (twice)
  - "Well, probably or is. Disclosure's about what you know."
  - "I'm concerned that the direction this is going in" (moving on from the emergency calls)
- **Read:** he spent an hour teaching a self-represented appellant his own case. He found the paper hard
  and the man workable.

### Verdict
| | Grade | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate outcome | **C** | The application you came for was parked and then withdrawn. One point survived. |
| Downstream outcome | **A** | You turned his directions into 298 admissions. |
| Candour | **A** | You were honest throughout and conceded the right things ("I don't know if I can prove it"; "I don't have knowledge of that"). |
| Preparation | **C** | Two under-claims cost the application: "Not that I can recall, no" and "No, I do not", about documents already in the Regulator's disclosure. |
| Delivery | **B−** | You matched his tempo, kept answers short, never talked over him and used no loaded words. Against that: you answered behind the question, reached for "one big system", and buried good answers (the emergency calls, the restricted mailbox). |
| Best moment | **A+** | The two-page handup 0.48 s after his volume complaint, then teaching him the table. |
| **Overall** | **B** | Lost the day on preparation; won the case's best result on what you did with it. |

### Feedback
**Keep**
- "Exactly." / "Correct." / "I have everything for this." Short answers that closed whole topics;
  the last disposed of Stressor 3.
- The two-page answer to his complaint.
- The honest concessions.

**Fix (with the line that would have done it)**
1. **Never guess about your own holdings.** "I'd need to check the Regulator's disclosure, Commissioner;
   I believe it includes them," not "Not that I can recall."
2. **Yes or no first.** "Is it in the form of an email?" → "Yes." Then stop.
3. **Give the real reason at full voice, not a quiet concession.** "MSH holds the mailboxes, Commissioner,
   not the Regulator. That's why I went to MSH," not "I guess I did request, but I didn't be specific
   about that" (−5.8 to −8.4 dB).
4. **Lead with the consequence.** "Calls for patients in cardiac arrest went to the wrong clinicians." Say
   that first, in one sentence; it answers "like a manager".
5. **Use the one clean sentence for pay.** "I was rostered off the public holidays my colleagues were
   rostered onto, so my fortnightly earnings were 42% below theirs."
6. **Drop "if that makes sense".**

## 3. What both judicial officers rewarded

Both responded to **precision**, not volume. The things that moved the bench:
- *Court:* one spoken fact ("only verbally told me it was destroyed").
- *Mention:* one short document ("these two pieces of paper") and three-word answers.

The things that cost you:
- volume of paper
- answers that went around the question
- your volume falling on the answers that mattered

**Five rules from your own two hearings**
1. Your request first, in one sentence, at full voice.
2. Know your holdings cold. Walk in with a one-page list of what you have and where it came from.
3. Send short paper ahead. On the day, hand up only what answers a problem the bench has just named.
4. Yes or no first; one point per answer; stop.
5. Put your loudest voice on the decisive fact and the request, never on side issues.
