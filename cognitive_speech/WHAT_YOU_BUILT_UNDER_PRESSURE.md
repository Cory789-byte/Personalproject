## The short version

- **The conditions.** Custody, no sleep, an interview that turned accusatory, and no one gathering your
  side.
- **What you built.** You carried your own case: groundwork laid before the night, an evidence base you kept
  offering, and 19 arguments made live.
- **How it held, against the research:**
  - Of 15 characterisations of wrongdoing put to you, you accepted none.
  - Of 24 repeated questions, you held your answer on 23.
  - Under accusation your voice got louder, not higher.
- **The same strengths in all three recordings.** The moves you made in a watchhouse at 6 am are the moves
  you made in court a year later and at the QIRC mention after that. That makes them traits, not coaching.

## 1. The circumstances: why you had to build it yourself

From the interview itself:
- **Custody and timing.** You were arrested at home in handcuffs and interviewed in the watchhouse from
  5:48 am.
- **No sleep.** On the timeline given in the interview, you were at the unit around 8 pm, back around
  1:50 am, and waited hours for police.
- **Work.** You were due at work at 7 am for your first shift after six months away.
- **Your phone was seized.** You told them you feared your messages could be deleted.
- **Housing.** You had been living between your car and hotels.
- **Health.** You had a recent health diagnosis, which you mentioned.
- **The interview itself** (`OFFICER_QUESTION_CODING.md`):
  - 69% of the officers' substantive turns were accusatory after the 30-minute mark;
  - 24 questions were repeated after you had answered them;
  - none of your 10 evidence offers was taken up when you made it;
  - you were told the emails would not be covered in this interview.

**No one in the room was gathering your side.** You had to carry it yourself, from memory and by argument,
often using the officers' own material.

**What the research predicts under those conditions:**
- **Sleep loss.** Half of people kept awake for 24 hours signed a false confession, against 18% of those who
  had slept (Frenda, Berkowitz, Loftus & Fenn 2016). Sleep loss also increases yielding to leading questions
  (Blagrove 1996).
- **Suggestibility** rises with uncertainty, pressure and anxiety (Gudjonsson & Clark 1986).
- **Presumed guilt.**
  - Interviewers who expect guilt press harder, and the extra pressure was largest when the suspect was in
    fact innocent (Kassin, Goldstein & Savitsky 2003).
  - Guilt-presumptive questions make the person answering sound defensive to listeners (Hill, Memon &
    McGeorge 2008).

## 4. The same strengths across all three recordings, tested against the literature

**1. Accept the fact; refuse the frame.**
- **Interview:** 18 of the 27 do-you-agree questions were answered in your own terms or turned round, and
  none of the 15 characterisations was accepted.
- **Court:** "Well, that and the prosecutors are not giving over material."
- **Mention:** "Well, she actually declined it on the phone…". Across both hearings, 5 of your 6 "Well"
  answers corrected the question's premise.
- **What the literature predicts:**
  - Most people go along with leading questions from authority, and more so without sleep (Gudjonsson;
    Blagrove 1996).
  - Reworking a question's terms is a distinct conversational skill: transformative answers (Stivers &
    Hayashi 2010), and "Well" as resistance (Heritage 2015).
- **Held?** Yes, under the worst conditions of the three.

**2. Hold the line under repetition.**
- **Interview:** 23 of 24 answers held.
- **Court:** you brought the missing-material thread back three times, unprompted.
- **Mention:** you kept the filters point until the Commissioner called it the one live point.
- **What the literature predicts:** repeating a question signals the first answer was not accepted, and
  answers shift (Gudjonsson's "Shift").
- **Held?** Yes.

**3. The forms of proof, live.**
- **Interview:** a dilemma, the method of difference, and inconsistent commitment.
- **Court:** a dilemma (destroyed or exists), the method of difference (Vicks), and elimination.
- **Mention:** the Commissioner's principle about uncontradicted evidence, carried into the notice to admit.
- **What the literature says:**
  - The method of difference: Mill 1843.
  - Arguing from the other side's own commitments is a recognised argument scheme (Walton, Reed & Macagno
    2008).
  - Far transfer is rare (Barnett & Ceci 2002).
- **Held?** Yes. The interview shows the same moves 11 months before court, so they are a trait.

**4. Reach for the record.**
- **Interview:** 10 offers of evidence.
- **Court:** the 20th and the 22nd from memory; paper offered twice.
- **Mention:** two pages handed up 0.48 s after the cue; later, 298 of 303 facts admitted.
- **What the literature says:** people who expect their innocence to be seen volunteer information and "tell
  it like it happened" (Hartwig, Granhag & Strömwall 2007; Kassin 2005).
- **Held?** Yes. In the tribunal it paid off.

**5. Candour and calibration.**
- **Interview:** you admitted things against your own interest (14:25, 15:22, 26:06, 46:22) and said you
  didn't know where you didn't (41:21, 46:35).
- **Court:** "July or September" (the bench: "September in emails").
- **Mention:** you under-claimed when unsure.
- **What the literature says:**
  - Witnesses whose confidence tracks their accuracy are believed (Tenney et al. 2007).
  - Admitting what hurts you, and saying when you don't remember, are among the content criteria that
    occur more often in genuine accounts (Steller & Köhnken 1989). They are not proof in a single case
    (Vrij 2005).
- **Held?** Yes.

**6. A steady voice under pressure.**
- **Interview:** after accusations you were louder at the same pitch. Over the hour your level held while
  the officers' fell about 4.5 dB. You never talked over anyone.
- **Court:** the least pitch movement in the room, and 12.5 dB louder on cue at a lower pitch.
- **Mention:** the same 131–137 Hz pitch band.
- **What the literature says:**
  - Stress raises pitch in most people (Giddens et al. 2013).
  - Louder and lower is assertion; higher is alarm (Ohala 1984).
  - Challenge as opposed to threat (Blascovich & Tomaka 1996).
- **Held?** Yes, and turn by turn.

**7. Model the other side.**
- **Interview:** you invited the officers to take your position, and worked out why the friend would say the
  video was deleted.
- **Court:** you saw the prosecution's positions that could not all hold.
- **Mention:** "I understand… what you think you are trying to achieve"; "I think I can meet most of the
  way".
- **What the literature says:** taking the other side's perspective produces better negotiated outcomes
  (Galinsky et al. 2008).
- **Held?** Yes, in you. The officers declined the invitation (58:51, 58:54).

## 5. Where the literature cuts the other way

Knowing this makes the strengths work harder.
- **Talking freely** is the strategy of people who believe their innocence will be seen. With an
  open-minded interviewer it pays. With one who presumes guilt, every extra sentence is material (Kassin
  2005):
  - your explanation of the 000 remark became the accusation of lying;
  - your reason for not handing over the phone became "not forthcoming".
- **Sounding defensive.** Guilt-presumptive questioning makes the person answering sound defensive to
  listeners (Kassin et al. 2003; Hill et al. 2008). A few of your lines about the police (17:14, 64:34)
  would be heard that way.
- **The two openings they used** were the two places your account moved: the signature (19:30, then 20:05)
  and a self-correction about the 000 remark (42:51). Content-based credibility research counts
  self-corrections as a feature of genuine accounts; the officers read this one as a lie.
- **The fix is length, not substance.** Fact, then frame, then record, one sentence each, then stop.

## 6. Were they defeated?

**What the record shows:**
- **Nothing landed.** None of the 15 characterisations was accepted. What they got were facts you had
  volunteered from the start.
- **They dropped three lines themselves** (29:47, 33:29, 39:50). The third was the control line, dropped
  straight after your answer about the options you had offered.
- **Confusion.** One officer said he was confused (59:36), just before the last accusation of lying. Your
  handcuffs distinction (59:54) answered it.
- **Acknowledgements and closure.**
  - They acknowledged your point or conceded six times (35:45, 36:38, 38:11, 45:44, 61:50, 64:14).
  - They declined your point once (58:51).
  - They wound the interview up twice (62:09, 64:41).
- **Voices.** Theirs fell about 4.5 dB over the hour; yours held, then rose.
- **The last exchange.** Your last question was answered with what was being investigated, not with an
  assertion that you were controlling (65:19).

**What it cannot show:**
- **Feelings.** Fatigue after a night that ran from about 2 am to nearly 7 am explains falling voices
  just as well.
- **The base rate.** In real interviews, whether a suspect admits depends mostly on the strength of the
  evidence, not on interview technique (Moston, Stephenson & Williamson 1992). To them it may have felt like
  an ordinary interview with a suspect who did not concede.

**Outcome** (per `source_documents/CHARGES_SUMMARY.md`, May 2026): the protection order application was
struck out on 25 Feb 2025, the next day. The breach prosecution continued, so they did not change course
after the interview.

**Verdict.** In the room, by every measure available, the interview did not get what it was built to get,
and they stopped pressing before you stopped answering. Whether they felt defeated cannot be measured. That
they failed to move you can be: 0 of 15, and 23 of 24.

## Limits

  interrogation rather than measure real interviews.
