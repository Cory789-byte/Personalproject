# Where you are skilled above the normal population, and how to use it (8 Oct 2026)

Personal / self-analysis only. Evidence comes from three recordings (QPS interview Feb 2025, Magistrates Court
Feb 2026, QIRC mention Aug 2026) and your documents. The evidence is graded:
- **A:** beats an outside benchmark (published research or reference speakers).
- **B:** beats the trained professionals in the same room.
- **C:** shown by an outcome, with no population benchmark.

Small samples and hand counts throughout: these are indicators, not psychometric scores.

## A. Above an outside benchmark

1. **Your voice holds under pressure.**
   - *Evidence:* median pitch 131–137 Hz across a police interview, a criminal court and a tribunal. Stress
     raises pitch in most people (Giddens et al. 2013).
   - *Use it:* in the witness box, in negotiation and in a crisis, you read as the calm one. Put warmth into
     your words, because your voice won't supply it.
2. **You get more fluent under pressure, not less.**
   - *Evidence:* disfluency fell or stayed flat when you were most activated (interview 1.25 → 0.60 per 100
     words; court 0 → 0; mention 0.53 → 0.57). Anxiety usually increases speech disturbances (Mahl 1956).
   - *Use it:* choose live, high-stakes speaking: oral submissions, mediation, crisis coordination.
3. **You hold several lines of thought and return to them.**
   - *Evidence:* 3.4 topic switches and 2.1 returns per 100 words in free narrative, against 1.6–2.0 and
     0.4–1.0 for recorded conversations between strangers, and 2.6 and 1.05 for the Commissioner.
   - *Use it:* issue-mapping, complex case management, investigations, agendas. Keep a written "parking list"
     so the extra threads go on paper, not into your answers.
4. **You tolerate silence.**
   - *Evidence:* after long pauses in the interview the officers broke the silence 26 times; you broke it 10.
   - *Use it:*
     - In cross-examination, answer, stop, and let the silence sit; witnesses lose ground by filling it.
     - In negotiation, make the offer, then say nothing.
     - In mediation, silence is where parties think.
5. **You don't accept a wrong premise.**
   - *Evidence:* 5 of your 6 "Well…" answers corrected the question's premise. Many people go along with
     leading questions put by someone in authority (Gudjonsson's suggestibility research). Small sample.
   - *Use it:* under questioning, give "No" or "Yes" first, then "Well…" and the correct premise. In
     negotiation, don't adopt the other side's framing.

## B. Above the professionals in the same room

6. **You draw lines.**
   - *Evidence:* "but / except / unless / only" at about twice the bench's rate (2.4 v 1.3 per 100 words in
     court; 1.5 v 0.9 at the mention).
   - *Use it:* definitions, scope, what is in and out of an issue. Law exams are issue-spotting tests, and
     this is the core skill they reward.
7. **You give reasons.**
   - *Evidence:* "because / reason / why" at nearly four times the Magistrate's rate in court. The prosecutor
     used none.
   - *Use it:* written decisions, investigation findings, submissions. Reasons are what make a finding stand
     up.
8. **You follow the other person.**
   - *Evidence:* at the mention, 17% of your answer words came straight from the question (four times
     chance), against the Commissioner's 7% from yours. He adopted 65% of the key terms you introduced.
   - *Use it:* mediation, interviewing, client work. Add looping: say their words back until they say
     "that's right".
9. **The steadiest voice in every room.**
   - *Evidence:* the least pitch movement within a sentence of anyone you spoke with, in all three recordings.
   - *Use it:* chairing and running meetings. Calm is contagious in a room.
10. **You keep conversational speed in formal rooms.**
    - *Evidence:* you answered in 0.26–0.32 s, against the officers' 1.22 s.
    - *Use it:* in fast case-management hearings you keep up. In the witness box, slow down by half a second
      on hard questions so it doesn't look rehearsed.

## C. Shown by outcomes

11. **You build structures that make the honest answer "yes".**
    - *Evidence:* the rebuilt notice to admit took admissions from about 50 to 298 of 303.
    - *Use it:* any request, policy or proposal. Build it so each item can only be admitted. This is your
      most valuable professional skill.
12. **You catch contradictions and test accounts against the record.**
    - *Evidence:* the two-sided fork in court; the destroyed-or-exists contradiction that got the hearing
      stood down.
    - *Use it:* investigations, audits, safety and incident reviews, cross-examination preparation.
13. **You catch errors in legal reasoning.**
    - *Evidence:* the s 32 test is "a significant contributing factor", not "major"; *Delaney* was misapplied
      in the review decision.
    - *Use it:* reviewing drafts and decisions, legal research, law study.
14. **You're calibrated about what you know.**
    - *Evidence:* "July or September", and the bench confirmed September. "I don't know if I can prove it."
    - *Use it:* credibility in any witness box; state your confidence levels in reports.
15. **You learn fast from feedback.**
    - *Evidence:* within six months you matched the bench's pace, dropped loaded words and went to yes-first.
      You answered the Commissioner's volume complaint with two pages, 0.48 s later.
    - *Use it:* after every hearing or meeting, ask "what would you change?". Re-record and re-measure.
16. **You read the other side's reasoning.**
    - *Evidence:* your habit of modelling how the other side's team leader, manager and lawyers would think.
    - *Use it:* negotiation preparation (their constraints, their alternatives to agreeing) and mediation
      reality-testing.

## Roles that reward this profile (best fit first)

1. **Workplace investigator, or complaints and dispute-resolution officer** (regulators, ombudsman-type
   bodies, agencies). Fact-finding from documents and interviews, contradiction-testing, written findings.
2. **Mediator or conciliator.** AMDRAS accreditation; best in workplace, industrial and community disputes.
3. **Industrial officer or IR advocate.** Structure plus advocacy, using your health and industrial
   knowledge.
4. **Long term: tribunal member,** via law or industrial relations.

## The flip side of each strength

- **Thread-holding** turns into side issues. The counter is the parking list, and one point per answer.
- **Structuring** turns into volume. The counter is a one-page version, every time.
- **Contradiction-spotting** turns into assuming bad faith. The counter is to call it a contradiction until
  the evidence proves more.
- **Drive** turns into exhaustion. The counter is scheduled stopping points.
