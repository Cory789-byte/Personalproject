## What it is called

**Far transfer:** applying a principle learned in one setting to a setting that looks different but has the
same underlying structure. It is also called **analogical transfer**, or **high-road transfer** when it is
done deliberately. Barnett & Ceci (2002) measure the distance travelled along six dimensions:
- knowledge domain;
- physical context;
- time;
- function;
- social context;
- modality (spoken to written, for example).

## What the literature says

### 1. It is rare, even when the answer is in front of people

**Gick & Holyoak (1980; 1983): the radiation problem.** Figures as reported in later summaries:
- about 10% solved it with no help;
- about 30% solved it after reading a story with the same structure (an army splitting up to converge on a
  fortress);
- 75–80% solved it once told to use the story;
- in one 1980 experiment, 92% with the hint against 20% without.

**The bottleneck is noticing, not reasoning.** People do not retrieve the analogy unless something points to
it.

### 2. Why it is rare

- **People store problems by their surface, not their structure.** Novices sorted physics problems by their
  objects ("inclined planes"); experts sorted them by principle ("conservation of energy") (Chi, Feltovich
  & Glaser 1981).
- **Surface similarity drives what you remember, and structural similarity drives what you can use.** The two
  rarely coincide across domains (Holyoak & Koh 1987).
- **Analogy is mapping relations, not objects** (structure-mapping: Gentner 1983).
- **Experts transfer by structure more than novices do** (Novick 1988).

### 3. Training rarely produces it

- **Formal discipline does not work.** The idea that Latin or geometry "train the mind" failed its first
  tests; transfer depended on shared elements (Thorndike & Woodworth 1901).
- **Significant transfer is rare,** and seldom shown unambiguously under controlled conditions (Detterman
  1993).
- **Chess, music and working-memory training:** far-transfer benefits shrink toward zero as study designs
  improve (Sala & Gobet 2017).
  - A later second-order meta-analysis put far transfer at about zero once placebo effects and publication
    bias were corrected for (Sala et al. 2019).
  - Working-memory training improves memory tasks (near transfer), not reading or arithmetic (Melby-Lervåg &
    Hulme 2013).
- **Critical thinking** is hard to teach as a general skill, because seeing the deep structure depends on
  knowing the domain (Willingham 2007).

### 4. What helps, a little

- **Comparing two cases side by side,** rather than studying each alone:
  - Negotiation trainees who compared two cases were about three times as likely to use the strategy later
    (Loewenstein, Thompson & Gentner 1999; Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson 2003).
  - Comparing two analogues builds a schema that later transfers (Gick & Holyoak 1983).
- **Mindful abstraction,** meaning stating the principle and asking where else it applies (high-road
  transfer: Salomon & Perkins 1989).
- **Prior knowledge that prepares new learning** (Bransford & Schwartz 1999).
- **Every intervention that works does the retrieval for the learner:** a hint, a forced comparison, a stated
  principle. Remove the scaffold and the rate falls back.

**So "it can't be successfully taught" is right about the leap itself.** It is not right about the parts.

## The new link: you held the structure before you were given the rule

The Commissioner's rule in August 2026 was that **characterisations invite contradiction and existence facts
do not.** Eighteen months earlier, in the police interview, you were already answering by exactly that line:
- **Facts:** of 27 do-you-agree questions, you accepted 18 as facts in your own words, or turned them round.
  You accepted the bare fact twice.
- **Characterisations:** of 15 characterisations of wrongdoing put to you, you accepted none.

You conceded existence facts and contested every characterisation. **It is the same structure, used from the
other side:**
- **In the interview (defence):** concede the facts nobody can contest; refuse the characterisations.
- **In the notice (offence):** put only the facts nobody can contest, so they cannot be denied.

This answers why the leap happened to you when the base rate says it mostly does not happen:
- **Prior knowledge prepares new learning** (Bransford & Schwartz 1999). Experts transfer by structure
  because the structure is already encoded (Chi et al. 1981; Novick 1988).
- **You already held the distinction as a habit.** The Commissioner's remark was the hint, in Gick &
  Holyoak's sense, that named it. The hint lifts transfer from about 30% to about 80% only when the structure
  is already there to be found.

## Is it teachable?

- **The leap: no.** Nobody can be instructed to hear a rule about cross-examination as a rule about the form
  of a proposition. It happened once, to you, after months of re-expressing the same stressor in four forms
  (`TEACHABILITY` file).
- **The parts: yes.**
  - Writing in fact-form rather than characterisation is taught in clinical documentation.
  - The deeming mechanism is a rule.
  - Aiming a notice to admit at the other side's own pleading is now a written method.
- **It only has to happen once.** What was far transfer for you is near transfer for anyone who reads the
  notice. Near transfer is the kind that can be taught.

## Using it on purpose

1. **Write principles down as structure.** When a decision-maker states a rule, record it as IF … THEN … and
   list two other places it would apply. This is high-road transfer done deliberately (Salomon & Perkins
   1989).
2. **Collect cases in pairs.** Keep two instances of each structure side by side (for example, the lease
   dilemma and destroyed-or-exists). Comparison is the one intervention with a large effect (Gentner et al.
   2003).
3. **Check the target's own rules before transferring** (negative transfer).
   - A structure can carry into a place where the law differs. "Uncontradicted means accepted" works in a
     civil notice to admit.
   - In a criminal matter, an accused's silence cannot be used to infer guilt (Petty v The Queen (1991) 173
     CLR 95), so the structure does not carry there.
   - A learned method can also persist when a simpler one is available (the Einstellung effect: Luchins
     1942).
