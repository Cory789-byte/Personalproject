## PART I — PURPOSE

1. This submission respectfully invites the prosecution to discontinue the proceeding against the accused in its entirety, by offering no evidence at the next mention or by such other mechanism as the prosecution considers appropriate. It is made in accordance with the case-conferencing practice applying to summary proceedings and against the two limbs that govern every decision to commence or continue a prosecution in Queensland: whether there is a **reasonable prospect of conviction** on the admissible evidence, and whether the prosecution is in the **public interest** (Director's Guidelines (Qld), Guideline 4; QPS Operational Procedures Manual, ch 3.4).

2. The submission is founded almost entirely on material generated by the Queensland Police Service itself: the attending officers' body-worn camera recordings, the Service's occurrence records, the issuing officer's written confirmation of a no-evidence strike-out, and the contemporaneous records of co-ordinate institutions — the Southport Magistrates Court, the Residential Tenancies Authority, and the property manager's own email record. Where the accused relies on material of his own, it is identified and offered for production.

3. Nothing in this submission is advanced as rhetoric. Each proposition is anchored to a record. The prosecution is invited to verify every quotation against the original recordings, which the Service holds.

## PART II — THE CHARGES

4. The accused faces charges arising from two occasions [conform to bench charge sheet]:

   **Charge 1** — Contravention of a domestic violence order: *Domestic and Family Violence Protection Act 2012* (Qld) s 177, arising from the accused's presence at Unit 104, 158 Scarborough Street, Southport on the night of 23–24 February 2025.

   **Charge 2** — Stealing (a companion animal): *Criminal Code* (Qld) ss 391, 398, arising from the accused's possession of the dog "Romeo" on or about 22 March 2025.

## PART III — THE GOVERNING FRAMEWORK

5. The decision to continue a prosecution is a continuing decision. Guideline 4 of the Director's Guidelines requires more than a prima facie case: there must be a reasonable prospect of conviction before a reasonable tribunal of fact on the admissible evidence, assessed in light of the reliability and credibility of the witnesses whose accounts constitute that evidence, and any lines of defence fairly open. If that threshold is not met, the prosecution must not continue, and the public-interest limb is never reached. If it is met, the public-interest factors must still favour prosecution.

6. The discretion is the prosecution's alone (*Maxwell v The Queen* (1996) 184 CLR 501), but it must be exercised on a candid assessment of the whole of the material, including material pointing away from guilt (*Whitehorn v The Queen* (1983) 152 CLR 657 at 663–664 per Deane J; the disclosure duty codified in *Criminal Code* (Qld) s 590AB; *Mallard v The Queen* (2005) 224 CLR 125; *Grey v The Queen* (2001) 75 ALJR 1708). The courts' supervisory jurisdiction over proceedings that have become oppressive or unfair (*Jago v District Court (NSW)* (1989) 168 CLR 23; *Walton v Gardiner* (1993) 177 CLR 378) marks the outer boundary within which the present assessment falls to be made.

7. The Queensland Police Service and the ODPP are public entities under the *Human Rights Act 2019* (Qld). Section 58 makes it unlawful to act or make a decision incompatibly with human rights or without proper consideration of relevant rights — here, liberty (s 29), fair hearing (s 31), property (s 24), privacy and reputation (s 25), and protection of families and children (s 26). The continuing decision to prosecute is such a decision.

## PART IV — CHARGE 1: NO REASONABLE PROSPECT OF CONVICTION

### A. The order could not be contravened by presence

8. The Temporary Protection Order of 3 December 2024 (0022800685; MAG-00196566/24(4)) was made **by consent, without admissions** (see *DFVPA* s 51). It contained two conditions only: the standard good-behaviour condition (*DFVPA* s 56 — be of good behaviour and do not commit domestic violence), and a single property-recovery condition that **expired on 1 January 2025** (D5). There was no exclusion condition, no ouster condition, and no no-contact condition. On 23–24 February 2025 the only operative condition was good behaviour.

9. The attending officers said precisely this, on their own recordings, before the arrest:

   - "the DV order that you've got says that he can be here" — Constable Davies, to the aggrieved by telephone (D37, 00:06:19);
   - "there's no contact conditions in there, so no one's done anything there" (D50, 00:23:05);
   - "So technically you can be here" (D50, 00:13:56);
   - "technically, he's not breaching the DVO by being in here … There's nothing on that order that's saying he can't be here" (D37, 00:25:29–00:25:47);
   - "I'm more inclined to believe him … because of what the order's saying. Why would the order say let the aggrieved back in?" (D37, 00:20:26–00:20:36).

10. Accordingly, to prove Charge 1 the prosecution must prove beyond reasonable doubt not presence, but an **act of domestic violence** within the meaning of *DFVPA* s 8, committed by the accused on the night. The material discloses none.

### B. There is no admissible evidence of any contravening act

11. The complainant **was in Perth throughout the events**. Her statement of 25 February 2025 concedes that her account of the entry is second-hand in its entirety (D9 ¶¶3, 28). As to the events charged, her evidence is hearsay upon hearsay and inadmissible to prove any contravening act.

12. The only independent adult eyewitness apart from the two households, Mr Thomas Balsley — a process server of fifteen years' and bailiff of ten years' experience — recorded the doorway interaction and observed **no physical contact by the accused with any person** (D14; D32). No statement was sought from him for approximately fifteen months. The prosecution's only civilian eyewitness statement (Ms Phillips) was served **missing page 3 of 4** — paragraphs 16–20 — a defect notified in writing in September 2025 and still unremedied (D10).

13. The accused's conduct on the night, on the Service's own records, was that of a tenant engaging process: a Form 9 entry notice given through building management on 19 February (D15; D40); two counter attendances at Southport station on 20 and 23 February presenting the lease and the order (D14); a Policelink call of over thirty minutes at 7:50pm (D47); and approximately five hours waiting at the scene for police attendance (D14). That is the opposite of an evasion of lawful process; it is reliance upon it.

### C. The accused's presence was lawful: the tenancy analysis

14. The accused was Tenant 1 under the operative Form 18a lease and sole holder of bond BL-37274182 (D1–D2). No instrument known to the *Residential Tenancies and Rooming Accommodation Act 2008* (Qld) had removed him:

    (a) a **Form 13** notice of intention to leave (7 December 2024; execution circumstances contested, D4; D43) is not, and cannot be, an instrument of removal — the Act contains no provision by which a Form 13 alone removes one co-tenant while the agreement continues for another;

    (b) the **Form 20 domestic-violence pathway** (ss 308A–308E) that the co-tenant herself initiated on 5 December 2024 was never completed (D42) — and had it been, it would have ended only *her* interest, leaving the accused's tenancy on foot;

    (c) the property manager's own correspondence records the tenancy as unresolved throughout — "As long as either of you are staying, it is okay" (8 December 2024, 7:23am, D44) — and her only step toward a new lease was a proposal emailed **to the co-tenant alone** at 6:14pm on 22 February 2025 (D44); the new bond was received by the RTA only on **27 February 2025, three days after the arrest** (D41; D45);

    (d) the RTA released the existing bond **solely to the accused** on 25 February 2025 (D3);

    (e) the building manager, Mr Denis Constable, swore that at approximately **1:54am — before the arrest** — he told an attending officer by telephone that the Form 13 did not terminate the lease and that the accused remained a lawful tenant (D15 ¶¶28–29); Constable Easthope's own camera records the contemporaneous police assessment: "**it's his lease, his bond, and it's his key**" (D28, ~02:00); and when Constable Davies rang building management again at 5:21am, the management said three times that the lease alteration had not been accepted (D37, 00:01:55–00:05:52).

15. A tenant lawfully on his own lease, giving notice, seeking police assistance, and collecting his property, does not commit domestic violence by being there. The officers said so contemporaneously. The tenancy law says so. The RTA's conduct two days later says so.

### D. The foundation and fate of the order itself

16. The order alleged to have been contravened must carry the weight the prosecution puts on it. It cannot:

    (a) it was made **by consent, without admissions** — it establishes no fact against the accused (D5);

    (b) the application behind it was **struck out for want of evidence**: Senior Constable R. Yaun confirmed in writing that it was struck out "upon my submission of **no evidence from the aggrieved**", and that the struck-out occurrence number was thereafter incorrectly reused in a fresh application (D7; D8);

    (c) the application was withdrawn and the order **vacated on 25 February 2025** — within approximately thirty hours of the arrest (D6);

    (d) the material said to have supported the application is contradicted by contemporaneous records: **CCTV of the relevant period [E-1]**, and **records the aggrieved herself sent to the accused evidencing attendance for cosmetic procedures — including "thread" treatment at a clinic, with payment records referencing Oh La La Cosmetics [E-2]** — records which, on the accused's instructions, account on innocent and self-declared grounds for matters otherwise advanced against him. *(Exhibits to be conformed and produced; the point is put no higher than the documents themselves put it.)*;

    (e) the application's foundation is further contradicted by presence evidence: whereas the application is understood to have advanced that the aggrieved **sought refuge away from the premises**, footage from the period following the application **places her at 104/158 Scarborough Street on a daily basis [E-3: body-worn camera and/or building CCTV; to be conformed]**. A person genuinely displaced by fear does not reside daily at the premises said to be the site of that fear. The contradiction goes to the foundation of the very order the prosecution must now rely upon — an order whose applicant officer subsequently struck the application out for want of any evidence from her (D7).

17. The complainant's own statement — taken on 25 February 2025, **only after** the application had been withdrawn and the order vacated — is on its face a property document, not a protection document: it describes the order as a good-behaviour condition and nothing more (D9 ¶3); it records her request for **100% of the shared property, namely the animals**; and it records that she **sought no additional orders and no no-contact conditions** [D9; conform paragraph references]. A complainant who, the day after the events charged, seeks no protective condition of any kind and frames her concerns entirely in property terms is not a witness upon whose account a domestic-violence contravention can realistically be proved beyond reasonable doubt.

18. Further and cumulatively: at the time of the events charged, the aggrieved **was in Perth** — which is why her account of the entry is second-hand in its entirety (D9 ¶¶3, 28). Her conduct in the preceding period, on the record already before the Service, was not that of a person in refuge from the premises but of a person in occupation and control of them: she resided at the premises daily (¶16(e); [E-3]); she did not merely collect personal property under the expired property condition but asserted control over the shared apartment; access passes were reinstated for her "and her friend" while the leaseholder was in custody (D37, 00:02:27–00:03:24); and she accumulated the couple's property to herself — conduct the officers had classified as "civil" when the accused reported its beginning on 1 December 2024 (D22–D23; D40). The asymmetry is complete: the same subject matter, the same premises, the same parties — civil when reported by him; criminal when reported against him.

19. It follows that Charge 1 fails at the first limb. The only operative condition was good behaviour; the only percipient evidence discloses no act of domestic violence; the presence relied upon was lawful; and the order's own evidentiary foundation was abandoned by the applicant officer for want of evidence within thirty hours. A reasonable tribunal of fact, properly directed, could not convict.

## PART V — CHARGE 2: NO REASONABLE PROSPECT OF CONVICTION

20. The ownership record is one-way: sole City of Gold Coast registration in the accused's name (tag 986255; microchip 991003002674289); breeder purchase and payment records from December 2023; continuous veterinary-care payments through 2024–2025; and the parties' own separation correspondence recording the accused's position that the animal remained with him (D40, sworn and exhibited in FCFCOA proceeding M14056-25).

21. Even were ownership contestable, s 22(2) of the *Criminal Code* (Qld) affords a complete answer: an honest claim of right, exercised without intention to defraud, in respect of property. The claim need only be honest; it need not be reasonable, still less correct (*Walden v Hensler* (1987) 163 CLR 561). On this record the claim is not merely honest but documented, registered, and paid for. The prosecution cannot negative it beyond reasonable doubt.

22. The Service's own treatment of the identical subject matter forecloses the charge:

    (a) in **December 2024**, when the accused reported the animal's taking with documents on offer, the matter was classified **civil** and attendance declined (D40; D22–D23);

    (b) on **22 March 2025**, officers seized the animal in a retail carpark on the recorded basis: "**regardless of what paperwork he has, we've got to seize the dog**"; "All I've been told is that you've stolen the dog"; "**It would have been civil if you didn't get charged for stealing it**" (D48; D30 — Field Property Receipt 137237). The final statement is circular on its face: the charge is offered as the justification for the seizure, and the seizure as the substance of the charge;

    (c) the released footage for 22 March 2025 **ends at 00:20:02, mid-telephone-call, before the seizure** — no seizure, no property receipt, no examination of the ownership documents appears in what has been produced (D48), notwithstanding the agreed resolution of OIC external review 318762 on 11 September 2025 (D46), now ten months unfulfilled;

    (d) the FCFCOA proceeding in which ownership was to be determined (M14056-25) has been **discontinued without any finding**, the Court refusing the Service's costs application. No forum is now seised of ownership; the prosecution is being used as the de facto determinant of a civil property question — precisely what the Service itself said in December 2024 it would not do.

23. Charge 2 therefore also fails at the first limb: documented sole ownership, an unanswerable s 22(2) claim of right, an inverted classification the Service's own recordings render circular, and a seizure whose primary record has not been produced.

## PART VI — THE RELIABILITY OF THE ACCOUNTS UNDERPINNING THE PROSECUTION

24. Where a prosecution rests on the accounts of witnesses, Guideline 4 requires their credibility and reliability to be brought into the reasonable-prospects assessment. Three accounts underpin this prosecution; each is contradicted by contemporaneous records:

    (a) **The complainant.** She was in Perth at the time of the events charged; her account of them is wholly second-hand (D9 ¶¶3, 28). Her "my dwelling" assertion is contradicted by the tenancy record and by the recording officer's own contemporaneous camera ("it's his lease, his bond, and it's his key" — D28). Her statement was made only after the application's withdrawal, seeks no protective orders, and claims the whole of the shared property (¶17 above). The refuge claim understood to have founded the **original December 2024 application** is contradicted by footage of her daily presence at the premises in the period that followed it (¶16(e); [E-3]), and the material she herself sent the accused [E-2] further undermines that application's factual premise — an application in any event struck out for want of any evidence from her (D7). A complainant whose foundational application could not survive for want of her own evidence, and whose foundational claims are contradicted by presence footage and her own correspondence, cannot carry a criminal charge that depends on the order that application produced.

    (b) **The property manager.** Her witness statement of 27 May 2026 (D41) asserts that upon receipt of the Form 13 the accused "was no longer on the lease" — contradicted by her own email of the following morning ("As long as either of you are staying, it is okay", D44), by her 22 February 2025 6:14pm email arranging the very bond refund and new lease her statement treats as already complete (D44), and by the RTA's records (D3; D45). A written reconciliation was provided to the investigating officer on 7 July 2026 (D45).

    (c) **The arresting narrative.** The officers' own cameras record, before the arrest: that the order permitted presence; that the tenancy was unresolved; that third-party confirmation was needed ("we really need to confirm with the third party", 00:19:52); and that a less restrictive resolution had been identified and accepted (00:24:16–00:24:53). The arrest preceded the confirmation the officers had twice said was necessary (D37) — and on the building manager's sworn account the confirmation had already been given at 1:54am (D15 ¶¶28–29). When it was obtained again at 5:21am, it contradicted the arrest premise three times (D37).

25. A prosecution whose three foundational accounts are each contradicted by the Service's own contemporaneous records does not present a credibility contest for trial. It presents a reliability failure at the charge-assessment stage.

## PART VII — DISCLOSURE

26. The accused notes, without elaborating beyond what the record compels: disclosure has passed through three iterations and remained substantially incomplete beyond 220 days as at May 2026 (D10–D13); the sole civilian eyewitness statement remains incomplete (missing page 3 of 4) ten months after written notice (D10); two body-worn-camera segments are labelled on the Service's own index as not contained in the original brief (D31); and the complete footage that was the subject of the agreed resolution of OIC external review 318762 has not been produced ten months later (D46). The duty under s 590AB extends to all things in the prosecution's possession that would tend to help the case for the accused (*Mallard*; *Grey*). The state of disclosure would itself require remedy before any trial could fairly proceed; it also bears directly on the public-interest limb.

## PART VIII — THE PUBLIC INTEREST

27. If, contrary to Parts IV and V, some residual prospect were thought to exist, the public-interest factors under Guideline 4 tell decisively against continuation:

    (a) **the attitude and conduct of the complainant** — application withdrawn for want of her evidence; no protective conditions sought; property claims pursued and substantially achieved by other means;

    (b) **the true character of the dispute** — a civilly entangled separation and tenancy dispute, twice classified by the Service itself as civil when reported by the accused;

    (c) **the circumstances of the accused** — no criminal history; a healthcare employment background; documented psychiatric vulnerability (major depressive disorder with anxiety state, D20–D21) physically in the officers' hands before the arrest; a confirmed return to work at 7:00am on 24 February 2025 lost to the custody itself (D18);

    (d) **consequences already suffered, without conviction** — custody; homelessness recorded in the Service's own footage ("I have nowhere. I have to sleep in my car", D50); loss of employment re-commencement; seizure of his telephone (unreturned) and his dog; his family's documented housing displacement (D26–D27; D51);

    (e) **staleness and delay** — the events are seventeen months old; disclosure remains incomplete; the eyewitness was first contacted fifteen months after the events;

    (f) **the maintenance of public confidence in the administration of justice** — which is not served by continuing a prosecution the Service's own recordings undermine, and is served by the candid correction of a matter that co-ordinate institutions (the Magistrates Court, the RTA, the OIC) have already answered in the accused's favour;

    (g) **proportionality and cost** — a defended summary hearing of this record, against this disclosure history, invites a costs order on dismissal (*Justices Act 1886* (Qld) s 158; *Latoudis v Casey* (1990) 170 CLR 534). The accused expressly reserves that application.
