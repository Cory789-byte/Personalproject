# The ESC exchange (COM-26-01249): the decision-maker point, your letters, their silence, and the human-rights and anti-discrimination routes

Prepared for Cory Lea Shepherd, 10 November 2026. Companion to the assessment of the same date.
Draft for discussion with your lawyer. Privileged. Not for service or filing.

## 1. The point, expanded

**What it is.** The CCC referred CO-26-1318 to a named office for a stated reason. On 31 March 2026 it wrote that the allegations were "appropriate to refer to the QPS Deputy Commissioner, who is independent from QPS ESC". On 28 April it confirmed the referral had gone to the Deputy Commissioner, with your 8 April email. The Outcome Advice of 10 September says the complaint "was assessed by the ESC, including review by the State Coordinator" and refers to a "committee". It does not mention the Deputy Commissioner. So the body whose handling you complained about assessed the complaint that included that very concern, and nothing on the record explains how it came to do so.

**Why it has force.** Three reasons, in order of weight.

1. It is a question of fact, not opinion. Either the Deputy Commissioner made a decision on the referral or did not. The answer is in QPS's own allocation records. No argument is needed from you, and no argument is available to them except the answer.
2. The reason the CCC gave for the referral was independence from ESC. That was not a slip. The CCC's 31 March letter records that you had raised "concerns about the QPS Ethical Standards Command". The referral destination was chosen to meet that concern. When ESC decided, the CCC's own basis for devolving the matter was not honoured, whatever the legality of the internal allocation.
3. It is the one defect that survives every answer they can give. If the Deputy Commissioner never looked at it, the referral premise failed. If the Deputy Commissioner allocated it to ESC, the office complained about assessed itself. If the Deputy Commissioner endorsed the Outcome Advice, a senior officer has adopted a letter that mis-describes the complaint. Each answer is a fact the CCC does not yet have.

**Its legal limits, which you should hold in view.** Section 42(5) of the Crime and Corruption Act says a complaint referred by the CCC must be dealt with "in the way the commissioner of police considers most appropriate, subject to the commission's monitoring role". The Act does not require any particular officer to decide, and the CCC's independence reason was its reason, not a condition it had power to impose on QPS. So this is a fairness point and a monitoring point, not a breach of statute. The fairness principle is ordinary: a body should not assess a complaint about its own handling. The monitoring point is for the CCC, which is the only body with a statutory role over how QPS dealt with its referral. Neither point gives you a right of review.

**And the time-bar context.** Under s 7.12 of the Police Service Administration Act, a disciplinary proceeding must start within the latest of one year from the conduct, six months from receipt of the complaint by the Commissioner or the CCC, or six months after the end of a proceeding against the officer. For the February–May 2025 conduct every period had run by late September or late October 2026. So even an uncontested win on the decision-maker point cannot now produce discipline over the 2025 events. What it can still produce: a correct record of who decided and what was assessed; a real answer on the Information Commissioner agreement; attention to the March 2026 allegation, which is still inside the one-year period until March 2027.

### What the two letters together achieve

They put on the record, in your words and theirs, a closed chain: the CCC's stated premise → an ESC decision → ESC's own re-description of the complaint → a precise and bounded challenge, twice → no acknowledgment. That is the record a monitoring request to the CCC needs, and it shows you did what the CCC's 28 April letter told you to do first ("engage with the QPS in the first instance"). They also fix the date on which the March 2026 conduct was raised with QPS in writing.

## 3. The defects in their reasoning, listed

1. **Non-responsive summary.** The complaint summarised is narrower than, and different from, the complaint the CCC recorded. Two of the four recorded allegations (the OIC directions and the March 2026 conduct) disappear into "disclosure of court ordered material" and "potential reprisal action".
2. **Wrong forum applied to non-court matters.** "Raise it in court" has a legitimate application to the statements and the specimen while a prosecution is on foot. It has none for a statutory access agreement, for conduct in March 2026, or for a complaint about ESC's own earlier handling. None of those is before a court.
3. **Conditional description of the evidence.** "Information gathered … may include" footage, records and witness accounts. It is not a statement of what was examined. On a complaint whose core was three identified serials, the absence of a statement that those serials were viewed is conspicuous.
4. **Compliance asserted without a standard named.** "Consistent with applicable policy and legislative requirements" names no policy and no provision, including for the arrest power.
5. **Bare conclusions on rights.** A "committee" determined no undue limit on human rights and no Charter contravention. No right, fact or reason is given. Whether or not the conclusion is right, it is not reasoned.
6. **The decision-maker.** As above.
7. **The referral material.** No mention of the 14 July report or the 23 July guide, both acknowledged by the Deputy Commissioner's office; no mention of the 8 April particulars the CCC confirmed were included.
8. **Process timing.** Seven weeks from referral to registration; one business day for particulars; the Advice issued the day after your chaser to the Deputy Commissioner's office and within days of the six-month disciplinary clock from the CCC's receipt of the new allegations running out. Timing alone proves nothing, but it fits a file closed rather than assessed.

Against that list, one thing stands on their side: in form the Advice is a response under s 42(7). It states that no further action is taken and gives a reason. The law requires reasons; it does not prescribe their quality. Your complaint is about accuracy and completeness, which is a fair complaint and a monitoring question, not a breach you can enforce yourself.

## 4. Why they have not responded

In likely order:

1. **The file is closed in their system.** Once an outcome issues, post-outcome correspondence is logged as "noted" unless it contains new evidence. Your letters contain argument and record questions, not new evidence. The system has no step for them.
2. **The Complaint Management Unit is an intake unit, not a review unit.** The Outcome Advice's invitation to ask "how your complaint was assessed" is a template line. The unit can answer "who assessed it and when"; it cannot remit, reopen or re-assess. A letter asking it to do those things waits for someone who can, and that someone is not obliged to reply.
3. **You cited statutes.** A letter raising the Crime and Corruption Act, the Human Rights Act, the Anti-Discrimination Act and the arrest power is routed for legal review before anyone answers it. That adds weeks and often produces a shorter reply than the letter deserved.
4. **The copy to the Deputy Commissioner's office.** That office acknowledged twice and never substantively replied; its pattern is to forward. A letter copied to it goes into two queues, each able to assume the other is dealing with it.
5. **No legal duty to answer.** The s 42(7) duty was, in their view, discharged on 10 September. There is no statutory duty to answer follow-up correspondence, only general complaint-handling practice. Your 12 November date is your date; nothing happens to them when it passes.
6. **Time.** Twelve days since 29 October and five since 5 November. QPS correspondence routinely takes three to six weeks even when it is going to be answered.

The absence of any acknowledgment of either letter is the one feature that points to something other than ordinary delay: either the letters are unallocated, or a decision has been made not to engage. Either way, the next step is the same, and it does not depend on them.

## 5. Human rights and anti-discrimination: can you take it further?

Yes, in theory. In practice the routes are weaker than the ESC point, and two of them are largely spent. Here is each, with its precondition, its time limit, what it could produce, and the risk.

### The Human Rights Act

- **The underlying conduct (February–March 2025) has already been through the Commission.** EDR19098 (Shepherd v QPS) went to conciliation on 14 May 2026, was recorded as unresolved on 18 June 2026, and the Commissioner's report under s 88 made no recommendations. A fresh complaint about the same acts would be refused as already dealt with, and the "no recommendations" report tells you how the Commission saw it.
- **A complaint about the ESC decision itself is open but narrow.** The Act makes it unlawful for a public entity to make a decision without giving proper consideration to a relevant human right (s 58(1)(b)). A decision not to act on a complaint is a decision. The "committee determined" sentence, with no right identified, is the kind of bare conclusion that supports an argument that no proper consideration was given. The preconditions are that you first complain to the entity (your 29 October letter arguably does this, because it raises ss 13, 29 and 58) and that 45 business days then elapse without an adequate response (s 65(1)). Counting from 29 October, that period runs to about 5 January 2027; a complaint could go to the Commission from about 6 January 2027. The Commission may refuse a complaint made more than a year after the act (s 70(1)(d)); the decision is 10 September 2026, so time is not a problem.
- **What it could produce.** Conciliation, and if unresolved, a report. No damages are available for unlawfulness under s 58 (s 59(3)). The Act lets a human-rights ground ride on another proceeding (s 59), but the only candidate — judicial review of the ESC decision — is already past its ordinary 28-day period. So the realistic yield is another conciliation and another report, about a complaint-handling decision rather than about what was done to you.
- **Which rights.** For a complaint-handling decision the relevant rights are thin: equality before the law (s 15) if impairment is in play; privacy (s 25) as to the footage; the rights in criminal proceedings (s 32) only by a stretch, through the withheld footage. Liberty and security (s 29) and property (s 24) attach to the 2025 conduct, which EDR19098 has already covered.

### The Anti-Discrimination Act

- **The attribute exists.** Impairment is an attribute (s 7(h)); the diagnosis of 24 October 2024 supports it. Police functions are covered: a person performing a function or exercising a power under State law must not discriminate in doing so (s 101).
- **The test is the problem.** Direct discrimination requires that you were treated less favourably than a person without the impairment would have been in the same circumstances (s 10). That requires evidence the officers knew of the diagnosis at the time and that it bore on how they treated you. From the record I have seen, that evidence is not established. A diagnosis that exists is not the same as a diagnosis that was known to the decision-maker.
- **Time.** A complaint must be made within one year of the contravention (s 138(1)); later only if the Commissioner is satisfied you have shown good cause (s 138(2)). The February–May 2025 conduct is out of time. The March 2026 conduct is within time until March 2027.
- **Victimisation does not fit as pleaded.** Victimisation (ss 129–131) is detriment imposed because the person alleged a contravention of the Anti-Discrimination Act, refused to contravene it, or is involved in a proceeding under it. Your complaints to the CCC, the OIC and ESC are not acts under that Act. Only if EDR19098 (or any other complaint lodged with the Commission before March 2026) included a discrimination claim could the March 2026 conduct be framed as victimisation for involvement in a proceeding under the Act. That is a fact for you to check.
- **The reprisal you actually allege belongs elsewhere.** "They acted against me because I complained to the CCC" is a matter for the CCC: victimising a person for helping the Commission is an offence under s 212 of the Crime and Corruption Act. That sits naturally in the single monitoring letter to the CCC, not in a Commission complaint.
- **What the Act could produce.** This is the one route with enforceable remedies: if conciliation fails, the complaint can be referred to QCAT, which can order compensation. But only on a viable claim, and the viability problem is the knowledge-and-causation evidence above.

### The Public Interest Disclosure Act

The disclosure was made to Metro South Health in May 2024 about workplace matters and determined by its Ethical Standards Unit in December 2024. Linking police conduct in 2025–2026 to that disclosure needs evidence that the officers knew of it and acted because of it. Your own project rules keep this track behind the WorkCover appeal for good reason. Leave it there.

### The risks of opening either front now

1. **Spread.** Two CCC files, an ESC file, an OIC review, a QHRC conciliation and now letters citing three further Acts. Each new body is read as a pattern, and the CCC has already asked you to stop writing to it. A respondent's easiest submission to any of these bodies is the list of bodies.
2. **Your mental health in the criminal matter.** Pleading impairment-based discrimination against police while defending charges arising from the same events puts your diagnosis in play in the proceeding where it costs most.
3. **Low yield.** Conciliation and reports, against a complaint-handling decision, after the disciplinary time limits have passed.

### Recommendation on these routes

Not as new fronts now. Three narrow things instead:

1. **Keep the human-rights question where it is** — a yes/no question to ESC about whether and how the rights were considered — and let the unanswered question sit on the record. If by early January 2027 there is still no adequate answer, decide then whether a s 58(1)(b) complaint about the ESC decision is worth a conciliation. The precondition will have been met by your 29 October letter.
2. **Protect the one Anti-Discrimination Act clock that is still running.** The March 2026 conduct can be the subject of a complaint until March 2027. Before February 2027, answer two questions on paper: what exactly was done in March 2026 (dates, instruments, who acted), and what evidence shows the officers knew of your diagnosis. If both exist, a complaint is arguable. If either is missing, do not file.
3. **Put the reprisal allegation to the CCC, not the Commission**, in the single monitoring letter already recommended, as a one-sentence reference to s 212 — "I also note that the March 2026 conduct followed my complaint to the Commission" — and no more.

**Item by item.**

|---|---|---|---|
| Officer statements | "QPS officers providing contradicting statements" | "problems with consistency of officers statements" | Something officers did became a quality of the evidence. Whether an officer's statement is accurate is a conduct question whatever a court decides. A "consistency problem" is a question for the court. |

**The heading that was added.** ESC places the first three items under a heading that appears in neither the CCC's record nor your 8 April email: that officers interfered with "your court matters". That heading is what makes the later sentence possible: "your court matters were subject to judicial arbitration and any incongruities with evidence should be raised within these matters".

**What the changes do.** Every change moves in the same direction. Conduct becomes evidence. A process outside the court becomes court disclosure. A dated event becomes a possibility. The named subject of an allegation becomes unnamed. After the changes, three items can be sent to the court, one can be put aside as unsupported, and one need not be decided, and that is what the Advice then does. The allegation about ESC is listed in the summary; the findings that follow concern "the actions of officers" and "the officers", and the only words that could reach it are the general ones, "the available evidence does not support the allegations raised".

**The framing was fixed on the first day.** ESC's request of 18 June 2026 already used the same three phrases in the same order: "inconsistent statements of officers, disclosures of court ordered material, and forensic evidence handling". It asked for "the court matter that relates to your allegations", the "date of offence", the "offence you were charged with" and the officers' names. The complaint was being treated as a court-matter complaint from the start. Nothing was asked about the Information Commissioner, March 2026 or ESC. The summary of 10 September repeats the three phrases unchanged, which suggests that nothing received in between, including the 14 July report, altered how those items were classified. A fair reading of the 18 June request is that ESC needed identifiers to find the records. It still shows which allegations were on its list.

**Why it matters.** Section 42 of the Crime and Corruption Act requires the Commissioner of Police to "deal with the complaint" the CCC refers, and to tell the complainant the reason "if no action is taken on the complaint". The complaint is the one referred. A response that gives reasons on a differently described complaint answers the description. This is the companion to the decision-maker point: that one asks who decided; this one asks what was decided. The CCC can check it in two minutes, because it wrote one of the two texts.

**The limits.**

1. ESC's summary is selective, not invented. I have not seen your emails of 17 and 24 March 2026. The repository's note on the 24 March email says it also complained that court disclosure orders had not been met. If so, "disclosures of court ordered material" has a source in your own words, and ESC will say so. The accurate way to put the point: you raised both the court orders and the Information Commissioner agreement; the CCC chose to record the Information Commissioner agreement; ESC kept only the version a court can deal with.
2. The CCC's own letter said "we may have used different words to describe your concerns", and ESC's sentence begins "Essentially". A summary does not have to be word for word. The point is not that the words differ. It is that every difference removes something ESC would otherwise have had to assess.
3. Your 29 October letter made this point, but when it listed what the CCC recorded it left out the targeted roadside interception, the item ESC changed most. Any further letter should set out all five, in the CCC's words.
4. Your own material gives two figures for the specimen gap: 124.6 hours in your 8 April email, and 52.6 hours (03:39 on 24 May to 08:18 on 26 May 2025) in the repository's summary of your 1 March 2026 complaint to the laboratory. Settle which is right before either is quoted again.

**How to use it.** In the single letter to the CCC, set the CCC's sentence of 31 March and ESC's sentence of 10 September one above the other, word for word, and add one line: "Of the five matters the Commission recorded, the Outcome Advice does not mention the Information Commissioner or the roadside interception, describes the March 2026 conduct as 'potential', and makes no specific finding on the concern about Ethical Standards Command." No adjectives. The two texts do the work.

## 7. "Raise it in court" used to close the complaint, not to hold it

1. The Advice tells you to raise the evidential matters in your court case and, in the same letter, says "Ethical Standards Command considers the matter finalised". The complaint was not held until the court decides. It was closed.
2. Under s 7.12 of the Police Service Administration Act, the only court proceeding that extends the time for disciplining an officer is one brought against the officer. Your case does not extend it. A complaint closed on this basis cannot be revived after the court decides, and for the 2025 conduct the periods have already run.
3. The same sentence was applied to matters that are not before any court: the Information Commissioner agreement, the March 2026 conduct, and ESC's own earlier handling.
4. This is not "discrimination" in law. Being charged is not one of the attributes in s 7 of the Anti-Discrimination Act. The 2024 amendment that would add "irrelevant criminal record" is still shown as uncommenced in the current Act, and it would not cover live charges arising from the very events complained about. Put the point as a problem in how the referral was handled, which is what the CCC's monitoring role is for.

A paragraph for the letter to the CCC: "The Outcome Advice directs me to raise the evidential matters in my court proceeding and in the same letter finalises the complaint. The complaint was not held pending the court's decision; it was closed. Under s 7.12 of the Police Service Administration Act my proceeding does not extend the period for disciplinary action, so a complaint closed on this basis cannot be revived after the court decides. The same sentence was applied to allegations that are not before any court: the Information Commissioner agreement, the March 2026 conduct, and Ethical Standards Command's own earlier handling."

## 8. The short version

Your decision-maker point is strong, factual and well put. It is answerable, in one sentence, but every available answer gives you a fact you do not yet have, and the body that can act on that fact is the CCC. Your 29 October letter is right in its scope, its chronology and its omissions list; it is weakened by the arrest argument and by widening into anti-discrimination. Their reasoning is defective in the ordinary administrative ways — non-responsive summary, wrong forum applied to non-court matters, conditional description of evidence, bare conclusions, undisclosed decision-maker — but it is formally a response and there is no complainant's right of review. Their silence is most likely a closed file plus legal routing plus two queues, with no duty to answer. The human-rights route on the underlying conduct is spent; on the decision itself it is open from about 6 January 2027 but yields only conciliation and a report. The anti-discrimination route has a live clock only for the March 2026 conduct and depends on evidence of knowledge that has not yet been shown. The reprisal allegation belongs with the CCC. The companion point is what was decided: ESC's statement of the complaint leaves out the Information Commissioner and the targeted interception, calls the March 2026 conduct "potential", and removes its own name from the allegation about itself. Set the CCC's sentence and ESC's sentence side by side and let the CCC read them. Nothing in this changes the advice already given: one body, one issue, one page — the Deputy Commissioner's office for the yes/no question, the Information Commissioner for the footage, the CCC once, and the court for everything evidential.
