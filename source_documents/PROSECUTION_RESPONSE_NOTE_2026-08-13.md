## 2. The structural consequence — both surviving charges now turn on the dog

This is the most important thing in the letter, and it is not stated anywhere in it.

**Charge 1 — s 421(2)** is *entering premises **and committing an indictable offence** in the premises*. It is a two-part charge. The prosecution must prove:

- (a) entry into or presence in the premises; **and**
- (b) that an indictable offence was in fact committed inside.

The prosecution has not named the indictable offence in the letter. It can only be **stealing** — the third reason given ("the property (dog) belonged to the complainant") is the tell. There is no separate stealing charge on the indictment. Stealing is being run as a *component element* of Charge 1.

**Charge 3 — s 177(2)(b) DFVPA** requires an order in force and a contravention of one of its conditions. The only conditions on the 1 December 2024 Notice (QP0899) were: be of good behaviour towards the aggrieved, and do not commit domestic violence against the aggrieved. There is no no-contact condition, no ouster, no exclusion-from-residence condition. So the alleged contravention cannot be the entry itself. It must be that taking the dog constituted "domestic violence" — presumably relying on the limb of the s 8 DFVPA definition that covers causing or threatening injury to an animal **in order to control, dominate or coerce the aggrieved**.

**The consequence: both surviving charges collapse to the same question.** Whose dog is Romeo, and what did Cory honestly believe about that on 23 February 2025?

That is a single point of failure for the entire prosecution. It is also the point on which the evidence is strongest, and the point at which police destroyed the ability to test the issue at all (§5.1 below).

---

## 3. The choice of s 421(2) is itself an admission

s 421 has two limbs. Limb (1) is entering *with intent* to commit an indictable offence. Limb (2) is entering *and committing* one — no intent at entry required.

They have charged **limb (2)**. They have not charged limb (1), and they have not charged burglary.

That is because their own investigating officer negated intent at entry, on his own body-worn camera, on the evening of 24 February 2025:

> **Const. Davies** [23:55.50] — *"but he's collected the dog which doesn't sound like he had the intent of taking the dog but he's taken it anyway."*

The charge has been drafted around a recorded admission by the officer in charge of the investigation. That is worth putting to the prosecutor in terms.

## 4. Answering each of the three stated reasons

### 4.1 — "Form 13… this was accepted, he was removed"

The prosecution is relying on a statement from the leasing manager. The same leasing manager — Denis Constable — is recorded on QPS's own body-worn camera on the night of the arrest saying the opposite, twice, to two different officers:

> **Dennis Constable to Const. Davies** — *"the lease wasn't accepted by the property manager because he was not able to come off the lease because she's not able to have the apartment with her name only on it."*

> **Const. Easthope:** *"did at any point did cory sign a notice of intent to leave?"*
> **Dennis Constable:** *"Not that I'm aware… Both parties must sign that form, Corey and Alexia… it hasn't even started and we're not aware of it."*

> **Const. Easthope:** *"who can be here and who can't be here?"*
> **Dennis Constable:** *"They're both allowed to be there, but the third party…"* (the third party being Ms Phillips)

He also telephoned Constables Bickery and Harmer at **1:54 a.m. on 24 February 2025, during the lockout**, to tell them expressly: *"Form 13 does NOT terminate the lease."* That call is recorded in his statutory declaration (AFF11 ¶29) and in the evidence-export timeline (DV-03).

And Constable Easthope's own arrival briefing at 02:00, on his own camera, before any decision was made:

> *"going to enter the property he's got the key and he's got a lease… it's his lease, his bottom and it's his key, so he's entering the property to get stuff out."*

**Action:** the leasing manager's statement now relied on by the prosecution must be disclosed immediately. There are two inconsistent accounts from the same witness. The inconsistency is itself disclosable, and the earlier account is contemporaneous, recorded, and sworn. A Form 13 is a *notice of intention* to leave. On a joint co-tenancy it does not, of itself, terminate the tenancy — which is precisely what the leasing manager told police at the time.

### 4.1A — The complainant's own court material shows a new lease commencing months later

This is the strongest answer to reason (1), and it comes from the complainant herself.

Cory's instructions are that Ms Negro filed material in court showing that **a new lease commenced months after February 2025**.

If that is right, it is decisive, and for a reason that requires no witness at all:

- **You do not need a new lease if the old one had already ended.** If the joint tenancy had genuinely terminated in December 2024 or February 2025 with Ms Negro simply continuing on it, there would be nothing to replace. The existence of a *new* agreement, commencing later, is an admission that the old agreement was still the operative one until it was replaced.
- It matches exactly what the leasing manager told Const. Davies on the night: *"the lease wasn't accepted by the property manager because he was not able to come off the lease **because she's not able to have the apartment with her name only on it**."* She could not hold the unit alone. That is precisely why a fresh agreement had to be created later — presumably on different terms or with a different co-signer.
- It is documentary, it is dated, and it was put before a court by the complainant. It cannot be re-characterised by a later witness statement.

**Action:** obtain the complainant's filed material and the new general tenancy agreement, and identify its **commencement date**. That date is the answer to reason (1). If the filing was in QCAT, obtain the QCAT file. Cross-reference RTA matter RTA:0055411.

### 4.1B — The bond was never dealt with, and the bond record is held by a third party

Cory's instructions are that the instrument that actually ends a tenancy of this kind — which he understood from Ms Negro to be a Form 20 — was **only ever sent and accepted by her**, without his signature.

*(The exact RTA form number should be confirmed against the RTA's own record rather than taken from recollection. The substance does not depend on the number.)*

The substance is corroborated on QPS's own camera. When Const. Easthope asked the leasing manager about the process, the answer was not just about the Form 13:

> **Dennis Constable:** *"Both parties must sign that form, Corey and Alexia. **And then there's another form to fill out for the bond** and for the bond…"*
> **Const. Easthope:** *"so that hasn't been finalized, is that correct?"*
> **Dennis Constable:** *"No, **it hasn't even started** and we're not aware of it."*

So the leasing manager told police, on the night, that (a) the notice required both signatures, (b) there was a **further instrument required for the bond**, and (c) none of it had started. That is the same proposition Cory is making, from the mouth of the prosecution's own tenancy witness.

Cory said the same thing to Const. Harmer within hours: *"my bond's still there, everything's still there, my name's still on the lease."*

**Why this matters more than the witness dispute:** the bond is not held by the agent or by either party. It is held by the **Residential Tenancies Authority**. The RTA's record is independent, third-party, and contemporaneous. A bond history search will show, objectively:

- the bond number and the date it was lodged;
- the names of every bond contributor;
- whether and when any change of contributors was ever processed;
- whether and when the bond was refunded, and to whom;
- whether a **new bond** was lodged for a **new tenancy**, and on what date.

That last item is the same point as §4.1A, from a second independent source. If a new bond was lodged months later, the old tenancy was still on foot until then.

**Action:** request the full RTA bond history for the premises, and the agent's tenancy ledger. This is objective documentary material that does not depend on which version of the leasing manager's account is accepted.

### 4.2 — "he clarified if he was on the lease. He was not and clearly no mistake of fact"

This reason is backwards on the prosecution's own logic.

Cory's account, given to Const. Harmer on body-worn camera within hours of the entry:

> *"So I took a week to confirm that my name was on the lease, had to confirm it with the RTA, printed out the documents, had to confirm it with the management, because my bond's still there, everything's still there, my name's still on the lease."*

Whatever the answer he received, the act of spending a week verifying with the Residential Tenancies Authority and with building management is evidence **for** an honestly held belief, not against it. A person who checks is not a person acting recklessly.

And Const. Davies, on the excluded tape:

> [07:52.96] *"he was told he was informed that he could go to that unit his name's still on the lease and alexia so that's really tricked him because she said i've seen the messages she said two to three weeks can cory come and stay here two to three weeks."*

The investigating officer records that Cory was *told he could go to the unit*, that his name was still on the lease, and that he had been "tricked". That is the opposite of "clearly no mistake of fact."

### 4.3 — "The property (dog) belonged to the complainant"

**This is the wrong test.** For a stealing-based predicate offence, ownership is not the question. The question is whether Cory acted in the exercise of an honest claim of right.

**Section 22(2) Criminal Code (Qld)** — a person is not criminally responsible for an act done with respect to property in the exercise of an honest claim of right and without intention to defraud. It is a **complete answer**. It does not require the belief to be correct. It requires it to be honest.

The material going to honest claim of right:

- Cory purchased Romeo from a Sydney breeder on 25 October 2024. He and his mother drove to Sydney to collect him. Breeder receipt in the file.
- Cory registered Romeo with the **Gold Coast City Council** — that registration pre-dates the 17 December 2024 NSW Pet Registry transfer the complainant relies on.
- WhatsApp, 25 October 2024, 06:34 — *"I got Romeo babe."*
- The complainant's own written messages: *"I won't restrict you from the animals"*; *"All in your name now"*; *"He's always happy to see you"*; *"You can trust me too."*
- Ms Phillips told police on tape that Cory had said he had a text message from the complainant giving him permission to walk the dog.
- Cory's contemporaneous words at the moment of the encounter, in Ms Phillips's own statement at ¶13: *"its my dog."*
- At the seizure on 22 March 2025 he asserted ownership three times in immediate succession on the seizing officer's own camera, and had the breeder receipt and Council registration with him.
- NSW Pet Registry Service Desk, 15 May 2025: *"As you have moved the pet interstate NSW doesn't have any authority on the pet now unless it moves back to NSW to reside."*
- The complainant herself, in writing on 19 February 2025 — four days before — classified the whole dispute as *"a legal civil case that he is required to obtain a lawyer for and dispute me for these items."*

A registration record in another State is, at highest, presumptive. It cannot convert a genuinely contested civil property claim — one the complainant herself put in writing as civil — into a criminal stealing.

**Also relevant:** the 17 December 2024 transfer was obtained during the period now documented as coercive, and is on its face vulnerable to being set aside for undue influence or unconscionable dealing (§4.4). But that argument is not even necessary. The honest claim of right answers the charge whether or not the transfer stands.

**The short point on the dog:** Cory bought Romeo. He and his mother drove to Sydney to collect him on 25 October 2024. He registered him in his own name in Queensland with the Gold Coast City Council, and that registration pre-dates the 17 December 2024 NSW transfer the complainant relies on. Queensland is where the dog lived, where the dog was registered, and where all of this happened. The NSW Pet Registry itself confirmed in writing on 15 May 2025 that once the animal moved interstate, *"NSW doesn't have any authority on the pet now."* A man who buys a dog, drives interstate to collect it, registers it in his own name in his own State, and says *"its my dog"* at the moment in question is not a thief — at the very lowest he is a man with an honest claim of right, and that is the end of the predicate offence.

### 4.4 — Diagnosed impairment and the circumstances in which the signatures were obtained

Cory was diagnosed with major depressive disorder. That diagnosis is in the medical records (06_MEDICAL), along with PTSD and anxiety, and he was on prescribed Lisdexamfetamine, Fluoxetine and Quetiapine.

His instructions are that Ms Negro took advantage of that condition. On the documentary record, the same diagnosed condition appears at three separate points, and it is used against him at every one of them:

1. **The dog transfer of 17 December 2024** — signed during the period the message record shows as coercive, weeks after the December 2024 events, at a time when he was diagnosed and medicated. The elements of unconscionable dealing are all present in the material: a **special disadvantage** (diagnosed MDD in the immediate aftermath of a relationship breakdown and a police protection notice), that disadvantage **known** to the other party (she was his partner of five years and had told police about his diagnosis), and **advantage taken** of it to obtain a transfer of property for no consideration. *Commercial Bank of Australia Ltd v Amadio* (1983) 151 CLR 447. Undue influence runs in parallel.

2. **The Form 13 signature** — obtained in the same period and in the same conditions, and then relied on by police and now by the prosecution as the reason he was "removed." Cory's own account, on Const. Harmer's camera hours after the arrest: *"It took me one whole week to confirm that I was being tricked to think that my name was off the lease… she tried to trick me and force me to say that she's safe and say like two to three weeks."* Const. Davies adopted the same characterisation on his own camera the next evening: *"that's really tricked him."*

3. **The Police Protection Notice itself** — the grounds recite: *"The agg is aware that the resp has a mental illness and requires medication that he does not take regularly, and this can cause the resp to act crazy and go into rages without warning."* He was on his prescribed medication. He was working and studying. The building manager's sworn evidence describes him as *"consistently helpful and devoted."*

So the same condition was, on this record, the thing that was exploited to obtain the signatures, and then the thing cited to police as the reason he was dangerous. That is a single coherent pattern, not three separate coincidences.

**Relevance to each forum:**

- **Charge 1** — goes to honest claim of right, and to the vulnerability of the transfer the prosecution relies on for "the property (dog) belonged to the complainant."
- **Charge 3** — goes to the reliability of the Notice whose contravention is alleged.
- **QHRC (EDR19098)** — impairment is a protected attribute. Conduct that takes an impairment and converts it into the stated reason a person is a danger is squarely within the discrimination framework, and it was adopted by police without verification against the medical record they never asked for.

## 5. Did officers act outside their power?

The change in the charge sharpens this considerably. Taking it in order of strength.

### 5.1 — Giving Romeo to the complainant with no court order **(strongest)**

Police seized Romeo on 22 March 2025 on the stated basis that it was "stolen property." They then handed it to Ms Negro before any court order. That was admitted orally by QPS at the Form 5 hearing in August 2025.

There is no power to do that. The return-of-thing provisions of the PPRA (s 691 and related) direct a seized thing back to the person from whom it was seized, or to disposal by court order. Handing seized property to a competing third-party claimant on police say-so is not a power that exists.

**What the new charge does to this:** the ownership of the dog is now *the contested element of Charge 1*. The dog is the central exhibit in a live criminal proceeding. Police disposed of the central exhibit into the hands of the complainant. The defence can no longer independently test identity or ownership — no microchip scan, no independent veterinary record, no chain of custody. The prosecution asserts "the property (dog) belonged to the complainant" while relying on a state of affairs its own officers created and made untestable.

That is worth a formal notice.

### 5.2 — Seizing the phone and omitting it from the property receipt

Field Property Receipt 137237 lists "1 x dog" and nothing else. Cory's iPhone was taken the same morning. Recording seized property is not discretionary.

Compounding it: on tape during interview Cory identified a specific exculpatory message on that phone —

> **Cory:** *"Delete the message from Alexis saying I could walk the dog."*
> **Officer:** *"We're not going to do that. We want to see what's in the phone."*
> **Officer:** *"Your phone's been seized for the matter of the investigation."*

The phone has now been held for over fourteen months. It has not been forensically examined. The message identified on tape has never been produced. On the new charge, that message goes directly to the predicate offence.

### 5.3 — Directing the lessor's agent, and threatening him

Const. Davies, on his own camera, to the property manager:

> *"you've cancelled all those fobs on that unit so you need to reinstate those immediately because Corey's not the leaseholder… cory will not be going back to the foreseeable future."*

and

> *"your behaviour, like cancelling those fobs, it's been pretty unprofessional, okay? So it's obstructed police."*

Police have no power to direct a lessor's agent on tenancy access, no power to impose a de facto ouster in the absence of any ouster condition or court order, and no basis to threaten an obstruction charge against a person who had acted on the lawful instruction of a co-tenant. The property manager had corrected the officer on the lease position on the same recording.

The same threat pattern appears with the independent witness — *"we're starting to look at, you know, parties to offences and, like, possessing tainted property"* — said to Tom Balsey at his own home.

### 5.4 — Disposing of evidence handed to police at the scene

Tom Balsey gave Const. Bickery, the arresting officer, a printed packet at the scene: the lease, the email chain, the complainant's messages inviting Cory to stay two to three weeks, the terms of the Notice. It was not logged. It was not uploaded. It was left inside Unit 104 with Ms Phillips — the prosecution's principal witness.

### 5.5 — Using domestic violence emergency powers where the aggrieved was interstate

The forced entry at 1:50 a.m., the exclusion, the seizure of keys, and the pre-charge announcement of the bail objective were all carried out under a domestic violence framework. Those powers require a reasonable belief of a threat requiring protective action.

The aggrieved was in Perth. Officers recorded it themselves:

> **Const. Easthope** [18:18] — *"is on the way back right now she's on the plane."*
> **Const. Bickery**, per DV-02 — *"Aggrieved in Perth and currently on a flight."*
> **Const. Davies** [31:44] — *"alexia was 2 000 kilometres away."*

She gave no sworn statement until 25 February — two days after the operative decisions. She made no report to Western Australia Police at any point, in the State where she actually was. And she had classified the dispute in writing, four days earlier, as a civil case.

**What the new charge does to this:** the prosecution has now itself reframed the substance as a property offence — entry plus stealing a dog. A contested civil property dispute cannot ground the exercise of emergency domestic violence powers. The prosecution's own characterisation of the case now undercuts the lawful basis on which the operation was conducted.

### 5.6 — Announcing the bail objective before charging

On the transport recording, before any charge was laid:

> *"we haven't charged him yet so yeah so he'll be young like we're gonna put him on bowel [bail] conditions not to go back there."*

The exclusion from the residence is stated as the objective, and the charge as the means of achieving it. That is a purpose question, and it is now more acute: the charge that was eventually framed is one built around a stealing allegation that the same investigation's officer had, hours earlier, recorded as lacking intent.

## 6. What the Charge 2 discontinuance is worth

It should not be treated as a minor concession.

Const. Davies described that charge on his own body-worn camera on 24 February 2025:

> [27:16.84] *"you also mentioned at the start about the stalking charges that we put on him it's it's a blanket charge i believe it's stalk harass intimidate that they all fall under the one umbrella right and so the harassment side of that are some emails that he sent to alexia but also her family."*

The prosecution has now accepted the submission and will discontinue it. That confirms, from the prosecution's own side, that a charge was laid whose substance the investigating officer had already characterised on tape as emails between former partners. It also disposes of the "we were stalking her" assertion made by Ms Phillips in her email to police, about a man she had never met before that night.

Three consequences:

1. **Bail conditions** imposed by reference to the stalking charge should be reviewed now.
2. **The QHRC matter** — this materially strengthens it. The officer's own recorded characterisation of the charge is now vindicated by the prosecution's own concession.
3. **It confirms the pattern.** A charge was laid, maintained for approximately eighteen months, and then conceded — on material that was in the officer's own mouth on the second day.

## 8. The one point to be careful about

The strike-out on 25 February 2025 does not, by itself, establish that the Notice was not in force on 23 February 2025. A police protection notice generally continues until the court deals with the application. That needs to be pleaded precisely rather than assumed.

The stronger point on Charge 3 does not depend on the strike-out at all: **the order contained no condition capable of being contravened by this conduct.** Good behaviour and no domestic violence — nothing else. Const. Easthope said so on his own camera at the scene:

> [17:14.48] *"she's told him the wrong conditions because technically he's allowed to be here."*

That point stands whether the order was in force or not.

## 9. Summary

- Charge 2 is gone. It should not have been laid, and the officer's own recording said so on day two.
- Charge 1 has been drafted around a recorded admission by the investigating officer that there was no intent at entry — hence s 421(2) rather than limb (1) or burglary.
- Charge 1 and Charge 3 now both rest on the same proposition: that taking Romeo was a criminal act. Honest claim of right under s 22(2) is a complete answer to it, and the material supporting an honest belief is documentary, contemporaneous, and in part comes from the complainant's own messages.
- The prosecution's first stated reason relies on a witness account that contradicts what the same witness told two police officers on tape that night and what he swore in his statutory declaration — and, more importantly, is contradicted by the complainant's own court material showing a new lease commencing months later, and by the RTA's own bond record.
- The prosecution's second stated reason describes a week of verification with the RTA and building management, and treats it as proof of no mistake — it is evidence of the opposite.
- The prosecution's third stated reason asserts a fact about the dog that its own officers made impossible to test, by giving the dog away without a court order — and rests on a transfer obtained from a man with diagnosed major depressive disorder, by the person who had told police about that diagnosis.

The narrower the case becomes, the worse the conduct around the exhibit looks.

**The two documents to get first:** the new lease with its commencement date, and the RTA bond history. Both are held by third parties, both are objective, and either one on its own answers the prosecution's primary reason for rejecting the submission on Charge 1.
