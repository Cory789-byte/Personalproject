## PART C — Problems in the existing case file

These are errors or overstatements in the material as it stands. Each of them would be found by a competent opponent.

### C.1 — The strike-out probably does **not** mean the order was not in force on 23 February 2025

The file repeatedly states, as if dispositive, that "Element 1 fails — TPO struck out." That is likely wrong as an answer to Charge 3.

A police protection notice operates as an application for a protection order, and generally continues in force until the court decides the application. Magistrate Brunello dealt with the application on **25 February**. The conduct alleged is **23 February**. On the orthodox analysis the notice was in force at the relevant time, and the later strike-out does not retroactively unmake it.

The strike-out remains highly relevant — to the reliability of the underlying allegations, to the continuation of the prosecution afterwards, to Yaun's "no evidence" admission, and to the whole human rights case. But it should not be pleaded as an answer to the "order in force" element. If it is, it will fail and it will cost credibility on the points that do work.

**The good news:** the conditions argument (C.2) does not depend on this at all.

### C.2 — "There was no condition capable of being breached" is stated too strongly

The file says repeatedly that because the only conditions were good behaviour and no domestic violence, entry could not breach the order. That is right as far as entry goes. But it is not the prosecution's case.

Their case must be that **taking the dog** was domestic violence, relying on the limb of the statutory definition covering conduct in relation to animals. That is not an absurd theory — the DFVPA covers animal-related conduct deliberately, because animals are genuinely used as levers in real cases.

So the answer is not "there was no relevant condition." The answer is:

- the conduct was not directed at controlling, dominating or coercing her — she was 3,500 km away and did not know until she was told;
- he honestly believed the dog was his, and had bought and registered it;
- the investigating officer recorded that he did not go there intending to take the dog;
- she had written that he would not be restricted from the animals.

That is a much better argument, and it survives contact with the actual provision.

### C.3 — The December transfer is not obviously void

The file treats the 17 December 2024 NSW registration transfer as voidable, almost as a given. It is not a given. He signed it. It is on a government register.

Setting it aside requires establishing special disadvantage, knowledge of it, and unconscientious advantage taken — and the party asserting it carries that. The MDD diagnosis, the timing, and her knowledge of the diagnosis make it genuinely arguable, but it is an argument to be run and proved, not a conclusion to be assumed.

**In the criminal matter this does not matter much**, because honest claim of right does not require the transfer to be invalid — only that the belief was honestly held. That distinction should be kept sharp. The criminal answer is *honest belief*; the civil answer is *voidability*. Do not let them blur.

### C.4 — He did sign a Form 13, and that is a real fact

The file frames the Form 13 as effectively irrelevant. The prosecution is entitled to say he signed a document saying he intended to leave the tenancy, and that is not nothing.

The answer is not that it does not exist. The answer is that a single co-tenant's notice of intention does not end a joint tenancy, that the property manager said exactly that at the time, that the bond process never started, and that a new lease had to be created months later. That answer depends on documents not yet obtained (see D.1, D.2).

### C.5 — The "prison for 18 months in America" claim is not supported, and repeating it is dangerous

Cory's mother said on Const. Davies's camera: *"alexia has been to prison for domestic violence for 18 months i think in america"*, and Davies replied *"i've been told about that yeah."*

The actual documentary record does not support it. Miami-Dade Court Case M18017586 shows: arrest 21 June 2018 by Miami Beach police; charge of misdemeanor battery; intake unit classified as domestic violence; a pre-trial stay-away order; **disposition nolle prosequi**; case closed 8 August 2018. That is roughly seven weeks from arrest to closure, and the charge was **dropped**. There is no custodial sentence in that record, let alone eighteen months.

The mother's statement is her honest recollection, prefaced with "I think." Davies's reply is an acknowledgement that he had been told something, not confirmation that it is true.

**If anyone advances "she went to prison for 18 months for domestic violence" it will be destroyed**, and the damage will spread to every other proposition being advanced. The supportable statement is narrow: there was a 2018 arrest and misdemeanor battery charge in Miami, intake-classified as domestic violence, with a stay-away order, and the charge was not pursued. Even that has limited admissibility and limited relevance — it goes at most to what QPS knew when assessing credibility, not to whether she is telling the truth now.

The same applies to "criminal history in Spain and Italy." There are no documents for either. Do not assert them.

### C.6 — Some of the file records assertion as though it were evidence

Examples: "user-confirmed (4 May 2026): Negro had no marks on her neck"; the account of the August 2025 hearing admission, which the file itself flags as recollection with no transcript. These are fine as instructions. They are not evidence, and the documents should keep the line visible. The August 2025 transcript should be ordered (D.5).

### C.7 — The drug-driving matter has one real analytical risk

The cross-reactivity argument is sound in structure: Lisdexamfetamine metabolises to dexamfetamine, and without confirmatory chromatography you cannot distinguish an amphetamine-class prescription metabolite from a proscribed analyte.

But it only holds **if the analyte actually certified was in that class**. If the certificate records methylamphetamine or MDMA or THC, the prescription argument does not answer it, and the defence rests entirely on chain of custody, the destruction of the specimen, and the disclosure failures.

**Find out what the certificate actually says before building anything on cross-reactivity.** The chain-of-custody and destruction points are the durable ones and do not depend on the analyte.

Note also that the destruction cuts both ways: it prevents retesting, but a certificate of analysis may still be admissible as prima facie evidence. The disclosure failure under the mandatory framework, and the never-conducted non-conforming-work investigation, are what attack the certificate itself.

### C.8 — The human rights complaint may have a time-limit problem

A human rights complaint generally has to be made within **one year** of the alleged contravention, after first complaining to the public entity and waiting out the response period. The core conduct is 23–24 February 2025.

**Confirm the date of the original complaint to QPS and the date of first lodgement with the Commission.** If first lodgement was after February 2026, the limit is in play and will need either the continuing-contravention characterisation (the prosecution continues; the phone is still held; the address record was changed later) or an exercise of discretion.

This is worth checking now rather than discovering at a hearing.

### C.9 — Breadth carries a presentational risk

Two CCC matters, OIC external review, QHRC, RTI, an FSQ statutory complaint, immigration reports, a reverse protection order application, a Form 5 originating application, and a WorkCover matter running in parallel.

Every one of them is individually justifiable. Collectively, an opponent will characterise it as a person who complains about everyone. The August 2025 costs refusal is a useful counter — the Magistrate declined costs against him despite QPS pushing for them — but the risk is real and should be managed by **leading with the two or three strongest documents in any forum**, not the whole file.

## PART F — Summary

**The criminal matter is in a materially better position than it was.** One of three charges is gone. The two that remain have been narrowed onto a single question — the dog — where the evidence is documentary and largely favourable, and where a complete statutory answer exists in the form of honest claim of right. The prosecution's own three stated reasons are civil-law propositions, two of which are contradicted by the contemporaneous evidence of their own tenancy witness.

**The most valuable work available is not argument. It is two documents** — the later lease and the bond record — both held by third parties, both objective, and either of which answers the prosecution's primary reason without requiring anyone to be believed.

**The biggest risks are self-inflicted.** Pleading the strike-out as an answer to "order in force" will fail. Repeating the "18 months in prison" claim will do real damage. Leading forensic-linguistic video analysis will undermine the simple and powerful contradictions that sit beside it. Each of these can be avoided at no cost.

**The human rights matter is substantively strong and procedurally exposed.** Check the time limit now.

**The conduct issues around the exhibit get worse as the case narrows.** When the case was about stalking and a protection order, the dog was peripheral. Now that the case is only about the dog, the fact that police gave it away before any court order — and that nobody can now test its identity or ownership — sits at the centre of a charge the prosecution has chosen to maintain.
