## 3. What the law requires of police

**Accuracy.**

- Before 1 July 2025, police had to take reasonable steps, before using personal information, to make sure it was accurate, complete and up to date (former privacy principle 8), and to amend it on request (former principle 7). Police were excused only from principles 2, 3, 9, 10 and 11, and only where that was necessary (former s 29).
- Since 1 July 2025, police must take reasonable steps to make sure information they use or disclose is accurate, up to date, complete and relevant (principle 10.2), and must correct it on request (principle 13). Police are excused only from principles 3.6, 5, 6 and 10.1 (s 29).
- Police were never excused from the duties this matter is about.

**Correction (Right to Information Act 2009).**

- Anyone who has had access to a police document, "whether or not under this Act", may apply to amend personal information in it that is "inaccurate, incomplete, out of date or misleading" (s 78E(1)). You hold the 18 March statement, so you can apply now.
- The application must be in writing, identify the document, give an address for notices, and include evidence of identity (s 78E(4)).
- Police can refuse if they are not satisfied the information is wrong, if it is not your information, or if the document is not part of a record "available for use in the day-to-day or ordinary performance" of their functions (s 78S). These are the grounds to answer in advance.
- If police do not decide in time, they are taken to have refused (s 78R).
- Police may correct by altering the entry or by adding a notation (s 78U). If they refuse, you can require them to add your notation (s 78V).
- You can seek internal review (s 80) or go straight to the Information Commissioner (s 85). An application to the Commissioner must be made within 20 business days of the decision (s 88(1)(d)).

**Privacy complaint (Information Privacy Act 2009).**

- You complain to police first, in writing, with particulars, within 12 months of becoming aware of the act, or a longer period police agree to (s 166A).
- Police have 45 business days to respond (s 164A).
- You can then complain to the Information Commissioner if the response is not adequate or there is none (s 166(3)). The Commissioner may decline once 12 months have passed after the response period or the response (s 168(1)(f)).
- If mediation does not resolve it, you can ask within 20 business days for the complaint to go to the tribunal (ss 175A and 176).
- The tribunal can order that police not repeat the act, apologise, make stated amendments to documents, take a stated step to compensate, and pay up to $100,000, "including for any injury to the complainant's feelings or humiliation" (s 178(a)).
- For acts before 1 July 2025 the former rules still apply (s 223). Under them the Commissioner may decline once 12 months have passed since you first became aware of the act (former s 168(1)(f)). That is a choice, not a bar.

## 5. Police's best answers, and the replies

1. **"He told the managers he was staying with his mother, and his mother told us so."** Your email of 20 February says "temporarily visiting family", and a message the building manager passed on says "a temporary stay with my mother". Reply: a short stay is not a residence. On 24 February you told police on camera you were not staying with your mother and had nowhere. Police's own court brief, made after the arrest, still gave Unit 104, and the same officer wrote Unit 104 for service on 18 March.
2. **"The statement records what was known at the time."** Reply: the Act covers information that is "out of date or misleading", not only false (s 78E(1)). At the least, a notation must be added on request (s 78V). In Z18 [2020] QICmr 8 the Commissioner refused a correction at [48] and [66] even though it had rejected a police argument at [32]. That is why Annex 2 asks for a notation in the alternative.
3. **"Not a working record."** Reply: the statement is part of the police file for the job and was filed in court in MAG-00045795/25 (s 78S(2)).
4. **"Out of time."** Reply: correction has no time limit. The complaint is about acts within time. For the 2025 acts: a criminal case on the same events is still running, you raised the address with police on 18 May 2025 (RTI/54169), and the records needed to particularise the complaint have not yet been released.
5. **"The criminal court is the place for this."** Reply: the criminal court decides the charges. It does not correct a database entry or remedy its use.
6. **"Police are exempt."** Reply: police were never exempt from the accuracy and correction duties (former s 29 and s 29).

## 6. What the release should show, and what each result means

- **The date of the Benowa entry.** If it was made on or after 24 February 2025, it was made after you told police on camera you were not staying with your mother.
- **Its source.** If it came from the officers' visit to your mother's unit, police recorded a temporary arrangement as a residence. If it shows no source, the entry is unsupported.
- **Whether the typed form was filled from the database.** The Information Commissioner's report "Prioritising privacy to keep victims safe" (tabled 10 December 2025) found police forms filled from their database gave addresses to people who should not have had them.
- **Who received the address.** Any letter or contact with the Benowa complex manager or anyone else is a separate use or disclosure.
- **The bail record.** If the record still showed the old condition after the court changed it, or police gave out the old condition after that date, that is the second inaccurate record.
- **Do not ask for the record of who viewed your file.** Those logs have been refused since the Court of Appeal's decision in Commissioner of the Police Service v Shelton [2020] QCA 96. Ask for the record of each change instead. Shelton at [46] to [49] still requires the documents themselves to be examined.

## 7. Decisions that support this matter

- ZIL v Queensland Police Service [2019] QCAT 79, and Queensland Police Service v ZIL [2019] QCATA 162: police in breach over a misused address; their appeal dismissed.
- RM v Queensland Police Service [2017] QCAT 71 and CH v Queensland Police Service [2019] QCAT 297: apology and compensation ordered.
- Eaves v Commissioner of Police [2018] QCAT 180 at [55] and [65] to [68]: neither blanket immunity nor blanket release; confidential inspection by counsel.
- Isles and Queensland Police Service [2018] QICmr 27: the Events tab and the Tasks/Flags tab released without redaction.
- Commissioner of the Police Service v Shelton [2020] QCA 96 at [13] and [46] to [49]: access logs are not protected as a class; the documents must be examined; "amendments made" means changes of substance.

**Against.** Z18 [2020] QICmr 8 at [48] and [66]: correction refused. X59 [2021] QICmr 55, I63 [2024] QICmr 34 and W64 [2025] QICmr 32: access logs refused since Shelton.
