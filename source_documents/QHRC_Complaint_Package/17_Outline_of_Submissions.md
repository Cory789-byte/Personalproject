# Outline of submissions

Cory Lea Shepherd against the State of Queensland (Queensland Police Service). For use if the Commissioner raises acceptance, at conciliation, and as the base of the contentions if the complaint is referred to the tribunal. Not lodged with the complaint unless your lawyers decide otherwise.

Section numbers are to the Anti-Discrimination Act 1991 unless another Act is named. "Para" refers to the statement of complaint.

## 1. The issues

1. Was a one-business-day deadline, set for a man police had recorded as mentally ill, a term he could not meet because of his impairment, and was it reasonable?
2. Did the decision of 10 September 2026 give proper consideration to his human rights?
3. In the alternative, was the 2026 conduct victimisation?
4. On 1 December 2024, was a characteristic imputed to people with a mental illness a substantial reason for how police treated his account and his report?
5. From December 2024 to April 2025, was his sex a substantial reason for the difference in how police treated the two sides of one dispute?
6. Is there good cause to accept the events before 18 June 2026?

## 2. The 2026 deadline

2.1 **Area.** Police dealing with a complaint are "within at least s 101" (Woodforth v State of Queensland [2017] QCA 100 at [24]). Police exercising their statutory functions "must not have discriminated" (Cook v State of Queensland (QPS) [2018] QCAT 216 at [36]).

2.2 **Term.** A term includes a "condition, requirement or practice, whether or not written" (s 11(4)). It must be "formulated with some precision" (Leah v A.N. Burnett Investments [2025] QCAT 123 at [126]). This one is in writing: particulars by close of business the next day (para 42).

2.3 **Could not comply.** "Serious disadvantage" in complying is enough (Harding v State of Queensland (QPS) [2025] QCAT 415 at [90]). In Hunter v State of Queensland [2015] QCAT 179 at [61] the complainant "was required to adapt to a process which was straightforward and quick for persons without a disability". So was this complainant.

2.4 **Reasonableness is for the respondent to prove** (former s 205, applied by s 285AA). The circumstances include "the consequences of failure to comply with the term" and "the cost of alternative terms" (s 11(2)). The test is "less demanding than one of necessity, but more demanding than a test of convenience" (Ali v State of Queensland [2023] QCAT 109 at [35]). A term is unreasonable where the alternative has "little or no resource implications" (Tafao v State of Queensland [2020] QCATA 76 at [170]). The consequence here was that his complaint was decided without him. The alternative was more time, which cost nothing: police then took nearly three months to decide.

2.5 **Knowledge.** The person imposing a term need not be aware of the indirect discrimination (s 11(3)). In any case, police wrote on 1 December 2024 that he has "a mental illness and requires medication". This is not a case where police did not know (compare DM v State of Queensland [2025] QCAT 517 at [23]).

## 3. Human rights: the decision of 10 September 2026

3.1 A public entity must give proper consideration to relevant human rights, which includes identifying them (Human Rights Act 2019 s 58(1)(b), (5)).

3.2 The question is whether the decision-maker "seriously turned his or her mind to the possible impact of the decision" (Owen-D'Arcy v Chief Executive, Queensland Corrective Services [2021] QSC 273 at [137]). It is "a standard of consideration higher than that generally applicable at common law" (Mizner v State of Queensland [2025] QCA 169 at [268]). There is "no place for deference" (Owen-D'Arcy at [140]).

3.3 The outcome identifies no right. Failing to identify a relevant right made the decision unlawful in Owen-D'Arcy (at [265]–[266]).

## 4. Victimisation, in the alternative

4.1 The elements are an act, detriment, and "a causal nexus" with the matters in s 130 (Bero v Wilmar Sugar Pty Ltd & Ors [2016] QCAT 371 at [150]).

4.2 The complainant alleged discrimination in writing on 3 May 2026 in a complaint to which the respondent was a party. The deadline was set on 18 June 2026, the day that complaint was reported unresolved.

4.3 The respondent can answer one question: is one business day its practice for every complainant? If yes, it is a term and section 2 applies. If no, the respondent must explain why it was set for him.

## 5. The night of 1 December 2024: impairment

5.1 Discrimination on the basis of an attribute includes discrimination on the basis of a characteristic often imputed to people with the attribute (s 8(b)). The attribute need only be a substantial reason (s 10(4)). Motive is irrelevant (s 10(3)).

5.2 Police wrote into their grounds that his illness "can cause the resp to act crazy and go into rages without warning". They then recorded and set aside his explanation, and took no statement about his own report of being assaulted later that night.

5.3 A complaint that police did not properly investigate a man's assault reports, where he pointed to mental-health entries in police records, survived a strike-out application in Tedesco v State of Queensland [2025] QCAT 109 at [8], [15].

## 6. December 2024 to April 2025: sex

6.1 **Comparator.** The comparison is with treatment "in response to occurrences of the same kind" (Woodforth at [54]). A characteristic of the attribute cannot also be a circumstance of the comparison (at [53]).

6.2 **Inference.** Discrimination "is rarely overt and usually covert" (Watego v State of Queensland [2022] QCAT 341 at [25]). It may be inferred from a combination of facts "none of which viewed alone would support that inference" (Jenkins v State of Queensland [2018] QCAT 154 at [68]). An explanation "found to be dishonest" allows the inference "with greater certainty" (Watego at [32]).

6.3 **The facts relied on.** One dispute, decided twice, with opposite answers (para 34(b)). A reason given to the building manager, "cory's not the leaseholder", that contradicted what he had just told police (para 19). A property receipt promising a court process that was not used (para 26). An officer's answer to his documents: "It doesn't matter" (para 25).

6.4 **The practice, in the alternative.** A uniform rule is "potentially indirect discrimination, not direct discrimination" (Tafao at [64]). The law gave police a neutral route, an application to a magistrate (Police Powers and Responsibilities Act 2000 s 694), which police have used for a dog claimed by a former partner (Kaus v Coaker & Anor [2014] QMC 24), and on which each claimant must advance evidence of ownership (Blyton v Bates [2021] QDC 180 at [19]).

## 7. No exemption

7.1 Section 106 reaches only a court order or a provision in existence on 30 June 1992 (s 106(2); Malaxetxebarria v State of Queensland [2007] QCA 132 at [17]–[18]). The respondent has conceded the Police Powers and Responsibilities Act 2000 is not such a provision, and the tribunal said: "One can exercise powers in a way which is nevertheless discriminatory, and they are not reasonable just because the police officer in question was exercising a statutory power" (Philipp v State of Queensland (QPS) [2025] QCAT 505 at [185]).

7.2 The respondent must prove any exemption (former s 206).

## 8. Time

8.1 The Commissioner "is not fettered by rigid rules but must take into account all of the relevant circumstances of the particular case" (Buderim Ginger Ltd v Booth [2002] QCA 177 at [22]). A medical condition and pursuing other avenues were evidence of good cause there (at [7]).

8.2 The factors are delay, explanation, prejudice and merits (Smythe v State of Queensland [2024] QCAT 33 at [16]). Smythe failed because the complainant "prioritised other matters" (at [26]) and footage was lost (at [33]–[34]). Here the other matters were these matters, against this respondent, and the footage exists.

8.3 Delay caused by waiting on an internal complaint, without prejudice, did not defeat the complaint in Leah (at [28]–[30]).

8.4 The respondent has been dealing with these events before this Commission since April 2026 (EDR19098). It cannot claim surprise.

8.5 At the least, the in-time part should be accepted and the rest deferred until conciliation has been tried (s 141A).

## 9. Remedy

9.1 The tribunal may order compensation and other relief (s 209). Against police, indirect impairment discrimination has drawn $8,000 (Hunter at [61]) and $10,000 (Harding).
