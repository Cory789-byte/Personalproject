## A. Summary

1. Between December 2024 and March 2025 the Queensland Police Service dealt twice with the same dispute over the same dog between the same two people. When the complainant, a man, reported that people had entered his home, pushed him and taken the dog, police called it "a civil issue", told him to "let it go", took no statement from him, and left the dog with his former partner. When his former partner, a woman, reported that he had taken the dog, police called it "stolen property", charged him, visited his mother's home at night, telephoned his family, seized the dog from him while he showed them his receipts — "regardless of what paperwork he has" — and released it to her without the court application their own property receipt said would be made.

2. On 24 February 2025 officers said on camera that the complainant, a named tenant, was "technically ... allowed to be here" and that "no one's done anything there". The building manager confirmed his tenancy to them on speaker at 2:28 am. Police arrested him at 2:35 am, offered support services to a woman who was on neither the lease nor the order, and at 5:23 am told the building manager the complainant was "not the leaseholder" and would "not be going back".

3. In 2026 police gave a man whose records note "a mental illness and requires medication" until the close of business the next day to supply particulars of his complaint, and three business days to supply anything else. He could not meet either limit. Police later closed the complaint without contacting him again, and without referring to the report he sent them on 14 July 2026.

4. The complainant says that his sex, and characteristics often imputed to men in domestic disputes, were a substantial reason for the treatment in paragraphs 1 and 2; that police imposed practices with which men, as the large majority of respondents, are less able to comply; and that the 2026 time limits discriminated against him because of his impairment.

## C. What happened

### The night of 1 to 2 December 2024

6. At 8:30 pm on 1 December 2024 police issued a protection notice naming the complainant as respondent, with a good-behaviour condition only, on his former partner's account of an incident on 27 November 2024, which he denies. The notice records his address as Unit 104 and that he has "a mental illness and requires medication". The grounds police recorded include her statement that his mental illness "can cause the resp to act crazy and go into rages without warning", and his explanation, given by telephone, that a bruise she showed police may have come from a beauty treatment [5]. Messages between them and the building's CCTV show them living together during the days she told police she had stayed elsewhere [4A]. Police did not check.
7. At about 11:05 pm people the complainant did not know entered Unit 104. He was pushed, his ankle was injured, and his dog Romeo was taken. He called triple zero [6].
8. When police arrived, and later at the police station, he reported the assault, the entry and the taking of the dog, offered documents showing the dog was his, and asked that those responsible be charged. Officers told him to "let it go" and that ownership of the dog was "a civil issue" in which police would not intervene. No statement was taken from him [7]–[8].
9. Police escorted his former partner into Unit 104 to collect belongings. An officer told him she would collect only uncontested personal items. The dog, which was contested, stayed with her [9].
10. The next day police took a signed statement from her alleging that money was missing from her wallet [10].
11. The complainant had bought Romeo in December 2023, and the council registration and vet records are in his name [1], [3], [4]. Police's own December record has him saying that some animals were in his name and some in hers [11].

### The order and the dog

12. On 3 December 2024 a temporary protection order was made by consent and without admissions. It required good behaviour and allowed his former partner to enter Unit 104 with police to collect "undisputed personal property". It did not exclude the complainant from the unit and said nothing about the dog [12].
13. Between 16 and 19 December 2024, while she had the dog, the complainant signed a microchip-registry change-of-owner form at her request [16]. On 4 February 2025 he wrote that he still had "every legal right to still contest ownership even if in your name" [17].

### The home, 24 February 2025

14. The complainant was Tenant 1 under a lease running to 10 April 2025. His notice of intention to leave was "without grounds" and could not take effect before the fixed term ended. The bond authority recorded him as the only bond contributor on 24 February 2025. His former partner's own new lease began on 10 April 2025 [2], [14], [33], [43].
15. After police arrived, officers said on camera "technically he's allowed to be here" and "there's no contact conditions in there, so no one's done anything there" [26].
16. An officer asked his former partner's friend, who was on neither the lease nor the order, whether she wanted support or referral services. No welfare inquiry was made of the complainant, although police records noted his mental illness [5], [28].
17. On speaker, an officer asked the building manager "whose name is on the lease?". The building manager answered that both names were on it and "as far as I'm concerned, they're both allowed to be there" [29].
18. Five minutes later the complainant was arrested "for breach of the domestic violence order" [30].
19. At 5:23 am an officer telephoned the building manager and told him to reinstate access "because cory's not the leaseholder", that the complainant would "not be going back ... for foreseeable future", and that he was "inconveniencing a domestic violence victim survivor who's been put through a lot by her partner". The building manager answered that the change "was not accepted" and that the complainant was "still on the lease". Police had not spoken to the property manager [31].
20. Within the hour, in the recorded interview, the interviewing officer twice called the complainant's former partner "the other leaseholder" [32].
21. That evening two officers attended the complainant's mother's home about the dog. One said police were "trying to sort of assist her all we can"; an officer told his mother he had "essentially terrorized a female person"; and, about tracing the dog, an officer said "if there are cameras then we can get access to them" [34].
21A. At about 11:10 pm the same officers went on to the home of the complainant's friend, where the complainant was. An officer said "We just want to find the dog, man". Police had already spoken to the friend about the dog that morning. An officer told the friend that all police wanted was the dog, and: "that's all we want then we can stop just harassing him". An officer also told him: "we're starting to look at parties to offences and possessing tainted property" [34A].
21B. The friend said the complainant "was told it was a civil matter" when the dog was taken from him. An officer answered that there was a "civil aspect", that police had "a document that shows that he's transferred ownership", and: "when there is contested property we don't typically get involved but when we think it's stolen property we do" [34A].
22. On 25 February 2025 the application was withdrawn and the order vacated [35].

### The dog, March 2025

23. On 3 and 4 March 2025 police telephoned the complainant's sister's partner four times about the dog and questioned his sister, then 16 [37].
24. On 18 March 2025 police recorded the complainant's mother's home as his address and described Romeo as "the aggrieved's dog" [38].
24A. On the morning of 22 March 2025 an officer from another unit stopped the complainant to serve domestic violence papers and told him Southport officers "were wanting to speak to you". The complainant answered: "I've put them on for harassment and you can call my lawyer if you want." He was told: "you're not under arrest however you do have to remain with us for a reasonable time for this paperwork to be served". He said his lawyer wanted to arrange a time to collect the papers with him, and: "I said the police are harassing me." About half an hour later officers from Southport brought the papers [39A].
25. On 22 March 2025 police seized the dog from the complainant. He showed them his receipts and civil partnership papers and said "It's not stolen. I own the dog" and "It's a civil, it's a civil thing". An officer answered "It doesn't matter" and "It would have been civil if you didn't get charged for stealing it". An officer said "regardless of what paperwork he has, we've got to seize the dog and then he's got to go through the courts to get the dog back" [40].
25A. In that exchange the officer from Southport began: "Have you got the dog in here?" and "we will have to take that dog today". The complainant asked "Why did you guys not charge her?". An officer answered: "All I've been told is that you've stolen the dog." Told the dog would be seized, the complainant said "Let me call my solicitor", and asked again. The officer answered: "I'm just letting you know that the vehicle is detained" and "You're detained" [40A].
26. The property receipt police gave him names no other owner and states that where more than one person claims ownership, "an application will be made under the PPRA for an order concerning disposal ... you will be advised if this is to occur" [40]. No application was made, and he was not told of one. Police released the dog to his former partner [45]. When he applied to the Magistrates Court in August 2025, police said the dog had already been given to her [46].

### His reports and complaints, April 2025

27. On 4 April 2025 Policelink told him that information about his report "does not match our records", and declined his online report of the taking of the dog because "this matter has already been reported to Police with advice provided" [42].
28. On 17 April 2025 he complained in writing about the seizure, and about police visiting a friend, his mother's home late at night and his sister. Policelink replied that his documents had been "forwarded to the appropriate department". He has not been told of any outcome [44].

## D. Why this is discrimination

### Direct discrimination because of sex (s 10)

32. Police responding to a person's complaint of offending, and police exercising powers under the Police Powers and Responsibilities Act 2000 and the Domestic and Family Violence Protection Act 2012, perform functions and exercise powers under State law (s 101; Woodforth v State of Queensland [2017] QCA 100 at [24]). In dealing with his complaint in 2026, police were also performing a function under State law: the Commissioner of Police "must expeditiously assess complaints" and must deal with a complaint the Crime and Corruption Commission refers (Crime and Corruption Act 2001 s 42(1) and (5)).
33. The comparison is with how police treated, or would have treated, a woman in circumstances that are the same or not materially different, including the complaint or conduct police were responding to (s 10(1); Woodforth at [29] and [54]). Annexure B sets out each comparison.
34. The complainant was treated less favourably than his former partner was treated, and than a woman in his position would have been treated, in each of these ways:
    - (a) as a complainant of an assault, an entry into his home and the taking of property, he was told to "let it go" and no statement was taken, while his former partner's complaint about money was taken in a signed statement the next day (paragraphs 7–10);
    - (b) the dispute over the dog was "civil" while she held the dog and "stolen property" while he held it; he was sent to the courts both times; his documents did not matter; and the court route the law provides was not used although police's own receipt said it would be (paragraphs 8–9, 25–26);
    - (c) on the night after his arrest police went to his mother's home and then to his friend's home to recover the dog for her, told his friend that police were "starting to look at" other people for offences, and described their own contact as "harassing him"; they then telephoned his family repeatedly; and on 22 March 2025 they took the dog at a roadside stop made to serve court papers, after he had told police they were harassing him and could call his lawyer, and after he asked to call his solicitor first. They made no visit and no call for him (paragraphs 21 to 21B, 23, 24A to 25A);
    - (d) he, a named tenant whom officers had said was allowed to be there, was removed, and police told the building manager he was not the leaseholder, while a woman who was on neither the lease nor the order was offered support and had her access reinstated (paragraphs 15–20);
    - (e) his report was declined because it had "already been reported to Police with advice provided", when the only advice he had been given was that the dog was a civil matter, the opposite of how police then treated her report (paragraph 27).
35. The complainant's sex was a substantial reason for that treatment (s 10(4)). Discrimination on the basis of sex includes discrimination on the basis of a characteristic often imputed to men (s 8(b)): here, that in a domestic dispute the man is the aggressor and the woman the person to protect. The words officers used show that assumption at work: "a domestic violence victim survivor who's been put through a lot by her partner"; "essentially terrorized a female person"; "an exercise in control over your partner" (paragraphs 19, 21; [32]). The reason given to the building manager, "cory's not the leaseholder", was contrary to what he had just told police, and within the hour the interviewing officer called her "the other leaseholder" (paragraphs 19–20). A stated reason shown to be untrue leaves the real reason to be found. The pattern runs in one direction across five episodes and four months.
36. Each time police had to decide whose account to act on, they acted on hers. On 1 December they tested his account and not hers: they recorded and set aside his explanation of the bruise, did not check her account of having stayed elsewhere for several nights against the building's CCTV, and took no statement about his report of being assaulted later that night. When the dog was hers to recover, an officer said "if there are cameras then we can get access to them" (paragraph 21), and the officer who seized it said "All I've been told is that you've stolen the dog" (paragraph 25A). The complainant asks the Commission to obtain police's records of how his report that night was dealt with. Police's own procedures required more for him. When a person attends a police station to report an offence, "members are to ensure a report is taken at that time", and an occurrence "is to be created" for any suspected indictable offence. A conclusion that a matter is civil is to be recorded with "the evidence relied on to conclude that it is deemed to be civil rather than criminal" and approved by the officer in charge. When officers help a person collect belongings in a domestic violence matter, the person may take items "which are not of disputed ownership"; where ownership is disputed, "officers should advise persons not to remove the property", and should warn that removing jointly owned property without the other owner's consent "may constitute an offence" (Operational Procedures Manual, Issue 102, in force 1 October to 24 December 2024, ss 1.11, 1.11.2, 1.11.11 and 9.7). The dog was disputed and stayed with her. Three months later police treated his keeping of the same dog as stealing.
37. Motive is irrelevant, and police need not have considered the treatment less favourable (s 10(2) and (3)).

### Direct discrimination because of impairment (s 10)

38. In the grounds for the notice of 1 December 2024, police recorded and relied on his former partner's statement that his mental illness "can cause the resp to act crazy and go into rages without warning" [5]. Volatility, and the unreliability of what a person says, are characteristics often imputed to people with a mental illness. Those characteristics were a substantial reason for police crediting her account, setting aside his explanation, and declining to take his report of being assaulted that night, and they shaped how police treated him afterwards (s 8(b), s 10(4)). This applies in addition to, and in the alternative to, his sex. Police's protective assessment framework, which officers "are to conduct ... at all incidents or reports of DFV", lists as a risk factor whether there is "history of mental health issues for respondent" or "evidence of a diagnosed or undiagnosed disorder which might increase risk of DV to the aggrieved" (Operational Procedures Manual, Issue 102, s 9.4.2 and Appendix 9.1). The complainant asks the Commission to obtain the assessment completed on 1 December 2024 and to see what was recorded against him under that factor.

### Indirect discrimination because of sex (s 11), in the alternative

39. Police imposed a term (which includes a practice, whether or not written: s 11(4)) that, in a property dispute between people in a domestic relationship, property held by the person police treat as the aggrieved is a civil matter, while property held by the person police treat as the respondent may be seized as stolen, so that the respondent must go to court to recover it whatever documents of ownership he holds. An officer described police's approach on 24 February 2025: police do not "typically get involved" in contested property, but do "when we think it's stolen property" (paragraph 21B). In this dispute police treated the dog as contested when he reported it taken, and as stolen when she did.
40. The complainant could not comply: he was treated as the respondent, and the dog was released before he could obtain a court order. A higher proportion of women than men comply or are able to comply, because men are the large majority of the people police and the courts treat as respondents. Of the domestic violence orders made in Queensland from 1 July to 31 October 2026 for which the sex of both parties was recorded, 84.7% named a male respondent (11,618 of 13,720: Queensland Courts, domestic and family violence statistics, Table 10). From 2008–09 to 2017–18, 76.8% of applications between intimate partners named a male respondent (Queensland Government Statistician's Office, Applications for domestic violence orders in Queensland, 2008–09 to 2017–18 (2021), Figure 6). Of the people sentenced for contravening a domestic violence order from 2012–13 to 2023–24, 84.0% were male (Queensland Sentencing Advisory Council, Sentencing Spotlight on contravention of a domestic violence order (May 2025)).
41. The term was not reasonable (s 11(2)). Ownership was disputed and police were told so with documents; the law gave police a way to have a magistrate decide (Police Powers and Responsibilities Act 2000 s 694); police's own receipt said that route would be used. The respondent must prove the term was reasonable (former s 205, applied by s 285AA). Police's own procedure made the court route mandatory. Under an "ORDER" in force on 22 March 2025, "A reporting officer who becomes aware of a dispute about the ownership of property is to" apply to a magistrates court for an order under s 694, and "When disposing of property, members are to satisfy themselves that any person the property is disposed of to, is the person with a lawful claim to that property" (Operational Procedures Manual, Issue 104.1, s 4.7.1). Orders "are not to be departed from, unless exceptional circumstances exist" (Operational Procedures Manual, "Use of Manual"). The officers who seized the dog were told on the spot that he owned it and were shown his receipts (paragraph 25).

### Indirect discrimination because of impairment (s 11)

42. The complainant has a mental illness: major depressive disorder, diagnosed on 24 October 2024 (D20) and recorded in a specialist's report of 13 February 2025 (D21). On 18 June 2026 police imposed two terms: that particulars of his complaint be provided by close of business the next day; and, by their standard acknowledgment, that any further material be provided within three business days, failing which it might not be considered (paragraph 29). Because of his impairment he could not comply with either, and did not (paragraph 29A). A higher proportion of people without his impairment are able to comply with time limits of that length.
42A. The terms were not reasonable (s 11(2)). Police records had noted his illness since 1 December 2024 [5]. Police's own procedures treat mental illness as a circumstance that "should be considered as creating a vulnerability until the contrary becomes apparent", and direct an officer who wishes to communicate with a person, "including ... taking a complaint", to "first establish whether the person is vulnerable" (Operational Procedures Manual, Issue 111, ss 6.3.1 and 6.3.2). The particulars asked for were matters recorded in police's own systems. The deadline for the particulars was shorter than the three business days police's own acknowledgment allowed. The consequence of not complying was that his material might not be considered, and the outcome of 10 September 2026 does not refer to the report he sent on 14 July 2026 or to his condition [51], [52]. The alternative was more time, which cost nothing: police took nearly three months to decide. A report from his treating doctor will be provided. The respondent must prove the terms were reasonable (former s 205, applied by s 285AA).

## E. No exemption applies

43. Section 106 exempts only acts necessary to comply with, or specifically authorised by, a court order or "an existing provision of another Act", meaning a provision in existence when s 106 commenced on 30 June 1992 (s 106(1) and (2)). The Police Powers and Responsibilities Act 2000 and the Domestic and Family Violence Protection Act 2012 are later Acts. The respondent has conceded before the tribunal that the Police Powers and Responsibilities Act 2000 is not an existing provision (Philipp v State of Queensland (QPS) [2025] QCAT 505 at [185]). No court order required any of the treatment complained of: the orders of 3 December 2024 and 21 March 2025 did not exclude the complainant from his home and said nothing about the dog.
44. Section 104 (welfare measures) protects acts designed to benefit a group. It does not protect declining to take an assault complaint, seizing one person's property for another, or misinforming a building manager about a tenancy.
44A. No other exemption applies. The area in s 101 has no exemption for unjustifiable hardship; that exemption is confined to the goods and services area (s 51). The general exemptions in ss 107 to 113 (public health, workplace health and safety, religious bodies, charities, sport, legal incapacity and the tribunal) have nothing to do with police complaint handling or property disputes.
45. The respondent bears the burden of proving any exemption (former s 206).

## F. Human Rights Act 2019

46. The Queensland Police Service is a public entity. In deciding complaint COM-26-01249 it had to give proper consideration to the human rights relevant to the decision, which includes identifying them, and to act compatibly with them (s 58(1)(a) and (b), s 58(5)). The relevant rights were the complainant's rights in the process and the rights his complaint was about: equality before the law and equal protection against discrimination (s 15(2)–(4)); property (s 24(2)); privacy, family and home (s 25(a)); and liberty (s 29(2)). Police's own acknowledgment of 18 June 2026 said that "consideration will be given to the impact of police actions on human rights". The outcome of 10 September 2026 says only that a committee "determined that police actions did not unduly limit your Human Rights". It identifies none of these rights and does not refer to his condition (paragraph 30). Police's own manual says the same: proper consideration "includes ... identifying any human rights that may be affected by an action or decision"; the requirement extends to "administrative matters"; and "Upon subsequent review, members are to be able to explain their considerations and justify their actions and decisions" (Operational Procedures Manual, Issue 112.2, s 1.2.3).
47. The complainant complained to the Service about that decision on 29 October 2026 (s 65(1)). Forty-five business days from that complaint end on 5 January 2027. If this complaint is lodged before then, he asks the Commissioner to accept this part because of exceptional circumstances (s 65(2)), or to add it after that date.
47A. He does not ask the Commissioner to deal again with the rights to property and to privacy and reputation for the 2025 events. Those were the subject of EDR19098 (Part I). If this complaint is referred to the tribunal, he will rely on s 58 in support of the same relief for those events (s 59).
