# Memorandum of advice

**Cory Lea Shepherd — complaint against the State of Queensland (Queensland Police Service) under the Anti-Discrimination Act 1991 (Qld) and the Human Rights Act 2019 (Qld)**

18 November 2026. Privileged and confidential. Prepared for the complainant and his lawyers. Every judgment cited was read on the official Queensland judgments site or the NSW caselaw site; High Court authority is cited only as quoted in a Queensland judgment. Statutes were read on the official legislation sites, in the version in force at the relevant time.

## 1. Summary of advice

**The case.** Between 1 December 2024 and 22 March 2025 the Queensland Police Service decided the same dispute, over the same dog, between the same two people, twice, and reached opposite answers. When the man reported that the dog had been taken from his home, with an assault, the dispute was civil and he was told to let it go. When the woman reported that he had taken the dog, the dispute was criminal, he was charged, and the dog was seized from him "regardless of what paperwork he has" and given to her without the court process police's own receipt promised. Between those dates police removed him from a home he leased, five minutes after the building manager confirmed his tenancy to them, and told the building manager he was not the leaseholder. In 2026 police closed his complaint about all of this after giving a man recorded as mentally ill one business day to particularise it.

**The question the tribunal will ask** is not whether he was treated worse than she was. It is whether police would have treated a woman in his position better, and whether his sex was a substantial reason they did not (s 10). That is the ground on which every reported direct discrimination claim against Queensland police found for this advice has failed.

**Advice in brief.**

1. **Lodge one complaint** with the Queensland Human Rights Commission under both Acts, against the State only. Plead sex and impairment; direct discrimination as the narrative; indirect discrimination in the alternative; the human rights duties alongside.
2. **Lead with the strongest claim**: the 2026 complaint handling, as indirect impairment discrimination and a failure of proper consideration under the Human Rights Act. It is in time, the term is in writing, and police must prove it was reasonable. The only claims found to have succeeded against police in this jurisdiction are of this kind (Hunter v State of Queensland [2015] QCAT 179; Harding v State of Queensland (QPS) [2025] QCAT 415).
3. **The December records are in hand, and they do not carry the sex claim.** Police issued the notice of 1 December 2024 on her detailed account, a visible bruise and what they recorded as his partial admissions by phone. His own messages, the building's CCTV and a clinic record go a long way to show her account of fleeing on 27 November was untrue, but police did not have them. A tribunal will treat police's reasons as reasons that are not about sex (Watego v State of Queensland [2022] QCAT 341 at [33], [245]). What remains is the contrast in how police tested the two accounts that night, and the stronger point that police adopted her claim that his mental illness makes him "act crazy and go into rages" (impairment, s 8(b)).
4. **Police cannot shelter behind s 106.** It covers only acts under a court order or under a provision of an Act that existed when s 106 commenced on 30 June 1992. Police have conceded that the Police Powers and Responsibilities Act 2000 is not such a provision (Philipp v State of Queensland (QPS) [2025] QCAT 505 at [185]).
5. **The dog is best pursued outside this complaint.** The tribunal cannot decide ownership. A property claim between former partners in the federal family court can, but must be made within two years of separation, which may be days or weeks away.
6. **Time is the main procedural risk, and it is decided twice**: by the Commissioner (s 138) and again by the tribunal (s 175(2); Smythe v State of Queensland [2024] QCAT 33 at [14]). The good cause material must be complete at lodgement.
7. **Keep the events inside the unit out of the complaint** while the charges are pending. The respondent is the prosecutor. Schedule X holds that account.
8. **Lodge in early December 2026**, after the information requests are in and the doctor's report is in hand, and add the human rights complaint about the 2026 decision from 6 January 2027.
9. **Plead victimisation in the alternative for the 2026 conduct, if one fact is confirmed.** The Commission's own letters show his earlier complaint (EDR19098) was dealt with under the Human Rights Act only, conciliated on 14 May 2026 and reported unresolved on 18 June 2026, the day police set the one-day deadline. His written submission of 3 May 2026 in that complaint said police had discriminated against him because of psychiatric disability. If that submission reached the Commission or police before 18 June 2026, the 2026 conduct can be put, in the alternative, as victimisation (ss 129–130; section 5.5A).

**Prospects** (section 11). On the facts as instructed, if proved:

| Claim | Assessment |
|---|---|
| 2026 complaint handling (impairment, indirect) and the human rights failure | Good |
| December 2024 and the dog (sex, direct) | Weak. The December records give police a reason that is not about sex |
| December 2024: her "rages" characterisation of his illness adopted by police (impairment, direct) | Arguable |
| The "civil for her, criminal for him" practice (sex, indirect) | Arguable; untested |
| The 2026 conduct as victimisation, in the alternative | Weak until it is shown the officers knew of his discrimination allegation |

**Overall.** A moderate complaint. It has a sound, in-time core on impairment and human rights with a realistic prospect of a conciliated outcome. The sex discrimination narrative is well documented but weak in law, because police's December 2024 records give them a reason that is not about sex. Run it narrowly, and lead with impairment.

## 3. The legal framework

### 3.1 Anti-Discrimination Act 1991

- **Attributes.** Sex and impairment (s 7(a), (h)), including characteristics people with the attribute generally have or are often imputed to have (s 8).
- **Direct discrimination** (s 10): less favourable treatment than a person without the attribute "in circumstances that are the same or not materially different"; motive irrelevant; the attribute need only be "a substantial reason" (s 10(4)). The substituted definitions in s 7B of the Respect at Work and Other Matters Amendment Act 2024 have not been proclaimed (Act's legislative history, current reprint). The comparator test applies.
- **Indirect discrimination** (s 11): a term the complainant does not or cannot comply with, with which a higher proportion of people without the attribute comply or can comply, and which is not reasonable. A "term" includes a "condition, requirement or practice, whether or not written" (s 11(4)). The person imposing it need not be aware of the discrimination (s 11(3)). It must be "formulated with some precision" (Leah v A.N. Burnett Investments [2025] QCAT 123 at [126]).
- **Area.** A person performing functions or exercising powers under State law must not discriminate (s 101). Police dealing with a complaint are "within at least s 101" (Woodforth v State of Queensland [2017] QCA 100 at [24]). The goods and services definition "does not extend to the exercise of powers by police" (Cook v State of Queensland (QPS) [2018] QCAT 216 at [33]), although "the initial investigation of complaints of violence and the protection of the victims of violence provide services to the victims", while decisions "whether or not to prosecute or arrest" generally do not (Commissioner of Police v Mohamed [2009] NSWCA 432 at [87], Handley AJA; see also [36]), and the Service was treated as providing services in Nguyen v State of Queensland (QPS) [2026] QCAT 72 at [9]. Plead s 101, with s 46 in the alternative for receiving and investigating his reports of violence.
- **Liability.** The State answers for its officers (s 133).
- **Burden.** The reverse burden in new s 204 does not apply to contraventions before s 7B commences (s 285AA). Former s 204 applies: "It is for the complainant to prove, on the balance of probabilities, that the respondent contravened the Act." The respondent proves reasonableness of an indirect-discrimination term (former s 205) and any exemption (former s 206). Leah at [24] confirms the reverse onus does not reach earlier conduct.
- **Exemptions.** Welfare measures (s 104); equal opportunity measures (s 105); acts necessary to comply with, or specifically authorised by, a court order or "an existing provision of another Act" (s 106).
- **Process.** Complaint requirements (s 136); rejection of frivolous or misconceived complaints (s 139); a power to stay where there are concurrent court proceedings, with time not running during a stay (s 140); acceptance within 28 days (s 141); a rejected or unaccepted complaint lapses and cannot be made again (s 142); referral to the tribunal after conciliation fails (ss 164A, 166) or after six months (s 167).
- **Time.** One year, or later for good cause (s 138); deferral of the out-of-time part until conciliation (s 141A); a second, independent discretion in the tribunal (s 175(2)).
- **Evidence and orders.** The tribunal may adopt findings of other courts (s 208(1)(b), (c)) and may order compensation (s 209).

### 3.2 Human Rights Act 2019

- The Service is a public entity. It must not act incompatibly with human rights, and must give proper consideration to relevant rights, including identifying them and considering compatibility (s 58(1), (5)). "Proper consideration" is not codified by s 58(5); it is approached "in a common sense and practical manner", asking whether the decision-maker "seriously turned his or her mind to the possible impact of the decision" (Owen-D'Arcy v Chief Executive, Queensland Corrective Services [2021] QSC 273 at [134], [136]–[137]). It is "a standard of consideration higher than that generally applicable at common law" (Mizner v State of Queensland [2025] QCA 169 at [268]). There is "no place for deference" (Owen-D'Arcy at [140]).
- Rights relevant to the decision of 10 September 2026: equality (s 15(2)–(4)); property (s 24(2)); privacy, family and home (s 25(a)); arbitrary arrest (s 29(2)). Fair hearing (s 31) is no longer pleaded: it is about a charge or a civil proceeding before a court or tribunal, not a police complaint process. Property and privacy for the 2025 events were the subject of EDR19098 and are not run again (section 7). Limits must be justified (s 13); once a prima facie limit is shown, the Service must justify it (DM v State of Queensland [2025] QCAT 517 at [65]).
- A complaint to the Commissioner requires a complaint to the entity first and 45 business days (s 65(1)), unless exceptional circumstances (s 65(2)). The Commissioner may refuse a complaint over a year old, or one appropriately dealt with elsewhere (s 70(1)(b), (d)). No damages (s 59(3)). A tribunal may decline a declaration as "tokenistic" (Philipp at [202]).

### 3.3 The domestic violence framework

The Domestic and Family Violence Protection Act 2012, as in force from December 2024 to March 2025, is administered on the paramount principle of the safety of people who fear or experience domestic violence (s 4(1)). Responses should take account of characteristics making a person particularly vulnerable; the first example is "women" (s 4(2)(d)). Where allegations conflict, the person most in need of protection should be identified (s 4(2)(e)) on a behaviour-based test (s 22A(1)). A civil response operates "in conjunction with, not instead of, the criminal law" (s 4(2)(f)). Police must investigate where domestic violence is reasonably suspected (s 100). This is police's answer, and parts of it are the complainant's case (section 6.3).

## 4. How the tribunal will reason

### 4.1 The comparator

- The comparator's circumstances are hypothetical and include what police were responding to: in Woodforth, "the context of a complaint by a person to police of criminal offending against her" ([29]). The comparison is with treatment "in response to occurrences of the same kind" ([54]).
- A characteristic of the attribute cannot also be a circumstance of the comparison, or s 8 would be ineffective (Woodforth at [53]).
- The circumstances are "all of the objective features which surround the actual or intended treatment", including the law the officer was applying (Lyons v State of Queensland [2015] QCA 159 at [37], [39]), and the statutory, policy and operational framework (Tafao v State of Queensland [2018] QCAT 409 at [66]).
- In the closest analogue found, a woman arrested after the other person's account was given to police failed because her reported conduct "was a 'material circumstance' which did not apply to the man" (Watego at [245]).

**Application.** The tribunal will picture a woman named in a protection notice, reported by a man in the same way, holding the same papers. Her designation and his report will count as circumstances. The complainant's answer is that the designation itself is under challenge: if it rested on the characteristic often imputed to men in domestic disputes (s 8(b)), Woodforth at [53] keeps that characteristic out of the comparison. No decision found accepts that argument, and an imputed-characteristic claim needs evidence that the characteristic is imputed and was applied (Wright v Callvm [2018] QIRC 7 at [44], [46]–[49], where the tribunal did not accept that being a victim of domestic violence is a characteristic women generally have).

### 4.2 The inference

- Discrimination "is rarely overt and usually covert" (Watego at [25]); a conclusion of discrimination will often arise only by inference (Toodayan v Anti-Discrimination Commission Queensland [2018] QCA 349 at [43]).
- An inference may be drawn from a combination of facts "none of which viewed alone would support that inference", but not "where more probable and innocent explanations are available" (Jenkins v State of Queensland [2018] QCAT 154 at [68]). Briginshaw does not alter the standard, which remains the balance of probabilities ([59]).
- Possibility is not enough: "It is only a possibility that the decisions were made on the basis of race ... since this would be conjecture only, it is not enough" (Watego at [243]). That conduct was unreasonable is not enough on its own ([36]).
- What moves an inference: an explanation "found to be dishonest" allows the inference "with greater certainty" ([32]); unlawful conduct invites an explanation, failing which the inference may be drawn ([98]).
- An attribute that merely gives rise to the circumstances is not the basis of the treatment (Lyons QCA at [32]).

**Application.** The complainant's strongest inference material is where police's own explanation is contradicted by police's own record: "cory's not the leaseholder" against the building manager's confirmation minutes earlier and the interviewing officer's "the other leaseholder" within the hour; "It doesn't matter" in answer to his papers; a receipt promising a court process that was not used. Each must be shown to be more than a mistake (Watego at [32]–[33]).

### 4.3 What the decided police cases show

In the Queensland decisions found, every direct discrimination claim against police decided on the merits failed: Watego (race; arrest); Chester v Barnaby (No 2) [2014] QCAT 695 (failure to charge, arrest); Cook (closure of investigation); Hattabi v State of Queensland [2022] QCAT 8; Isles v State of Queensland [2021] QCAT 135; Given v State of Queensland (QPS) [2019] QCAT 16; Nguyen; Donnelly v State of Queensland [2026] QCAT 323; and DM, the closest on its facts, where a man named as respondent in a domestic violence matter failed because there was "no reliable evidence" police knew of his impairment and their footage contradicted him ([23], [42]). Breaches of police powers or procedures are "only relevant ... if those alleged breaches occurred because of the attribute" (Chester at [37]). One live case is close to this one: in Tedesco v State of Queensland [2025] QCAT 109, a man said police refused to take his assault statements while investigating a counter-complaint, and relied on mental-health entries in police records; police's strike-out application was refused because "his complaints of assault were not properly investigated" was arguable ([8], [15]). Its merits are undecided.

The two successes were indirect impairment claims: a wheelchair user made to use a fixed fingerprint scanner, $8,000 (Hunter, at [61]); a detainee kept from prescribed medication, $10,000 (Harding).

## 5. The claims

### 5.1 The night of 1 to 2 December 2024

**Treatment.** No statement taken from a man reporting an assault, a home entry and the taking of his property; told to "let it go"; the contested dog left with the woman police had escorted to collect "uncontested" items; a statement taken from her the next day about money.

**Police's case.** He had been named respondent at 8:30 pm on her report; the entry was her friends helping her collect her belongings; the dog was civil; he did not press charges (his message of 9 December 2024: "I havnt even pressed charges").

**What police recorded as their basis** (Statement — Police Protection Notice, QP 0899A, 1 December 2024, occurrence QP2402065947). At about 7:30 pm Robina police attended "an address where the aggrieved had sought refuge". She told them that on the evening of 27 November he had flown into a rage, shoved her over a bedside table, held her by the neck and smashed her phone, and that she had fled and "sought refuge at another address for a few nights". She showed a bruise on her chest and said a friend had photographed it. She described a history of violence, and said she was "aware that the resp has a mental illness and requires medication that he does not take regularly, and this can cause the resp to act crazy and go into rages without warning". Police spoke to him by telephone. They recorded that he denied her main allegations; said he had "ushered/pushed" her out of the unit during arguments but did not consider that an assault; said her phone screen broke when his own phone, thrown at the bed, hit the bedside table; and "suggested [the bruise] may be from a beaty treatment procedure". Police also reviewed texts, including one about surrendering some of the pets to a shelter. Their conclusion: "Due to the nature of the reported domestic violence incidents, the supporting evidence supplied by the agg and the partial admissions made by the resp, Police formed the opinion that domestic violence had occurred".

**What his own records show** (his exhibit "Police Protection Notice with Refuge-Claim Contradiction and 1 December 2024 Report").

- On the evening of 27 November the messages are ordinary: he offers to make her dinner; at 8:06 pm she is "home", showering, and needs to be somewhere at 9:00 pm; he drives her at 8:45 pm.
- At 3:01 am on 28 November she asks him to collect her, and he does.
- At about 4:00 am on 30 November he collects her from the Hilton.
- On 30 November she tells him "I made ravioli for later for you", they agree to dinner out, she says she has walked Romeo, and a restaurant booking for two is confirmed for 7:30 pm.
- The building's CCTV shows her at the building on 27, 28 and 30 November and 1 to 2 December.
- A cosmetic clinic's aftercare and her message "They put threads all over my neck and shoulders" place a cosmetic treatment of her neck and shoulders shortly before 1 December. That matches the explanation he gave police that night.

**What that means in law.**

- His records go a long way to show her account of fleeing on 27 November was untrue. They do not show that police's reasons were untrue. Police acted on a detailed account, a visible bruise and what they recorded as his partial admissions, and they did not have his messages, the CCTV or the clinic record. A tribunal will ask whether police would have done the same for a man who reported that his female partner shoved him over a table and held his neck, and showed a bruise. It will very likely answer yes. That is a more probable explanation than sex, and it leaves no room for the inference (Watego at [33]). **The December designation cannot carry the sex claim.**
- What the records do give him is a contrast in how police tested the two accounts that night. Her account of a refuge was accepted without checking the building's CCTV, minutes away. His explanation of the bruise was recorded and set aside. His own report later that night — an entry into his home, a push, an injured ankle, the dog taken — drew no statement and, on his instructions, no record at all. That contrast is the inference the sex claim must now rest on. Tedesco is the model, and it is an arguable claim, not a strong one.
- **Impairment is the stronger angle on this night.** Police recorded, and adopted into the grounds for the notice, her statement that his mental illness makes him "act crazy and go into rages without warning". Volatility is a characteristic often imputed to people with mental illness (s 8(b)). Unlike DM, police knew of his condition: they wrote it down. If that characterisation was a substantial reason for crediting her account and discounting his — including his report of being assaulted later that night — it is direct discrimination on the basis of impairment (s 10(4)).
- **Caution.** Putting the designation in issue invites police to put their whole statement before the tribunal, including texts that should not be in issue. Keep the complaint's focus on how police treated his own report and on the impairment characterisation, not on re-trying the events of 27 November.

**Weak joint.** The 9 December message ("I havnt even pressed charges"). On his account, he had not pressed charges because police would not take his complaint. That must be his evidence.

### 5.2 The dog

**Treatment.** Civil while she held the dog; "stolen property" while he held it; home visits and calls to recover it for her, none for him; his papers declared irrelevant; released to her without the s 694 process police's receipt promised.

**The logical bind.** Either ownership of the dog was a civil matter between former partners, in which case seizing it from one and handing it to the other without a magistrate was police taking a side in a civil dispute; or taking the dog from the other partner was a crime, in which case his December report of the taking, with documents, required investigation. Police treated it as civil when she held it and criminal when he did.

**Police's case.** By March she was the protected person; he had signed the change-of-owner form; he had been charged; the dog was returned to its registered owner (Police Powers and Responsibilities Act 2000 s 691).

**Reply.** The officer said the paperwork did not matter. Police's own December record had the animals split between the two names; their March statement called Romeo "the aggrieved's dog" before any court had decided anything. The Act gave a neutral route, an application to a magistrate where two or more claim seized property (s 694), which police have used for a dog seized from a man and claimed by his former partner (Kaus v Coaker & Anor [2014] QMC 24), and on which each claimant carries an onus to advance evidence of ownership (Blyton v Bates [2021] QDC 180 at [19]). Police's receipt told him that route would be used. The charge explains the seizure; it does not explain giving the evidence to the complainant before the charge was decided.

**The hard points.** A signed microchip transfer is strong evidence that ownership passed (Chivers v Gold Coast City Council [2010] QSC 98 at [25], [29]). And a failure to use s 694 is not discrimination unless it happened because of sex (Chester at [37]). The sex claim on the dog therefore rises or falls with the December records.

**Indirect discrimination, in the alternative.** The practice — civil for the aggrieved, criminal for the respondent, court for the respondent whatever his documents — is a "term". A uniform rule is "potentially indirect discrimination, not direct discrimination" (Tafao v State of Queensland [2020] QCATA 76 at [64]). He could not comply. If men are the large majority of respondents, a higher proportion of women can comply. Police must prove the practice reasonable (former s 205); the test is "less demanding than one of necessity, but more demanding than a test of convenience" (Ali v State of Queensland [2023] QCAT 109 at [35]), and a term is unreasonable where the alternative has "little or no resource implications" (Tafao QCATA at [170]). The alternative here was an application police said they would make. This claim avoids the causation problem, but it is untested, must be framed precisely (Leah at [126]), and needs the statistics.

**Human rights.** Releasing disputed property to one claimant without the process police said would be used engages s 24(2).

### 5.3 The home, 24 February 2025

**Treatment.** Arrest five minutes after the building manager confirmed the tenancy; a welfare inquiry of her friend and none of him; the building manager told he was "not the leaseholder" and would "not be going back"; access reinstated for her "and her friend".

**The logical bind.** Either his notice of intention to leave ended the tenancy, in which case it ended for both co-tenants and she was no more a leaseholder than he was until her own lease began on 10 April 2025; or it did not, and he was a leaseholder. Police told the building manager he was not, and within the hour the interviewing officer called her "the other leaseholder".

**Analysis.** The arrest explains the removal that night. An arrest on reasonable suspicion is lawful even if the charge later fails (Wood v State of Queensland [2025] QSC 95 at [62]), and her friend's account to police will be a material circumstance (Watego at [245]). The arrest does not explain the 5:23 am call, which went beyond the arrest and affected his tenancy, or the one-sided welfare inquiry. Those are evidence within the pattern. As a separate claim, the home is weak.

**The six hours.** Her friend first called police at about 7:50 pm. Police came at about 1:55 am, after her second call when he came back (court brief). If the first job was recorded as an urgent domestic violence matter, the gap shows that police did not treat what happened at 7:50 pm as an emergency, and that they had six hours in which to check the lease and speak to his witness. Get the job log and dispatch record (Request A2) for the priority code and times. Its main use is in the criminal case, the costs application and against police's later description of the night. As discrimination it helps only if it shows police responded differently to his call than to hers. What he said on his 7:51 pm call to the police line, and what police did with it, decides that.

**His witness.** Police spoke to his friend at the entrance (the court brief records that he said the complainant was inside and that he had driven him there), and on the complainant's account the friend handed officers the lease, the order and medical records. Police did not take his account of the evening or look at his video before the arrest, and did not contact him for fifteen months. That night they took her friend's account and her photographs. This is a failure to investigate for the costs application (Oates; Wells) and part of the human rights complaint about an arbitrary arrest (s 29(2)). As discrimination it is evidence within the pattern, not a separate claim: the tribunal will say police acted on the account of the person who called them (Watego at [245]), and a departure from procedure matters only if it happened because of sex (Chester at [37]).

**Human rights.** Section 25(a) (home) and s 29(2) (arbitrary arrest).

### 5.4 His report declined (April 2025)

The decline gave as its reason that the matter had "already been reported to Police with advice provided". The only advice he had been given was that the dog was civil. As a separate claim this is weak. Its value is in the pattern and in good cause.

### 5.5 The complaint handling (June to September 2026)

**The term**, framed precisely: that he provide written particulars of his complaint (the court matter, the date, the charge and the officers' names) by close of business on 19 June 2026, for the complaint to be assessed on them.

**Elements.**

- *He could not comply because of his impairment.* "Serious disadvantage" in complying is enough (Harding at [90]). His treating doctor's report must say so. A mental health condition and incapacity are different things (Ryle v State of Queensland [2021] QIRC 307 at [91]); the report must address the deadline, not just the diagnosis.
- *A higher proportion of people without his impairment could comply.* Hunter accepted that the complainant "was required to adapt to a process which was straightforward and quick for persons without a disability" ([61]). The same reasoning fits a one-day deadline.
- *Not reasonable.* Police must prove reasonableness (former s 205; Ali at [53]). They then took nearly three months to decide. Maintaining a term after officers know it causes a problem is unreasonable (Ali at [41]); police knew of his condition from their own records (December 2024) and from the Case Study of 14 July 2026, before the closure on 10 September 2026. Hunter also weighed that the requirement "was not in accord with the published policies" of the Service ([61]). Judge the reasonableness of the requirement itself (Philipp at [123]).
- *Area.* Whether s 101 covers police complaint handling has not been decided. Police exercising their Police Service Administration Act functions "must not have discriminated" (Cook at [36]); complaint handling is such a function.

**Human rights.** An outcome that identifies no right engaged does not show the decision-maker "seriously turned his or her mind" to the impact (Owen-D'Arcy at [137]); failing to identify a relevant right made the decision unlawful there ([265]–[266]).

**The condition.** If he answered within the deadline, the discrimination claim fails and only the human rights claim remains.

### 5.5A Victimisation for the 2026 conduct, in the alternative

**The law.** A person must not victimise another (s 129). Victimisation happens if the respondent does an act to the complainant's detriment because the complainant "in good faith, alleged ... that a person committed an act that would amount to a contravention of the Act", or because the respondent believes he did (s 130(1)(a)(ii), (b)). Three things must be shown: an act, detriment, and "a causal nexus" between the detriment and the matters in s 130. The complainant carries the burden (Bero v Wilmar Sugar Pty Ltd & Ors [2016] QCAT 371 at [150]–[151]).

**The facts.**

- On 15 April 2026 the Commission accepted complaint EDR19098 against the State (police) as alleging "a possible unreasonable limitation of the right to property and privacy and reputation" under the Human Rights Act. A conciliation conference was held on 14 May 2026. On 18 June 2026 the Commission reported it unresolved (Commission's letters of 15 April and 18 June 2026).
- His written submission of 3 May 2026 in that complaint has a part headed "protected attributes and the discriminatory pattern", with "Disability — psychiatric" and "The pattern of cumulative discrimination". Only extracts were available for this advice. The full document must be read, and it must be confirmed that it was sent, when, and to whom.
- On 18 June 2026 Ethical Standards registered COM-26-01249 and set the one-day deadline. On 10 September 2026 it finalised the complaint without contact.

**The bind.** Either a one-business-day deadline is police's practice for all complainants, or it was set for him. If it is the practice, it is a "term" and the indirect discrimination claim in 5.5 is stronger. If it was set for him, police must say why it was set on the day the Commission reported his earlier complaint unresolved, six weeks after his written allegation of discrimination. The complaint asks the question (paragraph 42C).

**The gaps.**

- *Knowledge.* The claim fails without proof that the officers who set the deadline and decided the complaint knew of his allegation (compare Flori at [14], on reprisal). Those who attend a conciliation for police are not necessarily those in Ethical Standards. The Commissioner can direct police to produce the records (s 156).
- *Confidentiality.* Nothing said or done in the course of a conciliation conference for a human rights complaint is admissible unless both sides agree (Human Rights Act s 86). The claim must rest on the written submission and the dates, not on the conference. Police may argue the submission is caught too. The answer is that it was a document lodged before the conference. That is untested.
- *Coincidence.* The Commission referral that produced COM-26-01249 dates from 28 April 2026. Police will say 18 June was simply when the file was registered.

**Advice.** Plead it in the alternative, in three short paragraphs (complaint, paragraphs 42A–42C), if he confirms the submission was sent before 18 June 2026. It is in time, it costs nothing to add, and it is the only claim that lets the Commissioner ask who knew what. Do not put it first.

### 5.6 Reprisal for the earlier complaints about Southport police

**The instruction.** Before December 2024 the complainant had complained about Southport police: a service delivery complaint in December 2023 (Policelink 8933558), handled by the officer in charge of the station complained about, and a complaint about police's response to a welfare concern for a family member who was a minor. He asks whether police's later treatment of him was reprisal for those complaints.

**Victimisation under the Anti-Discrimination Act does not fit.** Section 130 protects a person who alleged a contravention of that Act, or was involved in a proceeding under it. Complaints about police conduct that did not allege discrimination are not protected by s 130.

**The route that fits is the Public Interest Disclosure Act 2010.**

- A public officer, meaning an employee of a public sector entity (s 7(1)), may disclose information about conduct that could be "maladministration that adversely affects a person's interests in a substantial and specific way", or corrupt conduct (s 13(1)(a)). The Queensland Police Service is a proper authority for the conduct of its own officers (s 15(1)(a)(i)).
- Causing detriment to a person because he made a public interest disclosure is a reprisal (s 40(1)). It is enough that the disclosure was "a substantial ground", even if there was another (s 40(5)).
- A reprisal may be the subject of a complaint under the Anti-Discrimination Act, dealt with as if it were a contravention of that Act (s 44(1)–(2)), or sued for as a tort, with exemplary damages available (s 42). The State is vicariously liable unless it proves it took reasonable steps to prevent it (s 43).
- The two routes are exclusive: once a complaint about the reprisal is accepted under the Anti-Discrimination Act, he cannot later sue for it (s 44(3)(b)), and if he sues first, he cannot later complain (s 44(3)(a)).

**Three questions decide it.**

1. *Was the earlier complaint a public interest disclosure?* He must have been a public officer when he made it, and its subject must be maladministration adversely affecting a person's interests "in a substantial and specific way" (or corrupt conduct). A welfare complaint about a minor that police did not act on is the stronger candidate; a complaint about an officer's manner at the counter is weaker.
2. *Did the officers who acted against him in 2024 and 2025 know of it?* Without proof of knowledge the claim fails. A police whistleblower's reprisal complaint was dismissed because he had "not established that any of the [respondents] were aware" of his disclosure before the decision complained of (Flori v State of Queensland & Ors [2016] QCAT 80 at [14]).
3. *Was it a substantial ground?* A gap of a year is weak on its own. What would count: the same station or officer in charge; an officer involved in both; a flag on his police record noting him as a complainant; a remark linking the two.

**Advice: do not plead reprisal in this complaint now.**

- It competes with the sex claim. If police acted against him because he had complained, a woman who had complained would have been treated the same way, and that is a non-sex explanation (Watego at [33]).
- Lodging it under the Anti-Discrimination Act now would give up the tort claim (s 44(3)(b)), which has longer to run (Limitation of Actions Act 1974 s 10(1)(a): six years for a tort without personal injury) and can attract exemplary damages.
- The evidence of knowledge is in police's hands. Obtain the flags, the complaint files and the allocation records first (document 08, Requests A and E). Then decide between the routes.

**Cautions.** Whether he was a public officer when he made the earlier complaint is an employment fact: settle it with the lawyers on his work matter before anything about his employment goes into police papers. Keep the details of the welfare matter out of every document; refer to it only as a child-welfare complaint about a family member who was a minor. That information is hers as much as his.

**Further instruction: "possible reprisal" and a flag.** The complainant says the Crime and Corruption Commission said there was possible reprisal, that police put a flag on his record, and that the Commission would know what his police record says.

- *What was recorded.* The Commission's record of complaint CO-26-1318 (31 March 2026) lists among his allegations "reprising against you in March 2026". His email of 8 April 2026 particularised it as "reliance on occurrence QP2402065947 ... shortly after the service of statutory demands". The Commission referred the complaint, with his consent, to the Deputy Commissioner of Police, "who is independent from QPS ESC" (31 March 2026; confirmed 28 April 2026). Ethical Standards' outcome advice (10 September 2026) describes it as "potential reprisal action in response to your complaint activity". Each of these states what he alleged. None is a finding, and none should be quoted as the Commission's view.
- *The flag.* Nothing in his papers shows a flag on his police record. His earlier analyses treat one passage as showing a flag: the officer's camera of 22 March 2025 at 04:00 to 04:32 (machine transcript, to be checked against the footage), "our call sign's coming up under duress again ... the duress has never been removed from you". That is one officer speaking to another about their own radio call sign. He has no call sign. It must not be used as evidence of a flag (document 12, point 12).
- *What the Commission knows cannot be used.* Commission officers may record or disclose what they learn only for the Commission's purposes (Crime and Corruption Act 2001 s 213(2)–(3)). They cannot be compelled to produce it in a court unless the Commission is a party (s 213(4)). Information obtained or prepared for an investigation in the performance of the Commission's corruption functions is exempt from access (Right to Information Act 2009 sch 3 s 10(4)). The exception for finalised investigations (s 10(6)) has been applied to the officer investigated, not to the complainant (G31 and Queensland Police Service [2024] QICmr 20 at [18]). If there is a flag, it has to come from police: document 08, Request A, items 3 and 12.
- *Order matters.* A record that Ethical Standards obtains for a complaint can become exempt from access. CCTV of an applicant's own arrest was exempt because Ethical Standards had "obtained" it for her complaint (016UGU and Queensland Police Service (6 December 2013) at [18]–[23]). Ask police for the flag before asking the Commission to examine it (document 13, section 5.1).
- *Why a flag would matter.* A flag identifying him as a complainant against police, created before an officer acted, would answer the knowledge question on which Flori failed, because any officer who checked his name would have seen it. Its date would fix the order of complaint, flag and action. It may itself be a detriment, which includes "intimidation or harassment" and "damage to reputation" (Public Interest Disclosure Act 2010 sch 4). It engages his rights to privacy and reputation (Human Rights Act 2019 s 25). A flag of another kind proves nothing about reprisal. A mental-health warning taken from her statement of 1 December 2024 would go instead to the impairment claim (section 5.1).
- *The March 2026 conduct.* Causing detriment to a person because he "gave evidence to, or helped, the commission" is an offence (Crime and Corruption Act s 212). Whether making a complaint is "help" is untested, and the section gives no right to damages. It is for the Commission, and the letter of 20 November should say so in one sentence (document 12). For a civil remedy, the complaint the conduct followed must have been a public interest disclosure: made while he was a public officer (Public Interest Disclosure Act ss 7(1), 13(1)(a)(i)), or disclosing a reprisal for an earlier disclosure (s 12(1)(d)). A complaint under the Anti-Discrimination Act about that reprisal (s 44) must be made by March 2027 (s 138(1)). Section 40(1) also covers detriment caused "in the belief that" he had made a disclosure. In at least one complaint he wrote that he was employed by Queensland Health and "a protected PID discloser". Establish which complaint that was, who received it and when: if police had it before they acted, it goes to both knowledge and belief.
- *Victimisation under the Anti-Discrimination Act.* It does not fit the earlier complaints about Southport police, which did not allege discrimination. It may fit the 2026 conduct: his submission of 3 May 2026 in EDR19098 alleged discrimination because of psychiatric disability (section 5.5A). An earlier version of this advice said no complaint of his alleged discrimination. That was wrong.

The advice not to plead reprisal now stands. Revisit it when the flags item in Request A is answered, and in any event before February 2027.

## 6. Exemptions and defences

### 6.1 Section 106

The exemption applies to provisions in existence when s 106 commenced on 30 June 1992 (s 106(2); Malaxetxebarria v State of Queensland [2007] QCA 132 at [17]–[18]). Police have conceded that the Police Powers and Responsibilities Act 2000 is not such a provision, and the tribunal added: "One can exercise powers in a way which is nevertheless discriminatory, and they are not reasonable just because the police officer in question was exercising a statutory power" (Philipp at [185]). The Domestic and Family Violence Protection Act 2012 is later still. The remaining risk is the court-order limb (s 106(1)(b)), which police ran in Philipp and the tribunal left undecided ([186]). Here, the orders of 3 December 2024 and 21 March 2025 did not exclude him from his home and said nothing about the dog; nothing complained of was required by them. Where a statute commands an act, the High Court has held that doing it does not infringe the Act (Lyons v Queensland [2016] HCA 38 at [38], as quoted in Harding at [145]); that reasoning reaches mandatory duties, not discretionary choices such as declining to take a statement or releasing a seized dog.

### 6.2 Section 104

The section protects acts "to benefit the members of a group ... for whose welfare the act was designed". Declining to record an assault complaint, seizing one person's property for another, and misinforming a building manager were adverse acts towards an individual, not measures designed to benefit a group.

### 6.3 The "women" example in the domestic violence Act

Police will say the Act directs attention to the vulnerability of women (s 4(2)(d)). First, s 106 does not exempt acts under the 2012 Act (section 6.1). Second, the test for the person most in need of protection is behaviour-based (s 22A(1)); an example of a vulnerable group does not authorise deciding by sex. Third, the provision helps on causation: it shows sex is a factor police are directed to weigh. The risk is an argument that the later, specific Act qualifies the general one. No decision on that point was found.

## 7. Time

**In time.** The 2026 complaint handling, until 18 June 2027.

**Out of time.** Everything from 1 December 2024 to the release of the dog.

**The law.** The Commissioner "is not fettered by rigid rules but must take into account all of the relevant circumstances" (Buderim Ginger Ltd v Booth [2002] QCA 177 at [22]). A medical condition and pursuing other avenues of redress were evidence of good cause there ([7]). Good cause is not judged solely on medical evidence (GCE v Anti-Discrimination Commissioner [2006] QSC 58 at [22]). Claimed incapacity needs "credible evidence" (Singh v State of Queensland [2016] QCATA 35 at [5]). The tribunal's discretion is "separate and independent", the onus is on the complainant, and the factors are the length of the delay, the explanation, prejudice to each side, and the merits (Smythe at [14]–[16]). In Smythe the Commission accepted a late complaint from a man named as respondent to a protection order, and the tribunal dismissed it because he had "prioritised other matters" ([26]) and CCTV had been lost ([33]–[34]). In Leah the tribunal proceeded where delay was caused by waiting on an internal complaint and there was no prejudice ([28]–[30]). A refusal by the Commissioner is open to judicial review, but narrowly (Hart v Anti-Discrimination Commission Queensland [2011] QSC 319 at [54]; Jin v State of Queensland [2023] ICQ 10 at [31]). No decision was found accepting pending criminal proceedings as good cause.

**Application.** The reasons (Annexure D) answer each Smythe factor. The delay is explained by criminal charges brought by the respondent on the same events, continuous pursuit of every other remedy, police holding the evidence, and his health. There is no prejudice: unlike Smythe, the footage and records exist and their preservation was requested. Smythe is the warning: "prioritising other matters" defeated a late complaint, so the record must show the other matters were the same matters, pursued against the same respondent.

**Even if excluded.** Events outside time may still be relevant as context evidence (Singh at [20]).

**Two traps.** A rejected or unaccepted complaint lapses (s 142), so Annexure D must be complete at lodgement. And the 28-day period to require referral to the tribunal ordinarily runs from the Commission's notice (State of Queensland (QPS) v Fraser [2025] QCATA 39 at [36]–[37]): diarise it.

**EDR19098.** The Commission's letters of 15 April and 18 June 2026 are now in hand. The complaint was accepted as alleging "a possible unreasonable limitation of the right to property and privacy and reputation" under the Human Rights Act; a conference was held on 14 May 2026; it was reported unresolved on 18 June 2026 with no recommended action. Four consequences. First, it does not bar this complaint: it was not made or rejected under the Anti-Discrimination Act (s 142), and the power to reject a complaint "adequately dealt with by another entity" (s 140(2)) is about another entity, not the Commission. Second, the Commissioner may refuse to deal again with property and privacy for the 2025 events, so the human rights part is now confined to the decision of 10 September 2026 (complaint, paragraphs 46–47A). Third, it helps on time: police have been on notice since April 2026, and the Commissioner could have dealt with that complaint under this Act with his consent, in which case it would have been taken as made on the day it was lodged (Human Rights Act s 75). Fourth, the s 88 report is not admissible in a human rights proceeding unless both sides agree (s 88(6)); do not attach it. Still to find: the date he lodged EDR19098 and the complaint form itself.
