## 2. The law

1. A person is entitled to complain within one year of the alleged contravention. For a complaint made later, the Commissioner must accept it if satisfied the complainant has shown good cause, and otherwise must not accept it (Anti-Discrimination Act 1991 s 138).
2. Where a complaint includes at least one in-time contravention and at least one out-of-time contravention, and the in-time part is accepted, the Commissioner may defer deciding about the out-of-time part until conciliation has been tried (s 141A).
3. If the complaint is referred to the tribunal, the tribunal may deal with an out-of-time part if, "on the balance of fairness between the parties, it would be reasonable to do so" (s 175(2)).
4. For the human rights part, the Commissioner may refuse to deal with a complaint not made within one year after the alleged contravention (Human Rights Act 2019 s 70(1)(d)). That is a discretion.
5. The Commissioner "is not fettered by rigid rules but must take into account all of the relevant circumstances of the particular case" (Buderim Ginger Ltd v Booth [2002] QCA 177 at [22]). A medical condition, and having pursued other avenues of redress, were evidence of good cause in that case ([7]). Good cause is not judged solely by the medical evidence (GCE v Anti-Discrimination Commissioner [2006] QSC 58 at [22]).
6. The factors the tribunal weighs are the length of the delay, the explanation, the prejudice to each party, and the merits (Smythe v State of Queensland [2024] QCAT 33 at [16]). Where delay was caused by waiting on an internal complaint and the respondent was able to meet the case, the tribunal proceeded (Leah v A.N. Burnett Investments [2025] QCAT 123 at [28]–[30]).

## 3. Why there is good cause

**(a) The criminal proceedings.** On 24 February 2025 the complainant was charged with offences arising from the same events (CO2500030466). Those charges are still before the Magistrates Court. The respondent to this complaint is also his prosecutor. Until the charges are resolved, any account he gives of those events can be used against him. A person in that position is entitled to be cautious about setting out his case to the prosecuting agency, and he has been. This complaint still says nothing about the events inside the unit (Statement of complaint, paragraph 5).

**(b) He did not sleep on his rights.** He pursued every avenue open to him, in order:

| Date | Step |
|---|---|
| 4 Apr 2025 | Online report of the taking of the dog; status request [42] |
| 17 Apr 2025 | Written complaint to police about the seizure and the visits, within four weeks of the seizure [44] |
| 18 May 2025 | Wrote to police that his address had been changed |
| 2025 | Requests for the body-worn camera footage (RTI/56733) and his records (RTI/54169), and external review by the Information Commissioner (318762) |
| [date lodged] to 18 Jun 2026 | Human rights complaint to this Commission about the same events (EDR19098): accepted 15 Apr 2026; submission 3 May 2026; conciliation 14 May 2026; reported unresolved 18 Jun 2026 [48A]–[48C], [50A] |
| Aug 2025 | Application to the Magistrates Court for the dog and his phone (M14056/25) [46] |
| 11 Sep 2025 | The Information Commissioner closed review 318762 on police agreeing to process a new request for the footage [48] |
| 2025–2026 | Complaints to the Crime and Corruption Commission (CO-25-2722, CO-26-1318) |
| 2026 | Complaint to police (COM-26-01249) and the Case Study of 14 July 2026 [51] |
| 10 Sep 2026 | Police finalised that complaint without contacting him [52] |
| 29 Oct, 5 Nov 2026 | Letters asking who decided and for records to be preserved [53] |

He lodges this complaint promptly after the internal police process closed.

**(c) The evidence was in police's hands.** The footage police agreed on 11 September 2025 to process had not been released ten months later. The records of 1 to 2 December 2024 are held only by police. The complainant could not fully particularise these events until he had police's own material.

**(d) His health.** Police recorded on 1 December 2024 that he has "a mental illness and requires medication" [5]. A report from his treating doctor will be provided.

**(e) No prejudice to the respondent.** The body-worn camera footage, the police database records, the property records and the documents relied on all exist. Police have been on notice of the complaint about the seizure since 17 April 2025, and of the whole of it since the Case Study of 14 July 2026, which asked for records to be preserved. This is not a case of lost footage and faded memories (compare Smythe at [33]–[34]). A fair hearing remains possible.

**(f) The other matters were these matters.** The complainant did not set this complaint aside to pursue unrelated business (compare Smythe at [26]). Every step in (b) was taken against the same respondent about the same events.

**(g) The complaint has substance.** It rests largely on police's own records: their camera, their property receipt, their statements and their correspondence (Annexures A and B).

**(h) These events have been before this Commission and the respondent since April 2026.** The Commission accepted the complainant's human rights complaint about the 2025 events on 15 April 2026 (EDR19098) and the respondent took part in a conciliation conference on 14 May 2026. That complaint was assessed and dealt with under the Human Rights Act only. In his written submission of 3 May 2026 the complainant said police had discriminated against him because of his psychiatric disability. The Commissioner may deal with a human rights complaint under the Anti-Discrimination Act with the complainant's consent, and it is then taken to have been made on the day the human rights complaint was made (Human Rights Act s 75(2), (3)(b)). The complainant was not represented. The discrimination claims have never been considered, and s 142 does not apply because no complaint under the Anti-Discrimination Act was rejected.

**(i) One course of conduct.** The out-of-time episodes are the subject of the in-time one: the 2026 complaint police closed without contact was the complainant's complaint about these same events. Dealing with the in-time part without the rest would leave the substance unexamined.

## 4. What the complainant asks

1. That the Commissioner accept the whole complaint for good cause (s 138(2)(a)).
2. In the alternative, that the Commissioner accept the in-time part and defer the decision about the rest until conciliation has been tried (s 141A).
3. That the Commissioner not refuse the human rights part under s 70(1)(d), for the same reasons.
