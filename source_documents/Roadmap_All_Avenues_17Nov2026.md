# One roadmap: every avenue, in order

17 November 2026. For discussion with your lawyers. This pulls the three earlier plans into one sequence and answers the proposal headed "Shepherd v Queensland Police Service".

## 1. What I think of the proposal

**What it gets right.**

- It separates sloppy records from misconduct, and does not claim the second from the first.
- Its law on the privacy rules before and after 1 July 2025 is right.
- Eaves and the tribunal's power to direct production are described correctly.
- Its two obstacles are real: time, and the need for an arguable breach.
- It is right that the criminal court is the direct route for records about the arrest.
- It is right that the Public Records Act 2002 has been repealed and replaced by the 2023 Act. I confirmed the repeal, not the commencement date.

**Why I would not lodge its letter.**

1. *It states a fact your records do not show.* The letter says your address was recorded as your mother's during the watch-house interview on 24 February 2025, over your objection. The transcript of that interview has no address question. The earliest record of the Benowa address in your papers is a police application of 18 March 2025.
2. *It makes an admission against you.* "I acknowledge that I was aware of the alleged address discrepancy during the original watchhouse interview" starts the time limit at the earliest possible date, on a fact that is not established.
3. *It attaches the Case Study again.* That document still says a call was made from your phone while you were in custody. That is wrong by an hour (section 4).
4. *Three of its four items are not privacy matters.* A reference number that "does not match our records" and a report marked "declined" are evidence for the discrimination claim about the dog. Missing camera segments are a disclosure matter for the criminal court. The reused job number is an administrative error at most.
5. *It asks police to examine themselves on five topics at once.* The likely answer is a general "no breach", which the Information Commissioner can then treat as dealt with.
6. *It puts the complaint before the records.* A privacy complaint must say what was done, when and by whom. You do not yet know.
7. *It leaves out the limits.* Eaves had an admitted breach and a barrister. No access log has been released since the Court of Appeal's decision. Ethical Standards files are closed to the complainant. A privacy complaint about an address would not reach the Ethical Standards file.

**The larger point.** The proposal builds on Head 5, which is your weakest Head on the records. Your strongest material is Heads 1 and 2, the camera sequence between 1:55 and 2:35 am, and it belongs in the criminal court. A privacy complaint, won outright, corrects an address.

**What it underuses.** Since 1 July 2025 police must take reasonable steps to correct personal information when the person asks, unless they could refuse the correction under the access Act, for example because they are not satisfied the entry is wrong. A correction request made after Request 1 is a new act with its own time limit. It does not cure a weakness in the facts, but it removes the lateness problem for the 2025 entry.

## 4. The correction to make now

You said small discrepancies can be fixed or clarified. They can, and fixing them first is what makes the rest believed. This one is not small, because it accuses police of using your phone while you were locked up. It is an honest mistake about time zones, and one paragraph fixes it:

**Text to send.**

Correction to my Case Study of 14 July 2026. I stated that a call was made from my telephone at 3:26 am on 24 February 2025 while I was in custody. That is wrong, and I withdraw it. My carrier's record is in Sydney time. In Queensland time the call was at 2:26 am, before my arrest, and it is the speaker call recorded on the attending officer's camera. On that call, at about 2:28 am, the building manager told police that both names were on the lease. I had given that time as 1:54 am.

Two smaller ones, to fix when you next use the document:

- The address point "arises from the watchhouse interview". Check the footage. If no address is discussed there, remove the sentence.
- "Registered solely in my name" for the dog. Say which register: the council's.

## 5. The privacy complaint to use instead, if Request 1 supports it

**Privacy complaint: recording and use of my address**

1. On 24 February 2025 I told police, on camera at Unit 104 and in my recorded interview, that I had no other home and was sleeping in my car. That evening officers attended my mother's unit at Benowa and were told that my stay there was a two-to-three-week arrangement that had run on.
2. On 18 March 2025 a police application recorded that unit as my address. A handwritten police notice of the same date gave my address as Unit 104, 158 Scarborough Street, Southport.
3. [From the release: the date the entry was made, the unit that made it, and the document it was taken from.]
4. I ask police to state what steps were taken to check the address before it was recorded and before it was used, and to whom it was given.
5. I ask that the record be corrected to show that I had no fixed address in that period, and that the Benowa unit was my mother's home and not my residence.
6. This complaint is separate from COM-26-01249. I do not allege who made the entry or why.

Attach the release and nothing else.

## 6. The authorities, for and against

**For you.**

- Commissioner of the Police Service v Shelton [2020] QCA 96 at [13] and [46] to [49]: access logs are not protected as a class; the documents must be examined; "amendments made" means changes of substance.
- Eaves v Commissioner of Police [2018] QCAT 180 at [55] and [65] to [68]: neither blanket immunity nor blanket release; confidential inspection by counsel.
- ZIL v Queensland Police Service [2019] QCAT 79, and Queensland Police Service v ZIL [2019] QCATA 162: police in breach over a misused address; their appeal dismissed.
- RM v Queensland Police Service [2017] QCAT 71 and CH v Queensland Police Service [2019] QCAT 297: apology and compensation ordered.
- Isles and Queensland Police Service [2018] QICmr 27: the Events tab and the Tasks/Flags tab released without redaction.
- Z18 and Queensland Police Service [2020] QICmr 8 at [32]: the Commissioner rejected the police argument that the document was not a working record.
- "Prioritising privacy to keep victims safe" (Information Commissioner, tabled 10 December 2025): police forms filled from their database gave addresses to people who should not have had them.
- Justices Act 1886 ss 83A, 158 and 158A: disclosure directions, and costs where police failed to investigate a matter within their knowledge.

**Against you.**

- G31 and Queensland Police Service [2024] QICmr 20 at [18]; Darlington and Queensland Police Service [2014] QICmr 14 at [21]; Cameron and Queensland Police Service (7 August 2012): Ethical Standards files are closed to the complainant.
- X59 [2021] QICmr 55, I63 [2024] QICmr 34 and W64 [2025] QICmr 32: access logs refused since Shelton.
- N64 and Queensland Police Service [2026] QICmr 50: street-check reports exempt.
- Z18 at [48] and [66]: the correction itself was still refused.
- Anti-Discrimination Act 1991 s 285AA: you carry the burden of proof.

**Not confirmed. Do not cite.** N37 [2022] QICmr 33, D82 [2024] QICmr 62, H20 [2026] QICmr 142 and N11 [2026] QICmr 153 for what the earlier list said about them.
