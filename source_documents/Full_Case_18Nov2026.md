## 4. Strand 1: the charges

**Where the charges stand.**

- Stalking: discontinued.
- Entering premises and committing an offence: the prosecution proposes trespass instead (your account). Trespass is to "unlawfully enter, or remain in, a dwelling" (Summary Offences Act 2005 s 11(1)).
- Breach of the order (Domestic and Family Violence Protection Act 2012 s 177(2)(b)).

**What the prosecution must prove.**

- For trespass: that your entry was unlawful. The charge turns on whether you had the right to be in the home you leased.
- For the breach: that what you did was domestic violence towards her. She was not there. She was in Perth and flew home overnight; police rang her on the plane (D59, 1:58 am and 2:11 am), and she got home at about 8:15 am (her statement, para 28). At 2:10 and 2:17 am the officers said nothing in the order had been breached by your being there.

**The merits.**

1. **It was your home.**
   - The lease names you as Tenant 1 (D1).
   - The bond authority's record "as of 24 February 2025" shows you as the only bond contributor (D3).
   - On 22 February 2025 you declared to the Registry that your home address was 158 Scarborough Street, Southport.
   - The order of 3 December 2024, made on the police application, required you to let her into Unit 104 with a police officer to collect her "undisputed personal property" (D5). It treated her as the one collecting her things.
   - The building manager confirmed the lease to police on speaker at 2:28 am and again after 5:23 am.
   - In your interview the officer called her "the other leaseholder" twice and asked "Who is the co-leasehold[er]?". There cannot be another leaseholder unless you are one.
2. **The tenancy had not ended.**
   - A tenancy ends only in the ways the tenancy law lists. A tenant's notice of intention to leave ends it only when vacant possession is also handed over, on or after the handover day (Residential Tenancies and Rooming Accommodation Act 2008 s 277(c), as in force on 23 February 2025 and now).
   - Your lease was for 12 months from 11 April 2024, so it ran to 10 April 2025 (D1; leasing manager's emails of 27 January and 9 February 2024). Your notice is marked "Without grounds" and gives the leaving date as "Immediately (minimum notice periods apply-see overleaf)". A notice without grounds cannot set a handover day before a fixed term ends (s 327(2) and schedule 1, part 2). So it could not operate before 10 April 2025, the day her new lease began.
   - One tenant's share passes or ends while the other stays only in particular ways: a transfer the lessor agrees to in writing (ss 237 and 238), a notice from a tenant experiencing domestic violence (ss 308A and 308E), a tribunal order (s 245), or death (s 244). Your notice was not a domestic violence notice. Nothing in your papers shows a tribunal application; the tribunal would have had to consider that the order of 3 December had no condition keeping you out (s 245(4)(c)). What the leasing manager set up was a new lease in her name, not a transfer of yours.
   - On 23 February she was still living there under the same agreement, and your belongings were still inside. The leasing manager's own statement records your complaint that your property was "still being in the apartment" (para 14).
   - Her new lease and new bond, the step all three of you had described in December, did not exist until after your arrest: her bond was notified on 27 February, her lease was signed between 27 February and 6 March, and it began on 10 April.
   - The prosecution must choose. If your notice ended the agreement, it ended for both of you, and she had no tenancy either until April. If it did not, you were still a tenant on 23 February.
3. **No order kept you out.** The order of 3 December 2024 required good behaviour and required you to let her in to collect her property (D5). At 2:10 am and 2:17 am the officers said so on camera.
4. **You went for your belongings, openly, and called police.** In your interview: "first off I wanted to get my stuff ... I have receipts for everything"; "My intention is to beg for my belongings ... to get my belongings and to just escape from her grasp of control"; "I called the police. I thought you guys were coming." On your friend's video, when her friend said to wait until she got back, you said she was saying she would keep all your things and that you did not want to see the person you had just paid to separate from (your account: check the words and time on the video). An entry notice had gone to her on 19 February. On 20 February you wrote that you needed confirmation of your lease "before I arrange entry". Her friend's own statement has you on the phone to police, and has her telling you to take the bed and the couch (D10, para 11). Her friend's text at about 7:51 pm, "Cory's here, he opened the door" (her statement, para 27), matches the start of your 37-minute call to the police line at 7:51 pm.

About 12 February, before she left, you wrote to her: "When you leave I will take my animals" and "we can discuss ownership terms and possibly co parenting when you are back" (texts served in July 2026). A served message with no sender shown reads "Maybe you could even keep it while I was away"; you say she wrote it. The prosecution will set the 12 February message against your interview answer. Being an owner does not on its own answer a stealing allegation: the Criminal Code makes it "immaterial" that the person taking is an owner or one of two or more joint owners (s 396).
