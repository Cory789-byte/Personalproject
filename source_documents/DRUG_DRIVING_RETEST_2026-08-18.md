## 2. Two things in this email change the existing analysis

### 2.1 — The analyte is methamphetamine. The prescription defence does not answer it.

> *"The drug test will simply confirm whether **methamphetamine** is present in the sample."*

`ASSESSMENT_2026-08-13.md` §C.7 flagged exactly this risk and said to find out what the certificate actually said before building anything on cross-reactivity. It is now answered, and the answer is unfavourable.

**Lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse) metabolises to dexamfetamine, not to methamphetamine.** Fluoxetine and quetiapine do not produce it either. So the cross-reactivity argument recorded in the older case file — that the result could be lawful Vyvanse metabolites — **does not answer a methamphetamine finding.**

*(The reverse is not true: methamphetamine metabolises to amphetamine, so an amphetamine finding alone would have been equivocal. A methamphetamine finding is not.)*

**Correction to the file: stop relying on the prescription cross-reactivity point for this matter.** It survives only as a reason the confirmatory data must be produced — to see what was actually identified and on what basis.

## 4. Assessment of the proposal itself

**It is a good proposal and it should proceed, subject to those answers.**

The DNA test is the clever part. The three strongest features of this matter are the undocumented 52.6-hour gap, the shifting accounts of what happened to the specimen, and now the acknowledged problems with the testing process. Every one of those is consistent with a sample that is not what it is said to be. **DNA testing goes directly to identity, which no amount of argument about process can establish or refute.**

The combinations:

| Drug test | DNA | Result |
|---|---|---|
| No methamphetamine | — | **Matter over.** Nothing else needed |
| Methamphetamine present | Not his | **Complete defence** — sample mix-up, and a very serious finding against the laboratory |
| Methamphetamine present | His | Bad. Falls back to chain of custody and disclosure, both weakened |

Two of three outcomes are good, and the third leaves him no worse off than today except for the cost. That is worth doing — but the sequencing question is real, because $250 may answer it before $2,000 is spent.

**The one genuine risk:** a confirmed positive on his own DNA makes the chain-of-custody argument harder, because the substance is demonstrably in the sample. It does not destroy it — a 52-hour unrefrigerated gap degrades a substance, it does not create one, so the argument becomes about mishandling and contamination rather than about the result being wrong. But it is weaker. He should go in understanding that.
