## 1. The case on one page

**The theory.** The same police service gave opposite answers to the same dispute depending on who complained.

- When she had the dog and you complained, police called it civil and told you to let it go. When you had the dog and she complained, police called it a crime, seized the dog and told you to go to court.
- When she needed to get into the unit, police took her in. When you went in, holding the lease, police removed you and told the building manager you were not the leaseholder.
- Each time, the man was the one sent away.

**The three claims.**

| Claim | Attribute | What happened | In time? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. The home | Sex | Removed from the home you leased, 24 February 2025 | No. Needs good cause |
| C. The dog | Sex | Civil for her in December 2024; seized from you in March 2025 | No. Needs good cause |
| B. The complaint handling | Impairment | A one-day deadline, then your complaint closed without contact, June to September 2026 | Yes, until 18 June 2027 |

**Where.** One complaint to the Queensland Human Rights Commission, under the Anti-Discrimination Act 1991 and the Human Rights Act 2019.

**When.** After 6 January 2027, when the human rights part becomes available, and after your lawyers have read it. Earlier if the criminal case ends first.

**What you ask for.**

- A written acknowledgment that you were named on the lease, that the order did not exclude you, and that you reported the taking of the dog on 1 December 2024.
- Correction of the police record.
- Reassessment of your complaint by an officer outside Ethical Standards and Southport, with reasonable time to respond.
- An apology.
- Compensation.

## 2. The tests, in plain words

| Test | What it means for you | Law |
|---|---|---|
| Attribute | Your sex (male), and your impairment | Anti-Discrimination Act s 7(a) and (h) |
| Assumptions count | Treatment based on what is often assumed about men in domestic disputes is treatment based on sex | s 8 |
| Direct discrimination | You were treated worse than a woman was, or would have been, in circumstances that are the same or not materially different | s 10(1) |
| Motive does not matter | Police need not have meant to treat you worse | s 10(2) and (3) |
| More than one reason | Your sex need only be a substantial reason | s 10(4) |
| Indirect discrimination | A requirement you could not meet because of your impairment, which more people without it could meet, and which was not reasonable | s 11 |
| Where the law applies | Police performing functions or using powers under State law | s 101 |
| Who answers | The State answers for its officers | s 133 |
| Who proves what | You prove each claim on the balance of probabilities. Police must prove that the one-day requirement was reasonable, and must prove any exemption. The newer rule that would shift more of the burden to police does not apply to these events | s 285AA, applying former ss 204, 205 and 206 |
| Police's exemption | Acts "necessary to comply with, or ... specifically authorised by" an Act or a court order | s 106 |
| Time | One year. Later complaints need good cause | s 138 |
| Mixed timing | With an in-time claim, the Commission can accept it and decide on the out-of-time claims after trying conciliation | s 141A |
| Human rights | Police must act compatibly with human rights and give them proper consideration. Complain to police first and wait 45 business days. The Commission may refuse a complaint made more than a year after the act | Human Rights Act ss 58, 65 and 70(1)(d) |
| Orders | If a claim is proved, the tribunal can order compensation and other remedies | s 209 |

## 3. Claim A: the home

**What must be shown, and the evidence.**

1. **You were treated worse.**
   - You were arrested at 2:35 am and removed from the home you leased (D59).
   - At 5:23 am an officer told the building manager to restore access for her "and her friend" because "cory's not the leaseholder", after the building manager had confirmed the lease to police at 2:28 am (D59).
   - At about 2:23 am an officer asked her friend "would you want any support services or referral services?" No welfare question was put to you.
   - Her friend stayed. She was on neither the lease nor the order. The building manager's declaration says she was "not an authorised occupant under the lease agreement, nor an approved guest or visitor" (para 25).
2. **The comparison.**
   - On 1 to 2 December 2024 police escorted her into the unit to collect her belongings, under an order that let her in only to collect "undisputed personal property" (D5).
   - On 24 February 2025 police removed you from the same unit, after saying on camera "technically he's allowed to be here" (2:10 am) and "there's no contact conditions in there, so no one's done anything there" (2:17 am).
   - The comparison person is a woman named on the lease as Tenant 1, whom officers had said was allowed to be there, and whose lease the building manager had confirmed.
3. **Sex as a substantial reason.**
   - The reason police gave the building manager, "cory's not the leaseholder", was contrary to what he had told them and told them again on the same call. A stated reason shown to be untrue lets the tribunal look for the real one.
   - The words used: "you're inconveniencing a domestic violence victim survivor who's been put through a lot by her partner" (5:28 am); to your mother that evening, that you had "essentially terrorized a female person"; in the interview, that trying to have her friend removed was "an exercise in control over your partner".
   - Within the hour of telling the building manager you were not the leaseholder, the interviewing officer called her "the other leaseholder" twice.

**Police's answer.** You were removed because you were arrested, and a woman arrested on the same allegations would have been removed too. They will also say the arrest was authorised by law (s 106).

**The reply.**

- Before the arrest, the officers said you were allowed to be there and that nothing in the order had been breached.
- The arrest power is a power, not a duty. Section 106 covers acts "necessary to comply with, or ... specifically authorised by" an Act. Telling the building manager you were not the leaseholder, after he had confirmed the lease, was neither.
- If the charges end in your favour, the arrest stops explaining the exclusion. If they do not, this claim fails. Hold it until the criminal case ends.

**What must be shown, and the evidence.**

1. **You were treated worse.** Your complaint was called civil and no statement was taken. Her complaint led to a home visit, repeated calls, a seizure, and the dog being given to her without a court order (your account of the August 2025 hearing).
2. **The comparison.** A woman, in the same dispute, over the same dog, with the same police service. "Civil" means police leave the dog where it is and the person without it goes to court. Police applied that rule when she had the dog and reversed it when you had it.
3. **Sex as a substantial reason.** The officer said the paperwork did not matter. The reason given was her allegation and the charge. Your allegation in December, made with documents in your hand, did not get a statement taken.

**Your proof of ownership.** $3,150 on 10 December 2023 and $350 on 11 December 2023, the second marked "Cory Alexia Pom"; the vet invoice of 16 July 2024 naming you; the council registration in your name.

**The weak joints.**

- Your messages of 16 to 19 December 2024 and your signature on the transfer. Police will say that by February the dog was hers on paper.
- Your message of about 12 February 2025: "When you leave I will take my animals ... I will take Romeo then". Police will say it shows a plan. The reply: you said it openly, in writing, as a claim of ownership, as you had in December when police called it civil.
- The charge. Police will say the February entry made it criminal. That stands or falls with the criminal case. The prosecution has already proposed trespass for the entry charge.
- Proof of the station conversation. It rests on your account until the police records of 1 and 2 December 2024 are obtained.

## 5. Claim B: the complaint handling

**What must be shown, and the evidence.**

1. **The requirement.** On 18 June 2026 Ethical Standards required particulars (the court matter, the date, the charge and the officers' names) by close of business the next day.
2. **You could not meet it because of your impairment.** The police notice of 1 December 2024 records that you have "a mental illness and requires medication". You need a treating doctor's letter saying why a one-day deadline could not be met.
3. **More people without your impairment could meet it.** Your doctor's letter needs to address this as well.
4. **It was not reasonable.** Police must prove it was (former s 205). On 10 September 2026 the complaint was finalised without contacting you. The outcome names no right and does not mention your condition.

**If you answered in time,** drop the deadline and run this part under the Human Rights Act only: the outcome did not give proper consideration to your rights (s 58).

## 6. Police's answers, and the replies

1. **"Everything followed from the notice and the arrest, not from his sex."** The notice had one condition, good behaviour. It did not give her the dog, did not keep you out of the home, and did not stop police taking your statement.
2. **"It was authorised by law."** Section 106 protects acts necessary to comply with, or specifically authorised by, a law or a court order. Calling one complaint civil and the other a crime, taking one statement and not the other, and telling the building manager you were not the leaseholder were choices. Police must prove the exemption (former s 206).
3. **"By February the dog was hers on paper."** The officer said the paperwork did not matter. The New South Wales register wrote that it has no authority over a dog living in Queensland. No court has decided who owns the dog. The order of 3 December 2024 let her collect only "undisputed" property.
4. **"She took the animals because he said he would take cats to a shelter."** Your message was about two foster cats, not the dog, and police said civil on the night.
5. **"Her friend made a cross-complaint in December too, and nobody was charged."** Her complaint about money got a signed statement the next day. Yours got none.
6. **"He had already moved out."** Your lease ran to 10 April 2025, your notice of intention to leave was "Without grounds" and could not operate before then, your bond stood in your name alone on 24 February, and her new lease began on 10 April (full case, section 4).
7. **"It is out of time."** See section 7.
8. **"He has complained about this already."** The earlier complaint (EDR19098) ran under the Human Rights Act and ended unresolved with no recommendation.
9. **"He could have asked for more time."** Settle what you did on 18 and 19 June 2026 before lodging.

## 7. Time and good cause

- **Claim B** is in time until 18 June 2027.
- **Claims A and C** are more than a year old. The Commission must accept them only if you show good cause (s 138). The good cause:
  - a criminal case on the same events is still running, and a tribunal finding before it ends could cut across it;
  - your health in that period;
  - you took the same events to the same Commission earlier (EDR19098);
  - nothing is lost to police: the footage exists and you asked for it to be kept.
- Put all three claims in one complaint. The Commission can accept Claim B and decide on Claims A and C after trying conciliation (s 141A).
- For the human rights part, the Commission may refuse anything more than a year old (Human Rights Act s 70(1)(d)). The outcome letter of 10 September 2026 is inside that year.

## 10. Remedies, and what the tribunal will not decide

- If conciliation fails you can require referral to the tribunal. Each side usually pays its own costs. The tribunal can make orders including compensation (s 209).
- The tribunal will not decide who owns the dog. Only a property claim does that. Ask your lawyers about the time limit for a property claim between former partners now.
- The federal route is not used. It covers "services" only, which does not fit a seizure, an exclusion or an arrest, and using both routes lets police argue that one bars the other.
