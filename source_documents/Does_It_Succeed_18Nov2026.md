## 2. The entry charge

**What must be proved.** That you entered or were in the unit and committed an indictable offence there (Criminal Code s 421(2)). The court brief makes that offence the taking of the dog at about 8 pm on 23 February. So the prosecution must prove you stole the dog.

**What stealing needs.** A fraudulent taking (s 391). Being an owner or joint owner is no answer on its own (s 396), and a person in possession counts as an "owner" (R v McKiernan [2003] QCA 43 at [13]). The answer is an honest claim of right without intention to defraud (s 22(2)).

**What the cases say.**

- The entry charge covers lawful entry too, so it turns entirely on whether an indictable offence was committed inside (R v Smith [2008] QCA 406 at [21] to [22]). If the stealing is excused, the more serious charge built on it falls (R v Jeffrey & Daley [2002] QCA 429 at [23]).
- The claim "need only be honest, it need not be reasonable", and may rest on "a right the law does not recognise" (R v Perrin [2017] QCA 194 at [82]). "It is not necessary that the right claimed be one known to the law" (R v Waine [2005] QCA 312 at [23]). The High Court principle, quoted in Perrin at [89]: it is ignorance of the civil law that founds a claim of right.
- Once the claim is raised, the prosecution must exclude it beyond reasonable doubt (R v Robertson [2023] QCA 47 at [54]; Perrin at [63]).
- A man who told police "it's mine" and said he had bought the motorbike was entitled to have the defence considered, even though it was raised by a "self-serving statement" and seemed weak (R v C [2001] QCA 387 at [7], [26]).
- It does not matter how the property was taken. The accused need not believe he was entitled to take it "in the way that he did" (Jeffrey & Daley at [24]).
- "Offence relating to property" is read widely (Redland City Council v Burns [2025] QCA 222 at [52]). The High Court heard an appeal from that decision on 4 August 2026; its result could not be checked. Your lawyers should check it before trial.

**Your evidence.**

- You paid for the dog in December 2023. The vet invoice and the council registration are in your name.
- On 4 February 2025 you wrote: "I also have every legal right to still contest ownership even if in your name."
- About 12 February you wrote to her: "When you leave I will take my animals" and "we can discuss ownership terms and possibly co parenting when you are back".
- At the unit you said "its my dog" (her friend's statement; the court brief has you shouting "It's my dog").
- In the interview: "it can be contested because we're in a civil partnership".
- At the seizure you showed your receipts and civil partnership papers, called the registration "just a registration name", and said ownership between civil partners "goes to court".

**Their evidence.** The court brief relies on the change-of-owner form you signed in December 2024, "relinquishing ownership of the dog into the victim's name". They will add your message "I promise I won't take the animals from you", and her reply of about 12 February: "The animals are in my care and my ownership and I take the decision."

**The answer.** The form shows the microchip registration moved to her. It does not show you gave up your claim: seven weeks later you wrote that you still had "every legal right to still contest ownership even if in your name", and you said the same to her face, to police at the scene and to police at the seizure. A claim does not have to be right to be honest. A belief that the transfer did not pass ownership is a mistake about the civil law, which is what the defence protects (Perrin at [89]).

**The hard point.** A signed microchip change-of-owner form was treated as strong evidence that ownership had passed in Chivers v Gold Coast City Council [2010] QSC 98 at [25] and [29]. The difference: there the owner also told council that ownership had passed; you disputed it in writing seven weeks later. That goes to who owns the dog in a civil court. It does not make your claim dishonest.

**Verdict.** Your defence is strong. The prosecution's own proposal to replace this charge with trespass points the same way. Weigh any such offer against section 3: trespass needs an unlawful entry, and you were a tenant.

## 3. Trespass, if it replaces the entry charge

**What must be proved.** That you entered or stayed in the unit "unlawfully" (Summary Offences Act 2005 s 11(1)). The Act defines "unlawfully" as "without authorisation, justification or excuse by law" (schedule 2).

**What the cases say.**

- The prosecution must prove each element, including that the entry or stay was unlawful. A man who went to deal with his own property under a bail condition allowing it was acquitted on appeal (Cleret v Commissioner of Police [2019] QDC 20 at [9] and [31]).
- Trespass "is an offence relating to property", so honest claim of right is available here too, and the prosecution must exclude any excuse raised by the evidence (Preston v Parker [2010] QDC 264 at [194] and [196]).
- Before starting a proceeding for an offence involving entering a place, police must, if reasonably practicable, give the person a reasonable chance to explain why he entered. A proceeding may start only if no explanation is given or the explanation is not reasonable (Police Powers and Responsibilities Act 2000 s 634). Convictions were quashed where that did not happen (Bismark v Queensland Police Service [2014] QDC 152 at [19] and [27]). You told police on camera "This is my home" and that you were on the lease.
- **Time.** A complaint for a simple offence must be made within one year (Justices Act 1886 s 52(1)), or two years if an indictable charge is discontinued by the Attorney-General or the Director of Public Prosecutions (s 52(2)). A new trespass charge for 23 February 2025 may now be out of time. Your lawyers should check this before responding to any offer.

**Your evidence.** The tenancy could end only in the ways the tenancy law lists. Your notice of intention to leave was "Without grounds" and could not take effect before your fixed term ended on 10 April 2025. Her new lease began that day. The leasing manager wrote on 22 February 2025 that she would "refund the bond to Corey" and "prepare the lease only your name", and on 27 February that the new lease was "not yet" signed. The building manager confirmed the lease to police on speaker at 2:28 am. The order did not exclude you.

**Their evidence.** Your notice of 7 December 2024 ("Immediately"), your emails of 6 December agreeing to come off the lease, and the leasing manager's 2026 statement that "from my perspective" you were off the lease once she had your notice. Her friend's account that she told you to leave, and the barricade at about 1:55 am.

**The answer.** Whether you were a tenant is a question of law, not of anyone's perspective. Her friend was not a tenant and had no right to keep a tenant out of his home. If the court finds the tenancy had ended, it must still consider what you honestly and reasonably believed when you went in (Criminal Code s 24): the building manager had told you that you kept the right to go in for your belongings, the bond authority's website showed you on the lease, and you rang police as you went in.

**Verdict.** Your defence is strong if the court applies the tenancy law. Expect the prosecution to argue the December emails were a transfer the lessor agreed to. Your answer is in the full case, section 4.

## 4. Breach of the order

**What must be proved.** That while the order of 3 December 2024 was in force you contravened it. Its only live condition was to be of good behaviour towards her and not commit domestic violence against her (Domestic and Family Violence Protection Act 2012 s 56(1)(a)). The charge does not say which act was the breach.

**The law as at 23 February 2025.**

- The temporary order stayed in force until the application was withdrawn (s 98(d)). It was withdrawn on 25 February, so it was in force when you acted.
- Domestic violence includes behaviour that is emotionally or psychologically abusive, or economically abusive, or that controls or dominates and causes fear (s 8(1)). Emotional abuse is behaviour that "torments, intimidates, harasses or is offensive" (s 11). Economic abuse is behaviour that is "coercive, deceptive or unreasonably controls" the other person without consent, and one of its examples is "removing or keeping a person's property without the person's consent" (s 12). That example is the prosecution's foothold.
- The offence is contravening a particular condition, and you are entitled to know "the particular act, matter or thing alleged as the foundation of the charge" (ACP v Queensland Police Service (No 2) [2017] QDC 293 at [19] and [72]).
- No Queensland case was found that decides whether honest claim of right answers a contravention charge built on taking property.

**Their case.** You entered the unit while she was interstate, took the dog after signing the change-of-owner form and after her message that the animals were in her ownership, and came back at about 1:55 am through a barricade her friend had put up.

**Your answer.** She was in Perth. Nothing was said or sent to her. The dog was disputed, and the order itself let her take only "undisputed" property. You went for your belongings, rang police as you went in, and the officers said on camera that "no one's done anything there" by your being there. Her account of the night is second-hand.

**Verdict.** This is the charge with the real risk. The court will ask whether taking the dog while she was away, after her message, was "removing ... a person's property without the person's consent" in a way that was coercive or unreasonably controlling. Your lawyers should first demand particulars (ACP). Then the answer: openly taking a dog you bought and claimed, after telling her in writing that you would, while she was interstate, is not coercive, deceptive or unreasonably controlling, and it was not her property alone. Conduct towards her friend is not domestic violence against her. The withdrawal of the order on 25 February does not help on guilt: the order was in force when you acted. It helps on penalty and on whether a conviction is recorded.

## 5. Costs, if the charges are dismissed

The court must consider whether there was "a failure to take appropriate steps to investigate a matter coming to, or within, the knowledge" of those bringing the charge, and whether the investigation "was conducted in an appropriate way" (Justices Act 1886 s 158A(2)(b) and (c)). Costs are ordered only if the court is satisfied it is "proper" (s 158A(1)).

**What the cases say.**

- Costs were ordered where the defence had written to police six months before trial pointing to CCTV that cleared the defendant, and police did not engage with it (Oates v Commissioner of Police [2015] QDC 333 at [27]).
- Costs were ordered in a protection order contravention case where the defendant explained a complete defence at the field interview and his solicitor wrote to police, and the investigation stayed inadequate "despite being put on notice" (Wells v Commissioner of Police [2023] QDC 120 at [5], [32] and [39]).
- Winning is not enough on its own: costs were refused after an acquittal on honest claim of right (Burns v Redland City Council [2025] QDC 39 at [30]).
- **So:** your lawyers should now send the prosecutor written representations with the documents (the lease, the bond record, the leasing manager's messages, the 2:28 am confirmation, your ownership papers). That puts police on notice in the way Oates and Wells required.

**Your points.** The court brief leaves out the building manager's confirmation of the lease on speaker at 2:28 am. Your friend, the only adult witness from outside the two households, was not approached for fifteen months. The leasing manager's statement was taken fifteen months later and is contradicted by her own emails. The index to brief leaves blank the section for witnesses who would help you.

**Verdict.** Yes, if the charges are dismissed. These are exactly the points the section asks about.

## 7. Discrimination and human rights

### How the tribunal will test it

The Court of Appeal's leading decision on police and the Anti-Discrimination Act is Woodforth v State of Queensland [2017] QCA 100. A deaf woman said police handled her complaints of assault and theft worse because of her impairment.

- Police responding to a complaint are covered by the Act: the case is "within at least s 101" ([24]).
- The tribunal compares your treatment with how police would have treated a person without your attribute "in response to occurrences of the same kind" ([54]). The circumstances include the complaint police were dealing with ([29]).
- The circumstances include what police were responding to. In the High Court case the Court applied, a student excluded for violence had to be compared with another student who had been violent ([52], [56]).
- Her case was first dismissed because there was "no evidence" a person without her impairment would have been treated better ([32] to [35]). The Court of Appeal sent it back for the comparison to be done properly, and said the result might be the same ([57]).

**What the later cases show.**

- In every Queensland decision found where a direct discrimination claim against police was decided on its merits, the claim failed. The closest to yours is DM v State of Queensland [2025] QCAT 517: a man named as respondent in a domestic violence matter lost because there was "no reliable evidence" police knew of his condition and their footage contradicted him ([23], [42]). In Watego v State of Queensland [2022] QCAT 341, the other person's report to police was "a 'material circumstance'" in the comparison ([245]), and a mere possibility of discrimination was "conjecture only" ([243]).
- What moves a tribunal: an explanation "found to be dishonest" lets the inference be drawn "with greater certainty" (Watego at [32]). Police telling the building manager you were "not the leaseholder" minutes after he confirmed you were, and then calling her "the other leaseholder", is that kind of material, if shown to be more than a mistake.
- A man who said police refused to take his assault statements, and relied on mental-health entries in police records, got past police's application to strike his claim out (Tedesco v State of Queensland [2025] QCAT 109 at [8], [15]). Its result is not yet known.
- The two claims that succeeded against police were about rigid processes that ignored a known disability: a wheelchair user made to use a fixed fingerprint scanner ($8,000, Hunter v State of Queensland [2015] QCAT 179) and a detainee kept from his medication ($10,000, Harding v State of Queensland (QPS) [2025] QCAT 415). That is the shape of Claim B.
- Police cannot rely on s 106 for their powers under the 2000 police powers Act or the 2012 protection order Act: police have conceded the 2000 Act is not an "existing provision", and the tribunal said using a power does not make discrimination reasonable (Philipp v State of Queensland (QPS) [2025] QCAT 505 at [185]).

### Claim C: the dog

**What the tribunal will compare.** Not you against her. You against a woman in your position: named as respondent in a protection order, who took the dog from the shared home while the man was away, after signing the change-of-owner form, and whose former partner complained. Police will say they would have charged her and seized the dog too.

**What your evidence proves.** Different treatment, clearly: your report was called civil, no statement was taken from you, the dog was left with her, and your later report was declined; her report led to charges, a seizure and the dog's release to her without the court application police's own receipt said would be made.

**What it does not yet prove.** That your sex was a substantial reason. Police have an explanation that does not mention sex: by February she was the person protected by the order and the microchip was in her name. The tribunal will accept that explanation unless the evidence shows it does not hold.

**Where it can be won.** The night of 1 to 2 December 2024. That night you were the one who reported people entering your home, being pushed and the dog being taken. Police named you the respondent, took a signed statement from her the next day about money, and took none from you. Your case is that a woman who reported an assault and a home entry would have had her statement taken. If the December records show police decided who was the victim without a factual basis for it, the tribunal can infer that assumptions about men in domestic disputes were a substantial reason (ss 8 and 10(4)). If the records show she gave police specific allegations that they assessed, the explanation stands and Claim C fails.

**Verdict.** Not as it stands. Get the December records first (full case, Annex C). They decide this claim.

### Claim A: the home

**Verdict.** No, not as a separate claim. The comparison is a woman arrested on the same allegations. Police will say they would have removed her too, and nothing in the evidence shows otherwise. Telling the building manager you were not the leaseholder was wrong, but a mistake is not discrimination unless the evidence shows sex was behind it. Even if the charges are dismissed, an arrest is not shown to be discriminatory by its result. Use these facts as background to Claim C and in the criminal case and the costs application.

### Claim B: the complaint handling

**What must be proved.** That police imposed a term you could not meet because of your impairment, which more people without it could meet, and which was not reasonable (s 11). Police must prove it was reasonable (former s 205). The deadline is in writing, so the term is easy to prove. Handling a complaint about police is a function under State law, so s 101 covers it, as it covered police dealing with a complaint in Woodforth ([24]).

**Verdict.** Yes, if three facts hold: you did not meet the one-day deadline; your treating doctor says your condition is why; and the complaint was closed because of it. If you did answer in time, this part fails as discrimination and runs under the Human Rights Act only.

### Human rights

Proper consideration means identifying the rights affected and considering compatibility (Human Rights Act s 58(5)), in "a common sense and practical manner", asking whether the decision-maker "seriously turned his or her mind to the possible impact of the decision" (Owen-D'Arcy v Chief Executive, Queensland Corrective Services [2021] QSC 273 at [137]). Failing to identify a relevant right made the decision unlawful there ([265]–[266]). The Human Rights Act gives no damages (s 59(3)), and the outcome is conciliation or a report.

**Verdict.** Yes, if the outcome letter of 10 September 2026 shows no consideration of your rights. Complain to police first and wait 45 business days (Human Rights Act s 65), then to the Commission.

### Time

Everything before 18 November 2025 needs good cause, and it is decided twice: by the Commissioner (s 138) and again by the tribunal if the complaint is referred (s 175(2)). The tribunal's discretion is "separate and independent" (Smythe v State of Queensland [2024] QCAT 33 at [14]). In Smythe, a man named as respondent to a protection order had his late complaint accepted by the Commission and then dismissed by the tribunal because he had "prioritised other matters" and CCTV had been lost ([26], [33]–[34]). Your answer is that the other matters were these same matters, pursued against the same police service, and the footage still exists. A medical condition and pursuing other avenues were evidence of good cause in Buderim Ginger Ltd v Booth [2002] QCA 177 at [7]. Even events excluded for time can still be used as background evidence (Singh v State of Queensland QPS [2016] QCATA 35 at [20]).

**Verdict.** The in-time claim is safe. The rest has a real chance of being accepted, but not a certain one. The full submission is Annexure D of the complaint package.

## 8. The dog: the deadline

The anti-discrimination tribunal will not decide who owns the dog. The criminal court will not either. The place that decides property between former partners is the federal family court, under the Family Law Act 1975 (Cth).

- Your relationship counts: a relationship registered under a State law meets the threshold for a property order (s 90SB(d)).
- **Time limit: two years after the end of the relationship** (s 44(5)(a)(i)). After that you need the court's leave, which requires hardship (s 44(6)).
- The relationship ends, for this purpose, when you stopped living together as a couple, not when the registration ended. Your papers put that between November 2024 and February 2025. **If it was November 2024, the two years are ending now. If it was early December 2024, they end in early December 2026: in about two weeks.**
- A property claim can include your other belongings left in the unit, not only the dog. The family court can change who owns property, whatever the registers say.

**The second route.** The police seizure scheme is not the only way to decide ownership: a civil action to recover the dog remains open (Morrison Construction v Commissioner of Police Service [2020] QSC 191 at [33]). Its time limit is longer (six years for property claims without personal injury, Limitation of Actions Act 1974 s 10(1)(a)). But it decides who owns the dog now under the general law, and there the December change-of-owner form counts against you (Chivers v Gold Coast City Council [2010] QSC 98). A court has accepted that paying for a dog and registration can support shared ownership (Guilfoyle v Newman [2004] QDC 213 at [13]). Your council registration makes you a "registered owner" under the dog-registration law (Animal Management (Cats and Dogs) Act 2008 s 9(1)).

**Verdict.** The family court route is the strongest way to get the dog back, because it does not depend on the change-of-owner form. See a family lawyer this week, before the earliest possible deadline passes. If it has passed, the civil route remains.
