TITLE: Point 209
NUMBER: 1
SUBTITLE: Prologue — Tunisia, 27 March 1943
---
The Hand. Episode one.

Point two-oh-nine.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

Twenty-seventh of March, nineteen forty-three.

A dome of broken rock in Tunisia, numbered off a British staff map. Held by the second battalion of the four hundred and thirty-third Panzer Grenadier Regiment.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

The day before, on the lower hill in front of it, C Company of the twenty-eighth Māori Battalion had gone up in the afternoon believing the false summit was the objective. They took it anyway. And they held it through a night of counter-attacks.

The Battalion later gave that hill its own name, as a compliment to the Ngāti Porou men who took it. They called it Hikurangi — for the sacred mountain of the East Coast.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

On the twenty-seventh, the Battalion went at Point two-oh-nine itself.

The Vickers guns worked the summit over until the German garrison surrendered at five in the afternoon. Over two hundred prisoners. A hundred and seventy-eight of them marched back to Brigade at last light with their commanding officer, Major Meisser.

The Battalion lost close to a hundred men on and around those two hills. Twenty-two of them dead.

One of the dead was Second Lieutenant Te Moananui-a-Kiwa Ngārimu. Twenty-three years old. Killed on the morning of the twenty-seventh. Awarded the first Victoria Cross given to a Māori soldier.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

And on that hill, on both days, was a corporal in charge of a section.

Thirty-nine thousand, six hundred and thirty-nine. Tame Tamou.

Twenty-two years old.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

Now — everything you are about to hear about those two days comes off one document. Army Form W. three one two one. The recommendation for an immediate Military Medal. It is not a memoir and it is not a family story. It is a form, filled in by officers who were there, and countersigned up a chain of command.

So the words are theirs, not this book's.

[[PAUSE 0.8]]

On the twenty-sixth, in the attack on the forward hill, the recommendation form says he displayed outstanding courage, coolness and leadership. That he directed his men throughout the advance. That his personal example was a great encouragement to them.

On the twenty-seventh, on Point two-oh-nine itself, it says he was tirelessly aggressive. That he personally directed the fire of his light machine guns. And that he fired a captured Spandau himself.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

And then the line the book is named after.

During the attack, he was wounded in the hand. He carried on, urging his men forward by word and by deed.

[[PAUSE 1.5]]

The recommendation went up the chain with every endorsement it could carry.

Bennett, commanding the Battalion. Kippenberger, the Brigadier. Freyberg, commanding the New Zealand Expeditionary Force. Montgomery, Eighth Army. Wilson, Commander-in-Chief, Middle East.

Received at Brigade on the third of April. Through Army by the seventeenth.

The medal was awarded.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

Hold that for a second, because it is the strangest true fact in this book.

The entire command chain of the Eighth Army — up to and including Montgomery — put its signature to a piece of paper about a shearer.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

He came home. And he spent the rest of his working life in the two trades on earth that live most completely in the hands.

A shearer, and a knife hand.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

That is where this book starts, and it is not where it begins.

Because the question is not what happened on the hill. The recommendation form answers that, in the flat official language of nineteen forty-three, and it answers it well enough that five general officers signed it.

The question is how a man came to be standing on that hill at all. What he went home to. And what a family keeps, when everything with a price attached has been taken, or ground too fine to hold.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

To answer that, this book starts ninety years earlier and ten thousand miles away.

In Denmark. With a war that family lost too.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

One note before we go, and the book insists on it.

The title of this book was chosen off the nineteen forty-three form — off that one line about the hand — before the book knew anything about a nine-year-old boy called Ihaka Takarangi, or about what happened to his fingers at a woolshed near Waitōtara in eighteen sixty-eight.

It turned out to run the whole length of the thing.

That is a coincidence of evidence. It is not an argument. And it will not be dressed up as one.
