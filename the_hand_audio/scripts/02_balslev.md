The Hand. Episode two.

Balslev.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

Ground one. A village and parish on the island of Funen, in Denmark.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

Start with the name, because the name is a piece of evidence in its own right.

Denmark ran on patronymics. A man was Hansen because his father was Hans. Nielsen because his father was Niels. And his own son took a new surname from him in turn. For centuries, most of the country was a churn of five or six names.

Balslev is not one of those.

Balslev is a place.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

A nineteenth-century Danish family carrying a place name instead of a "sen" is carrying something specific. A farm. A parish of origin. Or a deliberate choice to be distinguishable.

That is INFERRED — and it is marked, because no document states it.

But it matters practically. A place-name surname points at a parish. And in Denmark, a parish is the key to everything.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

Balsley is Balslev, Anglicised at the point of arrival.

Now, one correction to head off, because the reference works will send you the wrong way. They define Balsley as an Americanised form of the Swiss German Balzli — a pet form of Balthasar.

That is correct. And it has nothing to do with this family.

It describes the Balsleys of Augusta County, Virginia. An unrelated line that shares the spelling by way of a different clerk hearing a different sound.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

Now the war.

[[PAUSE 0.8]]

On the first of February, eighteen sixty-four, the armies of Prussia and Austria crossed the Eider into Denmark with fifty-seven thousand men.

The Danes held the Danevirke — the ancient earthwork across the neck of Jutland. The oldest thing the country had.

They abandoned it within days, without a general engagement.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

Dybbøl fell in April. Als went at the end of June. The Treaty of Vienna closed it on the thirtieth of October.

Denmark lost thirty-five per cent of its territory. All of Schleswig and Holstein.

RECORDED.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

Men who had been Danish in the morning were Prussian subjects by the evening. Liable to Prussian conscription. Farming under a foreign administration.

That defeat is the recognised push behind the first substantial movement of Danes to Australia. A great many of them went to Hamburg, and got on a ship.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

Kele Balsley owned Tasmanian ground nine years later.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

And here is the gap — the first of the two hard gaps in this book, and the one worth the most.

Whether he was a Schleswig Dane, a Funen man, or something else again, is exactly what the Copenhagen emigration protocol will say.

That protocol recorded every emigrant's name, age, last residence and destination, from the late eighteen sixties on. His departure sits inside its window.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

One record. Free to search.

It names his parish. And with a parish, the Danish registers run the line back into the sixteen hundreds.

One document turns four generations into three hundred years.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

That is the whole of ground one. A lost war, a displaced population, a surname that points at a parish nobody has named yet — and a man who turns up on the other side of the world with the deeds to a farm.
