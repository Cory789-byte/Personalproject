The Hand. Episode three.

Levendale.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

Ground two. Tasmania.

And this episode has to be taken in the right order, because the order is the argument. The people before. Then the recruitment. Then the purchase.

Not the other way round.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

The people before.

[[PAUSE 0.8]]

Before Levendale had that name, the country belonged to the Portmairremener — a band of the Oyster Bay tribe, who lived mainly around the mouth of the Prosser River.

Their year had a shape, and the shape is recorded.

Winter on the coast, gathering shellfish and marine plants, until late July. Then the swans and the ducks came into the lagoons and the river mouths to breed. In August the bands moved inland — up the Little Swanport and up the Prosser, to the Eastern Marshes — and took birds, kangaroo and wallaby there.

A seasonal round. Two places. Two halves of the year. Movement between them dictated by what the country was doing.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

Hold on to that, because it comes back in this book. In another family. On the other side of the Tasman.

And there it is called shearing, and freezing work.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

The Portmairremener were gone from that country decades before eighteen seventy-three.

The war and the removals that emptied it are not this family's doing. And they are the precondition for every page that follows.

Land was for sale at Levendale because people had been taken off it.

[[PAUSE 1.5]]

The book in Hamburg.

[[PAUSE 0.8]]

Kele Balsley did not find Tasmania by accident. Tasmania was looking for him.

In the eighteen seventies the colonial government set out to attract Northern European settlers with offers of land. Its immigration agent, Friedrich Buck, published a handbook for emigrants in Hamburg in eighteen seventy — built from official colonial figures.

In August that year the Victoria landed German migrants at Hobart, who went up to Sorell Creek. And Danes followed.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

The machinery was already running.

The bounty regulations paid a migrant's passage against two years' work for an employer — over sixteen thousand assisted migrants came in on that in the eighteen fifties alone.

And from the eighteen sixties, the colony handed out small land grants for one blunt reason: to stop the people it had imported from leaving for the mainland goldfields.

RECORDED.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

So the sequence runs like this.

Denmark loses Schleswig. Hamburg fills with displaced Danes. And a Tasmanian agent is standing in Hamburg with a book promising land.

As to Kele himself — INFERRED. Until the passenger list is found.

[[PAUSE 1.5]]

What Kele had.

[[PAUSE 0.8]]

In eighteen seventy-three, Kele Balsley, from Denmark, bought land at Levendale, and built a family home on it. RECORDED.

He was not the first there. John McConnon had leased and then owned ground near the Prosser. When the Buscombe brothers died, William Brown took over their lease.

And Kele married Mary Ann Brown.

INFERRED — and reasonably strongly. The Browns were the neighbours.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

Two children are indexed. Helena May, born the twenty-sixth of May, eighteen eighty, at Levendale; died the seventeenth of May, nineteen thirty-one, at Stanley Street, Oatlands. And Lilly, born the ninth of July, eighteen eighty-eight, at Levendale; died the sixteenth of August, nineteen twenty-two, at Levendale.

UNVERIFIED.

A third — Sydney, born the twenty-seventh of October, eighteen seventy-eight, at Richmond. Forty kilometres off. An anomaly nobody has explained.

UNVERIFIED.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

Now — what Kele had is on the record for an almost accidental reason.

The district held a fair at his place, to raise money to finish building St Chad's church. And the Hobart Mercury sent a correspondent.

[[PAUSE 0.8]]

That correspondent described the residence and outbuildings of Mr K. Balsley. Set among the hills, six and a half miles from Runnymede. On a rise beside the road, adjoining Leven Banks. With the largest scope of open country in the district lying there, the Prosser winding through it, and the ground climbing north-east in undulating steps into heavy timber.

RECORDED.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

Read that again for what it actually says.

This is not a selector on a scrub block. This is the best open country in the district. A homestead with outbuildings. And enough standing in that district that when it needed to raise money for its church, it held the fair at Balsley's gate.

[[PAUSE 1.5]]

And then: what ended it.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

BROKEN.

[[PAUSE 0.8]]

By nineteen twelve the family had left Tasmania. And no Balsley holds a Tasmanian title now.

Sold, mortgaged and lost — or divided among too many children to remain a living. The transaction that ended it exists, in the Tasmanian historic deeds, eighteen twenty-seven to nineteen seventy-two. Free to look at.

Until somebody looks, this section of the book is one sentence long.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

That is the shape of the Balsley side in miniature, and you have just heard it happen inside forty years.

A man arrives with nothing, from a country that has just lost a third of itself. He is recruited by a government that wants bodies on ground that was cleared of the people who lived on it. He gets the best open country in the district. And two generations later, there is no title, no farm, and no explanation on file.
