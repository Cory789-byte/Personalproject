The River.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

Ground six. And it opens with a caveat that the book puts at the top of the section, so this episode puts it at the top too.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

What follows, on the Crown side, is the Crown's record of what happened to Whanganui Māori.

It is not their account of themselves.

[[PAUSE 1.5]]

The Waitangi Tribunal's report — He Whiritaunoka: The Whanganui Land Report — addressed eighty-three claims of the hapū and iwi of the district. It found them largely well founded.

And it concluded that since eighteen forty, the Crown caused Whanganui Māori substantial harm, through a multitude of policies, laws, decisions, acts and omissions.

By nineteen hundred, they retained one third of their land.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

It went in two stages.

First, the Crown's eighteen forty-eight township transaction — about which the Tribunal has expressed open concern.

Then the Native Land Court, which opened Māori land to private purchase. In this district, frequently by Pākehā farmers setting themselves up on it.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

Not seizure.

Conversion.

RECORDED.

[[PAUSE 1.5]]

And then the river.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

From nineteen thirty-eight to nineteen sixty-two — twenty-four years — Whanganui Māori litigated for it.

The Native Land Court. The Native Appellate Court. The Supreme Court. The Court of Appeal. And a royal commission.

They came to effectively the same answer.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

The Tribunal's later finding was that what the tribe had owned was a river.

And the courts could not see it that way.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

The Whanganui people protested the loss consistently, where other iwi had not.

They never stopped saying it was theirs.

RECORDED.

[[PAUSE 1.5]]

Into that world: Kimi and Pare.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

Kimi Tamou and Pare had their sons inside it.

Tame Kimi, born the twenty-seventh of June, nineteen twenty. Tio Kimi, born nineteen twenty-one. A third, born the twentieth of January, nineteen twenty-seven.

Pare had earlier been with Peter Neilson. That son — Peter TeRapa, also Pita Wiwo — was born the tenth of March, nineteen fifteen.

RECORDED, from the whānau's own whakapapa sheet.

[[PAUSE 1.5]]

Now look at the names, because the names are this book's mechanism made visible.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

Tame Kimi is Tame, son of Kimi.

Tio Kimi is Tio, son of Kimi.

Māori did not historically use fixed surnames. The father's given name travelled with the child, shifting every generation — until the Crown's registers froze it.

[[PAUSE 1.2]]

Tamou is Kimi's father's name. Frozen at the moment of registration.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

And that man — the oldest thing in this book on the Whanganui side — is one generation up, and unnamed.

He is not in an archive.

He is at the marae.

[[PAUSE 1.5]]

Two more things about names, and then the dates.

Tame is Thomas. The same name, written in Māori.

And Kimi means to seek.

[[PAUSE 1.5]]

Now set the river case against one man's life, because they run alongside each other exactly.

[[PAUSE 1.0]]

When the river case opened in nineteen thirty-eight, Tame was eighteen.

When the Battalion sailed in May nineteen forty, he was nineteen.

And when he stood on Point two-oh-nine, the case was five years old — and had nineteen years to run.

[[PAUSE 1.5]]

It ended in nineteen sixty-two, having achieved nothing.
