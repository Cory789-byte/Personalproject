# What the case has been refined into — measured against what Dwyer said at the mention
**15 September 2026. Internal. Transcript quotations verified from
`documents/transcripts/MENTION_7AUG2026_segments.jsonl` on this date, by timestamp.**

## 1. The difficulty Dwyer actually identified

Three passages, in his words.

**The crux is causation, not diagnosis** [247–275]:
> "As in most of these cases, the presence or existence of an injury for the purposes of a medical
> diagnosis is usually not so much in dispute. So the real crux of your case is going to be to show
> that if you have a diagnosis of a psychiatric condition **as you do in this particular case**, how
> certain events or things that took place in your workplace caused that, were significant
> contributors to the cause of that condition."

**Non-compliance is ubiquitous, so it proves nothing by itself** [3477–3500]:
> "failures to comply with legislation, failures to comply with protocol, failures to comply with
> policy — it happens every single day in every single workplace. **It's a question of whether or not
> there's anything material that arises from any of that** ... it's only a problem if somebody gets
> sacked or somebody gets killed ... it's only then when these things get a light shone on them and
> they become relevant."
> "it's not about you proving that this was a terrible place, a toxic place, that there's multiple
> examples of ways they didn't comply with this or didn't do that."

**And his doubt was about the advocate, not the case** [335–368, 3405–3432]:
> "I fully appreciate as a person who's not represented, you may have some difficulty
> differentiating between relevant and irrelevant. And in fact, it's not at all uncommon to find an
> unrepresented person with an intense focus on proving certain things that are ultimately, from an
> objective legal perspective, utterly irrelevant ... sometimes people in that situation are very
> unwillingly disavowed of that view."
> "you're floundering around a bit ... people do make what I think is the mistake of overcooking it.
> Your case, you've got to keep coming back to those stressors, but also what you can and can't
> prove ... there's parts of this that are going to need documents, but it sounds like **not the sort
> of volume of documents you're talking about**."

⇒ **The difficulty was materiality without volume.** How does an unrepresented appellant make ordinary
workplace events provable and material, without trying to prove the workplace was bad and without a
mountain of documents? Dwyer thought that was the hard part and doubted it would be understood.

## 3. The inversion — and it is the answer to the difficulty

Dwyer's implied route to materiality was **subtraction**: fewer stressors, fewer documents, prove the
ones that matter. The route actually taken was different and better.

**The documents were not obtained. They were made unnecessary.** An admitted document does not need
production, provenance, or an author. The 9 September letter fixes the footing in terms: where the
document *is* the step taken, the admission establishes that the step was taken on that date in
those terms. So the acts are proved **on paper, now, without a witness** — and the disclosure fight
Dwyer said was disproportionate was not won, it was dissolved.

That is why the volume problem disappeared without the case shrinking. Dwyer offered a smaller case.
What was delivered is a complete case that costs nothing to prove.

**And the materiality gap is answered on his own test.** He said non-compliance becomes relevant only
when something material arises from it — "somebody gets sacked or somebody gets killed". Here the
material thing is the diagnosed injury itself: work-timed onset at 18 June 2024, a clean pre-morbid
baseline, physiological corroboration, an unbroken referral-and-certificate spine, treating records
keyed to the admitted acts. The outcome that makes ordinary acts material is the injury, and it is
the one fact he said was "not so much in dispute".

## 4. ⛔ Three corrections to hold, or the claim overstates itself

1. **Dwyer did not say this case would be hard to prove.** He said causation is "the real crux" in
   cases of this type, and that the diagnosis here is not much in dispute. He was locating the
   contest, not predicting failure. What he doubted was whether an unrepresented appellant would
   tell the relevant from the irrelevant and resist overcooking. **That is the prediction that was
   defied** — and it is a better thing to have defied than a prediction about the merits.
2. **The admissions prove the acts. They do not prove causation.** 298 admitted facts establish that
   steps were taken on dates in terms. The bridge from those steps to the condition is medical, it is
   treating evidence, and it is not yet closed: Krishnaiah declined to provide a report on
   5 September, no expert report has been served, and element (d) runs on the treating records plus
   the oral evidence of Krishnaiah and Hawes. The schedule sets the bridge up. The witness box
   finishes it.
3. **Nothing has been found by anybody.** "Undismissable on the served record" is not a result. The
   s 32(5)(a) exclusion remains the Respondent's to run, and they have not named a witness for it.

## 5. What the case now is, in one sentence

One mechanism — responsibility imposed and the means to discharge it withheld — proved by the other
side's own 298 admissions without calling a witness, with the medical schedule keying the treating
record to those admitted acts, and with the only live element being whether employment was **a
significant contributing factor**, on a record that carries the Regulator's own delegate's finding
the other way.
