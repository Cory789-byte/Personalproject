## 1. ⭐⭐ THE PARADOX THAT PROVES IT — HIS CONFUSION *WAS* THE EVIDENCE

Dwyer could not read the table. *"Doesn't make any sense to me."* · *"What does press mean?"* ·
*"What does filters mean?"* · *"A–E, what's that a reference to?"* · *"I still don't understand."*

⭐⭐⭐ **That is the wrong reaction to a machine-generated document.** Generated text is
**conventional** — it reaches for the standard form, and a standard form is instantly readable to a
Commissioner who has read thousands of them.

**What he could not read was a bespoke scheme built by one person for their own use.** ⇒ **The
opacity was the authorship.**

## 3. ⭐⭐⭐ THE TWO MOMENTS THAT SETTLE IT

### 3.1 He adopted the word — in the next breath
> **[24:37]** Dwyer: *"status, **press**. **What does press mean?**"*
> **[24:46]** Appellant: *"To either swear it or produce it."*

> filters A–E."*

⭐⭐⭐ **Four seconds after asking what the word meant, the Commissioner was using it himself, in his

⚠ And note: ***"press"* is the right word.** *"Do you press that application?"* is ordinary advocacy
language. He had used a term of art **correctly, as a status field in a table** — which is why it
read as unfamiliar in that position and entirely familiar the moment it was explained.

⇒ **A judge does not adopt vocabulary from a document he believes has no author.**

### 3.2 ⭐⭐ The table checked out against a sealed court document

⭐⭐⭐ **The table was not a standalone document. It was an index keyed to a sealed filing — and the
Commissioner verified the cross-reference in the room, from memory on one side and the page on the

⇒ **That is a system with two ends, and both ends held.** No summary produces that. It requires
knowing what was asked for, where it was asked, and what remained live.
