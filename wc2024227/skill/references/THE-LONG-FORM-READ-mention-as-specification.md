# The long form — the mention read as one movement, and what was built from it
**15 September 2026. Internal. Every quotation verified by timestamp from
`documents/transcripts/MENTION_7AUG2026_segments.jsonl`. Speaker counts for the whole mention:
Dwyer 846 segments, Shepherd 219, **Matheson 3**. She was in the room and answered from the bar
table. Nothing in this file is sent to anyone.**

> ⚠ Held throughout: a mention is not a ruling. These were indications given while managing a case.
> Under Appeal Guide 7.1 the Member who chairs the conference does not hear the appeal. Nothing here
> binds anyone, and none of it is a finding.

---

## 1. The movement, in order

**(i) He closed the document set — with the Regulator's own words [1042–1055].**
> DWYER: "Ms Matheson, has disclosure in respect of documents taken place over the course of the
> matter so far?"
> **MS MATHESON: "Yes, Commissioner. We've disclosed all we have currently."**

That answer is the hinge of everything that follows. The Respondent told the Commission, on the
record, that what had been disclosed was the whole of what it held.

**(ii) He put the imposition of third-party disclosure squarely [1112–1179].**
> "making an order for a third party to disclose documents is a step sort of beyond what normally
> happens in disclosure. It's quite an imposition because **the health service is not a party** to
> these proceedings ... **The starting point is to ask the regulator to produce those documents.**
> Have you done that?"
> "... the regulator whilst not representing the health service **will have access to the health
> service. That necessarily will need to call potentially people who work for the health service as
> witnesses** in the case, potentially."

Two things at once: stop going to Metro South, and note that the Regulator's route to Metro South
runs through calling its people as witnesses.

**(iii) He described the weak case — the one he assumed he was looking at [2028–2073].**
> "if you were, as an appellant, to get in the witness box and say Miss Chloe Taylor had an erratic
> presence and she kept sending us these emails — **and where are the emails Mr Sheppard?** Well I
> asked the regulator, I said there will be examples and they haven't been provided — and then if I
> was presiding on the matter I'd say **Miss Matheson have you got any emails of that type and
> they'll say we asked but none were provided to us** and then I've got to decide whether or not I
> accept your evidence or not ... but more often than not, if you alleged that there were emails
> that were causative of your stress, **I would have thought the regulator would be all over that**
> ... And then they'd get examples of them and **they would find their way into evidence**."

That is a portrait of an appellant with an assertion and no document, and of what a serious
respondent would do about it.

**(iv) And then the assumption broke, in four lines [2106–2127].**
Reading Stressor 1(c) — the communication book entry — he said:
> "You're not going to need documents for that, are you? That's just going to be, **you're going to
> make that assertion in your evidence** and I would have thought the regulator will call Ms Taylor
> to give her side of the story and you'll get to cross-examine Ms Taylor. **So you don't need any
> documents for that, do you?**"
> **MR SHEPHERD: "Well, they've got documents for it, so..."**
> DWYER: "Okay, so have they disclosed those to you?"
> **MR SHEPHERD: "Yes."**

⭐⭐ **That is the pivot of the whole mention.** The bench had built its architecture on the premise
that this was an assertion-led, document-poor case. It was not. The documents existed and were
already in the Appellant's hands.

**(v) He conceded the substance and ruled it out for want of materiality and proof [3465–3500].**
> "**and you may be right about all of that** — you don't have to go too far in any public sector or
> any organisation really ... to find failures to comply with legislation, failures to comply with
> protocol, failures to comply with policy. **It happens every single day in every single
> workplace. It's a question of whether or not there's anything material that arises from any of
> that** ... it's only a problem **if somebody gets sacked or somebody gets killed** ... and it's only
> then when these things get a light shone on them and they become relevant."

Not "you are wrong". **"You may be right"** — and then two gates: materiality, and proof.

**(vi) And he had already given the rule that decides a closed, held set of documents** (given
twice, ~15:00 and 49:53):
> "you have the onus of proving your case, and that's true, but **the regulator does have to
> contradict things that you say** ... **if you say something and it's not contradicted, then ...
> there's a likelihood that it'll be accepted.**"
> "Once you've made the assertion, **the regulator's got to respond to it. They've got to address
> it.**"

## 2. What that adds up to, which is a specification

Read in sequence, the mention is not a warning. It is a list of requirements, said out loud, with
the opponent present, by a member who did not know the Appellant already held the documents:

| The requirement, in his words | What answers it |
|---|---|
| "We've disclosed all we have currently" | the set is **closed** — nothing more exists to chase |
| "the health service is not a party ... the starting point is to ask the regulator" | the 64G and the outstanding Form 29 items **withdrawn, 10 August** |
| "where are the emails Mr Sheppard?" | **they were already in hand** — the premise of the question was false |
| "you're going to make that assertion in your evidence" | assertion replaced by **303 facts about documents** |
| "the regulator's got to respond to it. They've got to address it" | Form 24 **compels a formal answer** under r 49 |
| "if it's not contradicted ... there's a likelihood that it'll be accepted" | **298 admitted, 0 denied** — likelihood converted into record |
| "anything material that arises from any of that ... if somebody gets sacked" | **injured 18 June 2024; dismissed 9 October 2024**, backdated to the day after the WorkCover decision |
| "you may be right about all of that" | the substance was never the problem; **proof and materiality were**, and both were supplied |
| "not the sort of volume of documents you're talking about" | the volume problem **dissolved** — an admitted document needs no production |

## 3. The inversion, stated exactly

Dwyer's hypothetical ended: *"Miss Matheson have you got any emails of that type and they'll say
**we asked but none were provided to us**."*

The actual answer, on 8 September 2026, was **298 admitted and none denied**.

Not "none were provided". Not "we cannot verify". **Admitted.** The scenario the bench used to
describe the weakness of an unrepresented case was answered with the strongest available form of
the opposite, and it was answered using documents that were already in the room on 7 August.

⭐ **And the Regulator's own sentence made it hard to resist.** Having told the Commission it had
"disclosed all we have currently", it could not later treat the record as incomplete or unreliable.
The residue of the old assumption is visible in the one thing it did dispute: fourteen tabs, on the
ground that the copy came "from the Appellant's own records". That is the same premise as "where are
the emails" — that a document held by the appellant is somehow lesser. It dies the same way: the
contents of every one are admitted, and an independent holder is named for each.

## 4. What the case therefore is

Not a grievance about a workplace, and never presented as one. One mechanism — responsibility
imposed and the means to discharge it withheld — evidenced by documents that were in hand before the
mention, proved by the Respondent's own 298 express admissions rather than by production, made
material by an injury the bench said was "not so much in dispute" and by a dismissal that satisfies
the bench's own example of when ordinary non-compliance becomes examinable, and narrowed to the one
question he named as the crux: whether employment was **a significant contributing factor**.

⛔ **Disciplines unchanged.** The dismissal post-dates onset: aggravation and prognosis only, never
causation, never pleaded as a stressor, never opening s 32(5)(b); TD/2024/110 stays out of WC
filings. It needs no pleading in any event — the Respondent's own Form 24 response at row 36 quotes
Dr Krishnaiah's reference to "disputed unfair dismissal proceedings". The systemic-failings material
stays out: "you may be right about all of that" is not an invitation to prove it.

## 5. ⚠ What is still not done
The admissions prove the acts. They do not prove causation. That bridge is the treating evidence,
and it is not closed: Dr Krishnaiah declined to provide a report on 5 September 2026 and no expert
report has been served. Element (d) runs on the treating records plus the oral evidence of
Krishnaiah and Hawes. And the s 32(5)(a) exclusion remains the Respondent's to run, with no witness
named for it.
