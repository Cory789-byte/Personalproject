# Thesis: why the Regulator reserves even where its reason is weak (18 Oct 2026)

Internal working theory. ⛔ Never in correspondence. State the chronology, never the motive, on paper.

## Thesis
The Regulator is preparing a **controlled exit, not a defence**. Since 8 Sep every move has served one aim: if it must concede, the concession should establish nothing beyond acceptance of the claim. That means no findings, no admission about MSH's conduct, and no published correction of the review's "two out of three" method. It should also happen on the Regulator's timetable, through counsel, after it has discharged every procedural obligation that could be criticised.

The 16 Oct reservation is part of that exit. It is a low-cost protective step attached to the single document that most damages MSH's manager, so that the document's meaning stays "not conceded" going into the conference and any settlement. **The reason is weak because it is not meant to win a hearing. It is meant to preserve the wording of the exit.**

## Mechanism: why it is rational for them
- **Officer limits.** Matheson can confirm documents but cannot concede the case. A reservation is how an officer confirms without conceding.
- **The internal brief.** An agency conceding must justify it to its delegate, typically as "arguable, but risk and cost outweigh". Reservations keep the "we had arguments" record intact.
- **MSH's exposure.** Acceptance opens s 237. Findings about the manager's conduct would help a civil claim and the employment matters. "For the proceeding only" and "without admission" keep the concession from carrying meaning elsewhere.
- **Institutional.** A published loss would correct the Review Unit's method (Mahaffey). Consent orders avoid that.

## Addendum (Cory's question, 18 Oct): is the reservation really about the civil claim?
**Mostly yes. It is aimed at findings, not admissions.**
- **Admissions cannot travel anyway.** r 49(1) makes them "for the proceeding only", and MSH is not a party to the appeal.
- **What can travel, as persuasion, is a reasoned decision after a contested hearing.** It could find that MSH knew of the fatigue risk, that the manager held the governance duties (Tab 32A) and they were not carried out, that complaints went unanswered, and that Cory's account is preferred to Taylor's and Reese's. Those are the civil claim's duty, breach and credit findings. They would not bind MSH (it is not a party, so there is no issue estoppel), but they would be public, persuasive, and drive settlement value.
- **The reservations on relevance and weight** (MSH officers' statements, the review's findings, Tab 32A) are the tools to keep the Commission from making those findings. Consent orders without reasons avoid them entirely.
- **Aligned interests, not proof of coordination.** WorkCover insures MSH's common law liability, and MSH wants no findings. The Regulator has its own institutional reason (the review method). The decision sits with counsel and the delegate.

**For Cory:** findings cut both ways. A contested hearing could also produce adverse findings (credit, causation, the stressors Dwyer was sceptical about on 7 Aug) that would hurt the civil claim. Consent orders with no findings are neutral for the civil claim, which is proved on MSH's own documents through a PI firm. Do not push for a hearing to get findings. Use their wish for "no findings" to secure the E4 terms, and never accept a release of the common law claim.
