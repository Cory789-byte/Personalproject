## 2. WHAT KIND OF CASE THIS IS

⭐⭐⭐ **A conduct-only defence on one element.** Every witness speaks to management action under
s 32(5)(a). Nobody speaks to injury, to whether employment was a significant contributing factor,
or to causation.

| The Respondent's pleaded point | Witness who supports it |
|---|---|
| SOFC ¶8 — past history of anxiety | **none** |
| SOFC ¶14(f) — the pandemic-leave error was *"human error by Ms Taylor on the background of high work demands"* | **none.** Taylor's outline does not mention the pandemic leave at all |
| SOFC ¶22(f) — the 7-hour break *"was not causative of the appellant's injury"* | **none** |
| SOFC ¶23 — *"no breach of any mandate"* | **none.** The only witness who touches it, Ms Wright, *"had no knowledge"* of it |
| Any expert opinion on anything | **none served**, though direction 4 required any expert report relied on to be served by 30 Sep |

⇒ **The Appellant calls two treating doctors as experts** (Dr Krishnaiah, Dr Hawes). **The
Respondent calls none.** On injury and causation the medical evidence is, as the directions now
stand, uncontradicted by any witness. Relying on an expert later would need the directions varied.

⇒ This is consistent with what the Respondent has already admitted about its own decision: fact
261 (*"you sustained a personal injury of a psychological nature"*) and fact 262 (employment *"a
significant contributing factor"* for factors 2, 3 and 4).

## 3. ⭐⭐⭐ WHERE THE OUTLINES CONFLICT WITH THE ADMITTED RECORD OR WITH EACH OTHER

Ranked by weight. Every item is a document against a document or a witness against a witness. None
depends on anything he says.

### 3.1 The 2020 agreement — three accounts of one document
| Source | What it says the agreement does |
|---|---|
| Ms Wright's outline | reduces his break *"from ten hours to eight for more than one break period, with no end date, so that it continues to apply indefinitely"* |
| Ms Taylor's and Ms Reese's outlines | applies; HR *"confirmed the agreement was on file and continued to apply"* — Reese has this only *"through Ms Taylor"* |
| ⭐⭐ **MSH's own HR, Ms Forrest, 7 July 2026 — admitted, facts 224–225** | *"allows you to work with only an 8 hour break, **however this is only applied where staff initiated shift swaps have occurred**"* |
| Fact 234, admitted | the SOFC *"does not allege that the consecutive shifts of 17 and 18 March 2024 arose from a staff initiated shift swap"* |
| Fact 254, admitted — the decision under appeal | *"there was uncertainty between you and the employer regarding whether the 8-hour agreement continued to apply"* |
| ⭐⭐⭐ Fact 260, admitted — the decision under appeal | *"I find the rostering of these two shifts amounted to **unreasonable management action** given that it was in direct contradiction to the award and the 8-hour agreement"* |

⇒ On the employer's own HR account the floor on 17–18 March was ten hours, not eight. On every
account the break was short of the floor. And the Respondent's own review officer found the
rostering unreasonable. **No HR officer is called to explain any of it.** Tab 21, the Forrest
email, is one of the nine now requested from MSH; its contents are already admitted.

### 3.2 Two different reasons for refusing the same fatigue payment
| When | Reason given | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 May 2024, to him | *"would not be processed due to the existing 8-hour agreement"*, with the note that he could terminate it | facts 247–248, admitted |
| Now | not overtime, so cl 18.10 does not apply; *"a rostering practice issue for the line manager rather than an unpaid entitlement"* | SOFC ¶24(b); Ms Wright's outline |
| ⭐⭐ Payroll's own email, 3 May 2024 | *"Fortnight 18.03.24 … has too many ordinary shifts for the fortnight resulting in wage reduction of 7.55hrs as **one of the shifts needs to be overtime**"* | fact 188, admitted |

⇒ The reason given at the time is not the reason relied on now. And the "not overtime" reason meets
payroll's own statement that a shift in that very fortnight needed to be overtime. ⚠ Which shift is
not identified in the email. Put it as a question, never as a finding.

### 3.3 "He could refuse shifts" against "he was on ordinary rostered hours"
- Ms Taylor and Ms Reese: *"the Appellant could refuse shifts at any time"* (SOFC ¶22(b)).
- Ms Earl: *"he was rostered on ordinary hours rather than overtime at the time"* — which is the
  whole basis for denying fatigue leave.
⇒ A rostered ordinary shift is not an offered extra shift. The Respondent needs the 18 March shift to
be **ordinary** to defeat the fatigue claim and **optional** to shift the responsibility. The two
propositions sit awkwardly together, and both come from its own witnesses.

### 3.4 "Human error, not repeated" against "a rostering practice issue for the line manager"
Taylor confirms *"human error, and was not intentional or repeated"*. Wright's technical advice calls
it *"a rostering practice issue for the line manager"*. A practice is not a one-off. And nobody says
whose error it was or how it happened. The seam between two fortnightly rosters is not addressed.

### 3.5 Easter Monday, 10 April 2023 — two witnesses, opposite uses of one shift
- Ms Taylor counts *"the Monday public holiday"* among the holidays he worked, to show he was
  rostered fairly.
- Ms Earl says his 10 April shift was a night shift treated as falling on 11 April, *"so no public
  holiday entitlement arose"*.
⇒ The same shift is a public-holiday shift for fairness and not a public-holiday shift for pay.

### 3.6 The allowance that only the manager can claim
- Ms Wright: the discretionary public holiday allowance *"must be approved and submitted by the line
  manager on an AVAC"*.
- Ms Earl: nothing is payable for 10 April 2023 *"because no AVAC was ever submitted"*.
- Facts 200–201, admitted: all five AVACs in Feb–May 2024 were initiated by Ms Taylor; **none** by him.
  Fact 193: payroll told him to *"speak to your Line Manager to have them corrected with an AVAC"*.
⇒ Nothing is owed because the form only the manager can lodge was never lodged. This is the
mechanism of the whole case — **responsibility sits with him, the means to discharge it sits with
the manager** — stated by their own payroll witnesses.

### 3.7 The pandemic leave, February 2024

- ⚠ The pandemic material sits in Ms Earl's outline but is attributed inside it to *"Ms Wright"*.
  Whose evidence it is will have to be settled.

### 3.8 The 15 May 2024 email about office hours
Ms Reese's outline calls it, *"read in substance"*, an accusation that Ms Taylor was not working her
hours. The admitted record around it:

| Fact | What it records |
|---|---|
| 111 | 13 May 2024, Ms Stibbard to the team: *"During business hours you are to still contact Chloe."* |
| 71–72 | ⭐ 23 Aug 2023, Ms Taylor to the whole team, *"What's Chloe's Hours?!"*: *"I am fulltime and work flex hours"*; start *"between 6-9am"*, finish *"2-5pm"*. **Authenticity confirmed today** |
| 73 | the SOFC does not allege she stated fixed office hours between 23 Aug 2023 and 17 May 2024 |
| 74 | his 15 May email asks her to *"share your office hours so the entire department can be aware"* |
| 76–77 | 6:23 pm, Ms Reese: the email *"did not comply with our Code of conduct"*; asks him to retract it |
| 78 | 7:09 pm, his reply: *"Requesting clarity on business hours is a reasonable question, especially when no one in the department can provide a definitive answer in response to directives to contact Chloe during office hours."* |
| 113 | ⭐ 17 May 2024, 9:30 am, Ms Taylor to the team, headed *"Switchboard Manager - On call and Hours."* |
| 79 | ⭐ 21 May 2024, Ms Reese to him: *"With regards to your concerns about having more clarity as to what are Chole's business hours I will follow up on the issues raised."* |

⇒ The directive was to call her in business hours. Her hours were a range she had published herself.
He asked for them. He was told the question breached the Code of Conduct. Two days later she emailed
the team about her hours, and four days later her manager said the concern would be followed up.
**That is the manner point under s 32(5)(a), and it is made entirely from admitted documents.**

⚠ **Honest limit.** The email went from his personal address to about eighteen staff and HR and used
the word *"inconsistency"*. A member could find a request to retract it reasonable. The strength is in
the manner — Code of Conduct, High importance, 6:23 pm, then the question answered — not in the
email itself.

## 4. WHAT THE OUTLINES DO WELL — STATED PLAINLY

- ⭐ **Ms Reese's 2023 response is a documented, responsive process.** She met him within three
  days, reviewed eight months of shifts, answered each point in writing, pointed him to the
  grievance policy, and he then went full time. That is the strongest management-action material in
  the file, and stressor 1(c) should not be pressed as though it were weak for them.
- **The Easter 2023 point has a real answer.** A 0.8 FTE eight-shift fortnight against full-time
  ten-shift fortnights explains much of a pay gap. The question that survives is who allocated the
  public holidays, which is a roster question, not a pay one.
- **The Communication Book reaction is corroborated** by a contemporaneous email he tendered himself.
- **The union-delegate text** is met with an account of support given.

⇒ The defence is strongest on stressor 1 in 2023 and weakest on stressor 3 in March 2024. That is
the right way round for him. **The case was always one mechanism with one admitted instance — the
7-hour break — and that is exactly where the outlines contradict each other and the admitted record.**

## 5. WHAT THE OUTLINES LEAVE OUT — THE STRONGER EVIDENCE

Per the method rule adopted on 21 September: what a document asserts about itself is weak evidence;
what it omits, and when it arrives, is strong.

1. ⭐⭐⭐ **No expert and no medical witness.** Causation is not contested by any evidence.
2. ⭐⭐ **No HR witness.** HR advice is the stated basis for the retraction request, for removing the
   email, and for the agreement continuing. Every one of those is hearsay, twice over for Ms Reese.
3. ⭐⭐ **Nothing about June 2024.** Tab 30A — confirmed authentic today — is Ms Taylor on the
   injury date: *"I am sorry I haven't been there for you all over the past week"* (facts 85–87).
   No witness addresses the onset.
4. ⭐ **Nothing about Ms Taylor's 17 May 2024 account to HR** — the character email, which sits in
   the Respondent's own disclosure. They have chosen not to lead it. If it is raised at hearing it is
   outside the outline.
5. **No witness for the SOFC's excuses** — Ms Taylor's workload (¶14(f)), the mandate (¶23),
   non-causation (¶22(f)).
6. **No Ms Stibbard, no Ms Smith, no delegate** for the pandemic leave.

## 6. ⛔ ONE PARAGRAPH THAT BELONGS TO ANOTHER TRACK

Ms Reese's outline narrows SOFC ¶16(a). It now says she was unaware of the disclosure *"at the time
she sent this email"*, and adds that on 16 May 2024 at 11:43 am HR sent her *"a forwarded copy of a
complaint the Appellant had separately made to the Ethical Standards Unit about Ms Taylor, provided
to her for information only"*.

- ⛔ **Nothing turns on this in the appeal.** Under *Prizeman* the question is the reality of the
  employer's conduct, not its motive. Engaging with knowledge or timing invites motive into the case
  and opens s 32(5)(b) from his own side.
- ⚠ **Why the Respondent led it at all.** The live pleading — the Form 9A of 8 April 2026 — still
  carries *"Reprisal"*, *"actively suppressed"*, *"deliberate"* and *"composite, hostile course"*.
  The Second Amended Form 9A that removes them has not been served. **Part of these outlines is
  answering language he has already decided to withdraw.** Whether and how to serve the amendment
  now, and whether leave is needed, is his decision. It is flagged, not recommended.
- The fact itself — HR sending the complaint to the complained-of manager's line manager — is
  material for the disclosure track only. ⛔ It is not set out, characterised or relied on in any
  workers' compensation document.
