# How the Respondent's counsel would run it — and whether it is a bad brief
> 14 September 2026. Written from Ms Willson's chair (WHS-specialist panel counsel, on the file
> since ≤17 Feb 2026). Internal. Prediction, for preparation only.

## 1 · What she would tell the Regulator first
1. **Concede the documents.** Do not fight authenticity or relevance in front of a Member who set
   the process in your hearing. The thirteen go; the reservations of 8 September have nothing to
   attach to on the operative ranges. Spend nothing there.
2. **Strip the case to two questions.** (a) Was the management action reasonable and reasonably
   taken — s 32(5)(a). (b) Did the injury arise out of employment with employment a significant
   contributing factor — s 32(1), including the pre-existing history. Everything else is admitted
   and is not worth a submission.
3. **Get our own medical.** s 556, or by consent. Without it the only expert evidence is his two
   treating doctors, and Guide 7.6.5 means they attend.
4. **Prepare the witnesses against the record, not against him.** Taylor and Reese will be
   examined on their own emails. Their conferencing notes of July 2025 predate the admissions and
   must be re-read against them. Any reason they give has to survive ¶220 ("a few rostering
   errors"), ¶221 ("not yet aware"), ¶219 ("Chloe and I have run through this") and the seven
   events with no document.

## 2 · The case she would actually run
**The legal line: relocate causation to the reasonable action.** Counsel does not need to win
that the March rostering was reasonable — the Regulator's own review officer found it was not
(¶260), and Reese has twice written that Taylor's errors were plural. Counsel needs to win that the
injury arose from something *else*, and that the something else was reasonable management action.
The candidate is May 2024: the sick-leave process email of 14 May, the request to retract of
15 May (a Code-of-Conduct matter, reasonably raised by a Director), the hours stated on 17 May, the
complaint of 13 May and its handling. Then the timeline does the work for her: **the psychiatrist
referral was renewed on 16 May 2024, the day after the retraction request** (outline topic 8), and
the last shift worked was 3 June. She will say: whatever the roster did in March, the injury
crystallised in May, and May was reasonable management action reasonably taken.

**The medical line.** The 26/10/2022 history entries (ADHD, anxiety); the 16 Nov 2023 note (poor
sleep, no psychological illness); the February 2025 report's "not for medico-legal use" footer;
"preoccupation with the claim" in the treating record; the attendance letter of 8 Sep 2025. Her
IME says: pre-existing vulnerability, precipitated by the complaint-and-retraction sequence and
perpetuated by the claim itself. Cross of Krishnaiah: your diagnosis rests on his account; you did
not have the employer's documents; you were not asked to give a medico-legal opinion.

**The cross of the Appellant.** Not on the facts — there are none to cross on. On four things:
(i) his 2024 words in Review Decision 69983 (corruption, plot, malicious) against outline topic 7's
"never said anything hostile"; (ii) Stressor 3(e) — sixteen hours awake, four hours' travel — his
account alone; (iii) the Member's own words — *"you have as a broader grievance a view that
systemically the place in which you worked had a number of failings"* — put to him as a
description of himself; (iv) the union-delegate and PID threads as evidence that what he was
reacting to was the organisation, not the roster.

**Submissions.** *Prizeman*: the reality of the conduct, not the worker's perception. The review
decision is not binding on a de novo hearing. Onus on the Appellant. Management is entitled to
decide without consultation (Dwyer's own "totally legitimate" at 27:24). Mixed causes: the
operative cause was reasonable.

## 3 · Is it a bad brief? — honestly, in two columns
| Bad for her | Good for her |
|---|---|
| nothing documentary to contest; every submission must be evidence-backed | causation is genuinely contestable — the May timeline and the 16 May referral are real |
| her witnesses are boxed: 298 admissions, ~40 negatives, seven unrecorded events, "not repeated" v "a few" | his credit on his own 2024 words — the single best half-hour she will have |
| the review officer's finding (¶260) and MSH's statements to the Commission (¶¶263–268) are admitted | 3(e) uncorroborated unless he corroborates it |
| the Member set expectations in the Regulator's hearing, and a published decision follows (s 580) | the Member's "broader grievance" diagnosis is quotable |
| the Appellant concedes rather than overreaches — no error to provoke | time: nothing is listed; the IME and the conference come first |
| conduct exposure under s 558(3) if the thirteen had been maintained | a statutory respondent defends on principle and is not embarrassed by losing |

**Her private assessment, if she is honest:** *we lose on the reasonableness of the March rostering
and the conditions of the work; we can win on causation if the IME lands and he performs badly
under cross; the conference is where the Regulator decides whether to find out.* That is the
scenario model's number — outcome if it runs ~53% for the Appellant — from the other side.

## 4 · What this means for preparation — the four things she is counting on
1. **The May relocation.** The answer is already pleaded: the course of conduct, with March a
   significant contributing factor (not "the" cause), and May as the continuation of the same
   conditions. The 16 May referral is disclosed by him; in chief, say why it was renewed then.
2. **The 2024 words.** Lead them yourself (see `MENTION-what-he-was-working-at.md` §8).
3. **3(e).** Corroborate distance and travel time; the register proves the codes.
4. **The IME.** Consent; be heard on the brief; make sure the examiner has the admitted facts and
   Tabs M1–M4, not the SOFC alone (`S556-EXAMINATION-and-the-s32-elements.md`).
