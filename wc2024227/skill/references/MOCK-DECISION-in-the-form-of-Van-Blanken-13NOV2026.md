## Reasons for Decision

### Introduction (follows Van Blanken [1]–[4])

[1] Mr Shepherd appeals against the decision of the Workers' Compensation Regulator of 24 October 2024 (Review
Decision 69983). That decision confirmed the rejection of his application for compensation for a psychological
injury. The facts materially relevant to this appeal are largely uncontroversial. On 8 September 2026 the Regulator
admitted 298 of the 303 facts in Mr Shepherd's notice to admit facts and denied none [9A p 1]. It later confirmed the
documents from which all but one of those facts are drawn. The Regulator did not press a challenge to s 32(1) (A1). The
only matters in dispute are restricted to a consideration of s 32(5).

[2] The summary of the facts that follows does not refer to all of the evidence. Much of it was either directed to
matters no longer in issue or added little to the documents.

[3] Ms Wright and Ms Earl had no personal dealings with Mr Shepherd. Their evidence was drawn from payroll records
already before the Commission [Outline 3, 4]. It added little to the material facts, which were almost entirely
admitted. Ms Wright's evidence that the pay issues were investigated and "corrected" did not engage with the dates on
Payroll's own record, which are set out below (A2).

[4] Ms Taylor and Ms Reese gave evidence of the events in which they took part. Where their evidence described those
events, it confirmed the contemporaneous documents (A2). Ms Taylor denied "maintaining an erratic physical presence"
and "imposing unassessed unilateral directives without consultation", and denied "any managerial hostility"
[Outline 1]. Those descriptions are not pressed in the statement of facts and contentions now relied on, and
nothing turns on the denials.

### Facts (follows Van Blanken [5]–[32])

### Legislation (follows Van Blanken [33])

[31] Section 32 of the Workers' Compensation and Rehabilitation Act 2003 relevantly provides:

> (1) An injury is personal injury arising out of, or in the course of, employment if the employment is a significant
> contributing factor to the injury.
>
> …
>
> (5) Despite subsections (1) and (3), injury does not include a psychiatric or psychological disorder arising out of,
> or in the course of, any of the following circumstances—
> (a) reasonable management action taken in a reasonable way by the employer in connection with the worker's
> employment;
> (b) the worker's expectation or perception of reasonable management action being taken against the worker;
> (c) action by the Regulator or an insurer in connection with the worker's application for compensation.

(Text from the authorised reprint current at 1 January 2025, `documents/instruments/ATT26`.)

### Matters in dispute (follows Van Blanken [34]–[38])

[32] The Regulator did not press any challenge to s 32(1) (A1). Its own review had found a psychological injury and
that employment was a significant contributing factor [¶¶ 261–262], and it called no medical evidence. The diagnosis,
and the stressors recorded at first presentation, were not in dispute (A3).

[33] The remaining contention was the exclusion in s 32(5). The Regulator relied on paragraph 27 of its statement:
that any management action involved in the causation of the injury was reasonable management action taken in a
reasonable way. Paragraph 27 does not identify, by particular, date, document or cross-reference, the management
action relied on [¶ 303]. In the rest of its statement the Regulator contended:
- that the events of 13 to 15 May 2024 were reasonable;
- that the seven-hour break was a one-off human error, that Mr Shepherd could refuse shifts, and that the eight-hour
  agreement applied;
- that the pay issues were remedied in a timely manner; and
- that the leave decline was human error against a background of high work demands.

[34] For the reasons that follow, I reject those contentions.

### Consideration (follows Van Blanken [39]–[55])

*The meaning of management action* (Van Blanken [39]–[41])

[35] Management action "relates to those actions undertaken when managing the worker's employment". Action that is not
administrative "could be operational, in the sense that it relates to the activities or business of the institution".
A decision of that kind is not management action within the meaning of s 32(5) (Van Blanken at [39]–[40], adopting
Allwood).

[36] Four of the decisions in evidence were operational. Each was a decision about how the Switchboard would run, made
for the whole team, and none was a decision managing Mr Shepherd's employment:
- the decision of 18 July 2023 to remove "everyone's access" while the database was fixed [7];
- the after-hours on-call process of 15 April 2024 [9];
- the data-entry checks of 19 April 2024 [9]; and
- the MASPER process of 9 May 2024, addressed to "Everyone" [10].

None of them is management action within the meaning of s 32(5).

[37] The calls that went to the wrong team and the incorrect directory entry, and the reports of them by the MASPER
Registrar and the Integrated Respiratory Service [10]–[11], were not action by the employer at all. They were
conditions in which the work was done. So were the eleven months in which the position remained responsible for the
accuracy of the contact information [6], while the operators could not amend it [7].

[38] It follows that, as to those matters, the Regulator's case must fail.

*Alternatively, if any of them were management action* (Van Blanken [42]–[43])

[39] Even if any of those decisions were management action, the Regulator called no evidence about any of them.
No outline addresses the removal of database access or its restoration, any consultation before 15 April 2024, the
Registrar's reports, or the emails of the Integrated Respiratory Service [9A p 28, C 5A]. On the admitted facts:
- access was removed for eleven months, with corrections depending on two people, one of whom worked three days a
  fortnight [7];
- the change of 15 April took effect on the day it was notified, with no record of consultation [9]; and
- a service's two requests went unanswered until Mr Shepherd escalated them [11].

I would not be satisfied that any of it was reasonable management action taken in a reasonable way.

*The management action* (Van Blanken [17]–[23], applied)

[40] The rostering of Mr Shepherd's own shifts was management action. The break of seven hours was below ten, and
below the eight hours allowed by the agreement on which the Regulator relies [19]–[20]. On the employer's own account
that agreement applies only to shift swaps, and none is alleged [19]. The Regulator's review found the rostering
unreasonable management action [30]. Ms Taylor's evidence is that it was human error [20]. That explains it. It does
not make it reasonable management action taken in a reasonable way.

[41] The handling of the fatigue concern was management action, and it was not taken in a reasonable way:
- an enquiry acknowledged on 9 April went unanswered until 1 May, and was then refused [21];
- the refusal rested on an agreement whose minimum the roster had itself breached [19]–[21];
- the error the Director said the Manager was working to fix brought no offer of alternative shifts [22];
- the toolkit received no feedback [22];
- the decision that his roster "will not be considered" was recorded on 10 May and kept from him [23]–[24]; and
- no fatigue risk management was implemented at the Switchboard before 30 June 2024 [24].

Ms Taylor's evidence that HR had confirmed the agreement continued to apply is not consistent with HR's account of
7 July 2026 [19]. Even on her account, the agreement allowed eight hours, and the break was seven.

[42] The handling of Mr Shepherd's pay and leave was management action, and it was not reasonable:
- the corrections Payroll directed on 3 May had not been made on 13 May, were still awaited on 21 May, were
  "Part Completed" on 30 May, and reached a payslip only after 33 days, and in one case 425 days [16]–[17];
- "Remedied in a timely manner" is not borne out by those dates; and
- the paid leave was declined twice, the second time in error [18].

Human error against a background of high work demands may explain those events. It does not make them reasonable.

[43] The request of 15 May 2024 that Mr Shepherd retract his email was management action. Ms Reese's evidence is that
she acted on HR's advice and read the email as an accusation [14]. The hours he asked for were stated to the
department within two days, and on 21 May she asked him which changes had concerned him [14]. It is unnecessary to
decide whether the request was reasonable management action taken in a reasonable way. As appears below, the result
would be the same either way.

*Whether the injury arose out of the events of 15 May* (Van Blanken [44]–[49]: the Regulator's causal chain)

[44] The Regulator's case is that the injury arose from the events of 13 to 15 May 2024: the referral to a
psychiatrist was made on 16 May, the day after the request to retract [9A p 2, 1.3].

[45] That logic is overly simplistic. The referral came at the end of a sequence, not at the start of one:
- the fatigue refusal of 1 May [21];
- the misdirected calls of 2 to 9 May [10];
- Payroll's direction of 3 May, and its finding on 13 May that nothing had been corrected [16];
- the recorded decision of 10 May not to consider his roster [23]; and
- the directory requests of 15 May [11].

[46] The records made at first presentation name pay withheld and overtime not processed, and the roster [25]. The
psychiatrist records the origin as management and rostering, with pay withheld or delayed [27]. None of those records
names the exchange of 15 May.

*The injury as a whole*

[47] The question is whether the injury, not any one event, arose out of reasonable management action taken in a
reasonable way (Langerak at [86]). The presence of reasonable management action among the causes does not by itself
answer that question (Mahaffey at [57]).

[48] On the evidence of the treating doctors (A3), significant causes of the injury were:
- conditions of the work that were not management action [36]–[37];
- the rostering of 17 and 18 March and the handling of the fatigue concern [40]–[41]; and
- the handling of his pay and leave [42].

Even if the request of 15 May were reasonable management action taken in a reasonable way, the injury did not arise
out of reasonable management action taken in a reasonable way.

*Perception* (Van Blanken [15]–[16], [51]–[54]: what the worker felt, against what was real)

[49] The Regulator pointed to the mechanism recorded on the certificate. The notes record Mr Shepherd's account in his
own words. Whatever words he used, the matters he named were real, and the Regulator has admitted them: the pay
corrections not made, the overtime not processed, the roster below the minimum break. The question is the reality of
the employer's conduct, not the worker's perception of it (Prizeman). Section 32(5)(b) does not apply.

*Conclusion* (Van Blanken [55])

[50] Section 32(5) does not operate to exclude Mr Shepherd's injury, and his claim is one for acceptance.

### Order (follows Van Blanken [56])

[51] I make the following orders:
1. The appeal is allowed.
2. The decision of the Respondent of 24 October 2024 is set aside.
3. The Appellant's application for compensation is accepted.
4. The Respondent is to pay the Appellant's costs of the hearing, to be agreed or, failing agreement, to be determined
   by the Commission.
