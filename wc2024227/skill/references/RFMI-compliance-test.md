## PART A — IS THERE POWER TO REQUIRE THE INFORMATION AT ALL?

**MSH cites:** "the provisions of the Human Resources (HR) Policy G3: Reasonable Adjustment, and
sections 17 and 19 of the Work Health and Safety Act 2011."

| Test | Finding |
|---|---|
| Does G03 confer a power to compel? | ❌ It is a policy. It binds MSH, not the employee. It **limits** what may be sought (cl 3, below). |
| Do WHS ss 17/19 confer a power to compel? | ❌ Both are **duty** provisions. s 17 requires elimination/minimisation of risk; s 19(1) is the primary duty of care. Neither creates a power over a worker. |
| What is the actual source? | The employer's right to give a **lawful and reasonable direction** (cf *Blackadder v Ramsey Butchering Services* (2005) 221 CLR 539). ⚠️ pinpoint not re-verified this session. |
| What is the limit? | **Reasonableness.** A direction is reasonable only so far as the information is genuinely needed for the stated purpose. Beyond that it is not a lawful and reasonable direction, and declining it is not misconduct. |

**G03 cl 3 — the necessity limit, in MSH's own instrument:**
> "Reasonable adjustment **may need to be supported by medical or rehabilitation information, where
> necessary, to either identify required workplace adjustments and/or assess the suitability of
> proposed adjustments**."

**IMEG — the same limit, expressed as scope:**
> information from the treating doctor which "sets out the impact of the employee's mental or
> physical illness or disability on their capacity to carry out **the genuine occupational
> requirements of their role**. **The employee's written consent must be obtained.**"

⇒ **Two permitted purposes only.** Every question is tested against them below.

## PART E — ⭐ THE HIERARCHY MSH WALKED PAST

**IMEG** sets out, expressly, what should be done *before* compelling a medical examination:

1. "early discussions between the manager, the employee and the employee's treating doctor (only with the employee's consent)" — **no consultation occurred**
2. "(a) exploring options for **temporary workplace rehabilitation arrangements such as a graduated return to work**" — **interim duties refused "in any capacity"**
3. "(b) **use of existing medical reports** … already provided to the entity … Consideration should be given to whether this information is current and relevant" — **MSH has held the ECC since 3 July and it answers 7 of the 9 questions**
4. "(d) seek information from the employee's treating doctor … The employee's written consent must be obtained" — **this is where MSH went, while excluding him**
5. "(e) if the employee does not consent … the entity **can ask the employee what, if any, additional support would assist**" — non-consent has a defined, non-punitive consequence
6. Only then, an IME under **PSA s 103**

**And the express prohibition (IMEG §4.1):**
> "**However, a chief executive cannot direct an employee not to attend the workplace for the
> purpose of requiring an employee to submit to a medical examination.**"

**The s 103 gateway (IMEG recital of the Act):**
> (a) the employee "is **absent from duty**, or the chief executive is reasonably satisfied they are
> not performing their duties satisfactorily"; **and** (b) the chief executive "reasonably suspects
> that the absence or unsatisfactory performance is **caused by** mental or physical illness or
> disability".
> Absence "refers to a **current absence from the workplace** and it is a question of fact… It is not
> limited to a voluntary absence such as sick leave and **may include involuntary absence such as
> suspension in the context of a genuine workplace health or safety concern**."

⇒ No performance issue has ever been put to him. That leaves absence — **which is MSH's own act**,
and the guideline permits involuntary absence to count only "in the context of a **genuine**
workplace health or safety concern", which has never been identified and which the ECC contradicts.
