## The architecture

1. **Three stressors, each complete.** Each has its own duty or standard, its own chronology, its own answer to the
   Respondent's pleaded positions, its own closing on 300–303, and its own medical linkage. The machine check confirms
   none now borrows a fact from another; where a fact is needed twice it is repeated and its function named.
2. **Repetition is functional, not cumulative.** The 48 crossover facts do different work in each place. The pleading
   says so once (the "Pleading architecture" callout) and the cross-map records it.
3. **Reasonableness per stressor; causation for the whole injury.** Section 32(5)(a) asks whether *the injury* arose
   out of reasonable management action. Part D makes the Member classify each circumstance, test each that is
   management action, then weigh the whole (Langerak/Carr). One stressor that is not reasonable management action and
   is a significant contributor defeats the exclusion. Stressor 3 alone does that on admitted facts and their own
   finding.
4. **Built from their material.** 298 admitted facts; the Regulator's own review; the CE's letter; Payroll's emails;
   HR's swaps-only email; the guideline they disclosed; and now their own outlines (B.6). The documents they have not
   yet admitted are listed with dates (B.7), all Metro South documents whose contents are admitted.
5. **The medical case is one injury from the composite course**, proved by treating records and the two doctors'
   oral evidence (1.11), with the non-work matters addressed in the pleading itself (1.2, 1.7).
6. **Costs follow** (Part F), including s 191(3) and s 192.

**Where it is still exposed.** Section 32(1) turns on the doctors' oral evidence and their preparation; the
classification argument in D.3 may not succeed (harmless if it fails); the register and call-load facts are yours to
prove; Langerak [86] is unverified in the repo.

## The whole case, in summary

You worked a continuous 24/7 role whose function the Regulator admits was critical to patient safety. From July 2023 the
tools for that function were taken away (database access removed and never restored; the contact book removed) and
processes were changed by announcement, "effective from today", with no consultation record. Calls were misdirected,
including a MET team asking where to go, while the directory stood uncorrected and a clinical service asked twice for
a fix. The manager's hours were undefined for nine months; when you asked for them and for consultation, the reply was
a request to retract, and the hours were given two days later. That is Stressor 1: the conditions in which the work was
carried on.

Your pay was wrong for four fortnights. Payroll told the manager the fix on 3 May 2024; ten days later nothing was
corrected; on 21 May she was still waiting; on 28 May it went in and remains part-complete. A paid leave entitlement
was declined twice on attachments the Respondent admits were there, approved thirteen minutes after the third attempt.
You could lodge neither correction yourself. That is Stressor 2: remuneration.

You were rostered 23:00 Sunday to 06:00 Monday, seven hours, into the first and busiest shift of the week, in a role
where a MET call, a Code Blue and two neonatal MET calls came through the console on those shifts. The standard was ten
hours, or eight by written agreement; your 2020 agreement, on HR's own later statement, applied only to staff-initiated
swaps, and no swap is alleged. You took sick leave the next day. You asked about it on 8 April and were refused on 1
May, 23 days later, on the strength of that agreement. Your roster concern went to HR, was rated "moderate", and "will
not be considered"; the fatigue guideline was sent to HR on 20 May; no fatigue system existed at the Switchboard until
after you were injured. The Regulator's own reviewer found the roster was unreasonable management action, found
employment a significant contributing factor, and rejected the claim by counting. Their own payroll witness will call
the break "a rostering practice issue for the line manager". That is Stressor 3: the roster and fatigue.

On 18 June 2024 the manager wrote "I am sorry I haven't been there for you all over the past week". You last worked on
3 June. Your GP recorded work stress on 28 June and 1 July, certified no capacity, and referred you; the psychiatrist
recorded major depressive disorder on 24 October 2024 and coded it in February 2025. No depressive illness was
diagnosed or treated before June 2024. No alternative cause is pleaded. The Regulator's contention is one sentence that
names no action, and its four witness outlines answer a case that is no longer pleaded, agree with the roster case, and
say nothing about the database, the directives, the misrouting, the 23 days or the missing fatigue system.

Each stressor stands alone; four facts run through all three; forty-four more serve two; the rest belong to one. If
the Member accepts Stressor 3 alone, the exclusion fails. If the Member accepts all three, the composite course is the
injury the doctors will describe.
