## 1. The arc in one paragraph

At the 7 August mention, Commissioner Dwyer stepped through every stressor. He told the Appellant to go to
the Regulator first rather than the health service, to prove the existence of documents rather than argue
their character, to keep to the stressors, and to "whittle this list right down to next to nothing". The
Appellant did all of that:
- withdrew the 64G (10 Aug);
- served a 303-fact notice to admit and a 39-document notice (28 Aug);
- obtained 298 admissions and no denials (8 Sep);
- met every direction on time;
- on 1 October, elected a second conference with a narrow request for directions and a proposed 9A that
  pleads dated events against those admissions.

The Respondent's evidence is four lay witnesses and no expert. Its outlines are tied to the old pleading,
and its s 32(5) contention names no action (fact 303). Of the 14 disputed documents, four were confirmed and
nine were sent back to Metro South, which wrote them. Metro South's own Chief Executive letter, now an
admitted document, says there was no fatigue risk assessment of the switchboard until after 30 June 2024.
Meanwhile the Appellant has been held out of work since 3 July 2026. **That has never been raised in the
appeal, and it must not be.**

## 9. How each party likely sees it now (hypothesis)

**Dwyer IC.** In August, the employer objected to production. In September, its own documents had to go
back to it to be confirmed, his own letter among them. Its Chief Executive told him the fatigue
assessment came after June 2024. The Respondent's s 32(5) contention names no action, and its witnesses say
"human error". The Appellant did exactly what was asked and pressed nothing. He'll keep any view to himself.
If anything, it shows in how firmly he presses the Respondent to particularise its contention, narrow the
issues and consider its position (Guide 5.1).

**The Regulator.** Options:
- A: oppose leave for the 9A (weak);
- B: particularise s 32(5) and run the hearing (exposed);
- C: seek leave for a late expert (hard to justify);
- D: review its position after the conference, narrowly, on the break, fact 260 and the new admissions.

D is the strongest on this record. What would keep it in the fight: the Appellant broadening the case,
showing emotion, or attacking a witness.

**Metro South.** It isn't a party but the evidence is its own. In order, what it would least want:
1. a published decision about an emergency-call switchboard;
2. its executives under attendance notices;
3. its staff contradicting HR in public;
4. internal complaint handling examined;
5. the current employment situation entering the record;
6. a renewed Form 29;
7. effects across the workforce;
8. a broad concession;
9. delay.

Its best moves:
- confirm its documents promptly, without comment;
- separately, in the employment track, resolve the return to work against its own checklist and settle pay.

## 11. Risks and open items

1. **Costs: corrected 2 Oct.** At hearing before the Commission, costs generally follow the event (Guide
   Part 10; reg 132), limited to the costs of the hearing. The "only if vexatious" rule (s 563) applies to a
   further appeal to the Industrial Court. Nothing up to and including the conference carries costs.
2. **Medical evidence.** A report counts only if its author gives evidence orally (Guide Part 7). The calling
   party pays. Prepare Form 32A requests for the treating psychiatrist and GP as soon as a hearing is likely.
   No report was prepared for this proceeding; the treating material was served instead.
3. **De novo.** Facts 260–262 help but don't bind the Respondent (Guide 5.1). Rely on the admissions.
4. **Tab 24** is unaddressed. **Tab 31** (facts 228–231) is the only document with facts not admitted.
5. **Stressor 2** needs one sentence: one comparator, one period, one figure.
6. **Perception (s 32(5)(b)).** The only real way the Respondent gets back into the case is through the
   Appellant's own words. Stay with dated events.
