## What the numbers mean

- **Stressor 3 carries the case on its own.** Even if a Member rejected Stressors 1 and 2 outright, there is a
  better-than-even chance the exclusion fails on the roster alone, and that is before the other two are weighed. The
  outlines make it stronger, not weaker: their payroll witness will call the break "a rostering practice issue for the
  line manager", which is the 9A's own characterisation.
- **Stressor 1 is the widest and the least contradicted, and still the harder of the two to win alone**, because its
  risk is legal (classification and "reasonable in isolation"), not factual. It is where the four outlines spend most of
  their words, and every one of those words is aimed at 1(i)–(k), not at 1(a)–(h). Its job in the composite case is
  to supply the course of conduct the doctors describe; alone it is a coin toss weighted your way.
- **Stressor 2 is the cleanest on documents and the weakest on law.** Their outlines concede the errors; the fight is
  whether a slow fix is unreasonable management action. The payslip dates from their own disclosure are what moved it
  from weak to moderate.
- **Together:** the chance that at least one stressor is found not reasonable and a significant contributor is about
  .70–.75 (they are correlated: the same Member, the same doctors). With s 32(1) at about .65–.70 on prepared oral
  evidence, the hearing sits at about .58–.62, which is where the last forecast left it.
