# The Week She Was Away

*A short novel. INTERNAL, for Cory only. Not evidence, not for service or filing, never quoted outward.*

*This is fiction built on the record. The dates, the documents and every quoted admission are real and checked against the admitted facts. Everything else is invented: what anyone thought, felt, said in a corridor or wrote in a margin. Nothing here says what any real person actually did or thought. The Team Leader, the associate and the Registry officer are invented composites. The Commissioner is written only as a case manager acting properly: he reads, he waits for the other side, he decides nothing he shouldn't.*

---

## One — Thursday, 4:45 pm

The email came in at 4:45 on the first of October. In Brisbane that hour belongs to people who are already looking at their bags.

It went to three places at once.

The first was the Industrial Registry, a shared mailbox read in rotation. The officer on the afternoon queue that day had been doing it long enough to sort by shape before reading. Three attachments, all PDFs, no zip file, no forty-page scan of a phone photograph. *Election and requested directions. Letter to Registry with Schedule. Proposed Second Amended Form 9A.* The file number was in the subject line, in the right order, with the right hyphens.

She noticed the hyphens. Over the years the file had arrived at the Registry in every state a self-represented file can arrive in. In February there had been a request for full-size A4 copies. In June a Form 4 had been sent back because it didn't follow the correct format. Each time the officer on duty had sent the polite paragraph and waited.

This one didn't need the paragraph. She opened the letter first, because letters say what the attachments are for. It was nine pages including the schedule. It told her, in the first paragraph, what was being asked: an election under direction 5 of the Further Directions Order (3) of 19 August, and leave to file a second amended notice of appeal. It said the proposed pleading was attached for the purpose of the leave application only and was not filed.

*Not filed.* She smiled slightly. People rarely understood that distinction, and this one had.

She logged it, marked it for the associate to the Industrial Commissioner who had the file, and went home.

The second copy went to the OIR Appeals inbox, the general box, read by whoever was rostered.

The third went to Renee Matheson. One minute later, at 4:46, her automatic reply went back to Cory. She was out of the office up to and including Monday 5 October. Urgent matters should go to the Appeals Team Leader.

Cory read the auto-reply on his phone, looked at it for a while, and put the phone face down on the table. It wasn't a refusal or a delay or a signal. It was a woman on leave.

But it did mean that, for four days, someone else was holding the file.

---

## Two — Friday: the Team Leader

The Team Leader had a rule about Fridays: nothing new gets opened after two o'clock. But the general inbox, sorted by matter number, showed something that broke the rule before nine in the morning.

*WC/2024/227.*

They knew the number before they knew the name. It was one of the files that came up in the fortnightly list: the switchboard operator at Logan. A long-running appeal, self-represented since February. Two Calderbank offers, both declined. A mention in August that had run over an hour, from which Renee had come back and said only, *he knows the file*.

Renee was away. So the file was theirs for the week, at least in the sense that if anything caught fire, the smoke alarm would ring on their desk.

They opened the letter. Then the election. Then, with the reluctance of someone who knows exactly how long a thirty-page pleading takes, the 9A.

They had expected argument. Self-represented pleadings were usually argument: what the employer *meant*, what the manager *wanted*, adjectives where the facts should be.

Instead they found this, repeated down the page:

> *(Admitted Fact: Form 24, para …)*

They scrolled. And again. And again.

At page six they stopped scrolling and got up for a coffee they didn't want, which was what they did when a file needed more attention than it was getting.

When they came back they opened two windows side by side: the 9A on the left, and the Respondent's own response to the notice to admit on the right. The one with the column of *Admitted* running down it like a fence line.

They checked ten tags at random. It was a habit from an earlier job: never trust a citation you haven't pulled yourself.

*The break between the shift on 17 March 2024 and 18 March 2024 equated to 7 hours.* Their own review decision, admitted (fact 258).

*I find the rostering of these two shifts amounted to unreasonable management action given that it was in direct contradiction to the award and the 8-hour agreement.* Their own officer, admitted (fact 260).

*The implementation of fatigue risk management assessment at Switchboard Logan Hospital occurred after 30 June 2024.* That was from the Chief Executive's own letter to Commissioner Dwyer of 5 June 2026, admitted as to its words (fact 264).

*This new process is effective from today.* Admitted (fact 51).

*Cory's roster will not be considered, however Cory is not yet aware of this.* Admitted (fact 221).

They checked the last one twice. It was admitted. They had admitted it.

Ten out of ten. A pinpoint one paragraph off here, a range drawn slightly wide there. Nothing that said something they hadn't conceded.

The Team Leader sat back.

There's a particular quiet that comes over an experienced litigation officer when they realise the document in front of them isn't an attack. It's an inventory. You can argue with an attack. An inventory of your own admissions you can only count.

They wrote a note on a yellow sticky and put it on the inside cover of the hard-copy file, where Renee would see it on Tuesday:

> *1 Oct pkg — read 9A against F24. Built on our admissions. Pls see me before responding. Not urgent-urgent but not routine.*

Then, because it was Friday and because they were thorough, they opened the rest.

---

## Three — The election

The election was short. They read it twice because it was short.

It didn't ask for a hearing date. It didn't ask for costs or an urgent listing, and it threatened nothing. It said, in substance, that the Appellant had complied with directions 1 and 2. It asked for leave to amend, and then for a second conference under s 552A before a hearing, so the disputed issues could be refined. It asked the Commission to manage the case.

The Team Leader leaned back.

*He's not asking us for anything.*

That was the uncomfortable part. A party who wants money from you can be bargained with. A party who wants a hearing date can be stalled. A party who has made two offers, had both declined, and now asks only that the Commissioner manage the case is a party who has stopped negotiating with you. He has handed the timetable to the Commission.

And a conference in front of the Commissioner, with that 9A on the table, would go one way. *Which facts do you contest?* Five, out of three hundred and three. *What is the management action relied on in paragraph 27?* They looked at their own pleading, at paragraph 27 of the amended statement of facts and contentions of 13 May. The appellant's notice had a fact for that too, admitted: paragraph 27 "does not identify, by particular, date, document or cross-reference, the management action relied upon" (fact 303).

They had admitted that as well.

The Team Leader opened the Respondent's witness outlines from 24 September. Four witnesses: Taylor, Reese, Wright, Earl. Each one read sensibly on its own. Laid against the admissions, each one had to work around an admitted fact. None of them withdrew one, because none of them could.

There was no expert report.

They stared at the list of four names for a while. Then they did something they rarely did on a Friday afternoon. They opened the file's correspondence folder and read their own side's letters from the start.

18 February: *does not accept your offer, and we will be defending the appeal.*

16 July: *position remains…* and then the sentence Renee had added after it went upstairs: *Should our position change at any point, we will advise you as soon as possible.*

They had written that sentence for a reason, and the reason was in the room with them now.

---

## Four — The Registry, Monday

The associate to the Industrial Commissioner arrived at 8:10, as associates do, and worked through the overnight and Friday-afternoon material in order of urgency.

WC/2024/227 was not urgent. Nothing in it needed doing before the Respondent had been heard. But it went into the Commissioner's tray, and the associate wrote a short covering note, which was their job:

> *WC/2024/227 Shepherd v WCR. Appellant's response under dir 5 of FDO(3) 19 Aug. Elects second s 552A conf before hearing. Also seeks leave to file a second amended 9A (proposed, 30 pp, attached; not filed). Leave application: Respondent's position not yet known. Suggest await R's position before listing? R's officer out of office until 5 Oct.*

The Commissioner read the note at 9:40 between two other files.

He had the kind of memory for files that comes from decades of listening to people explain their working lives. He remembered this one: the switchboard operator. The August mention. An hour on the telephone. The appellant hadn't been quiet, and hadn't been unreasonable. He had stopped the Commissioner more than once, politely, and been right to.

He remembered saying at one point, *Anyway, let's not get into the case.* He'd said it because a mention isn't a hearing, and because the appellant had arrived, at speed, at the emergency codes and the doctors' numbers. It was clear he could go on about them for an hour. The Commissioner had kept it out of the mention, which was the proper place for it to stay out of.

He also remembered, toward the end, saying something like *you might be on to something there*. He didn't regret it. Case managers are allowed to notice things.

He opened the letter, then the election, then turned the pages of the proposed 9A. He didn't read every word. A case manager doesn't hear the appeal, and he wouldn't hear this one. But he read enough to see what his associate had seen and hadn't written down: the column of bracketed tags, *Admitted Fact*, running down the margin like stitching.

He thought about direction 5 of his own order, which said that once directions were finalised the appellant was to contact the Registry to proceed to a second conference or to hearing. The appellant had done exactly that, on time, and had added a leave application that the Respondent was entitled to answer.

So there was nothing to decide yet. That was the proper course and he took it.

He wrote two words on the associate's note, the way he usually did, and put it in the out-tray:

> *Await R.*

Then, because he was human and the file had been a long one, he spent a few more seconds than he needed to on the first page of the 9A. He put it down and moved on to the next matter, an unfair dismissal involving a council depot. He didn't think about the switchboard again that day. Not in words.

---

## Five — Tuesday: the note on the cover

Renee Matheson came back on Tuesday 6 October with a backlog and a yellow sticky note.

She read the sticky first because it was yellow, and the 9A second, because the sticky told her to.

She had known since the mention that this file was different. *He knows the file* had been the most she'd been willing to say out loud. She had spent the year doing what a careful officer does with a difficult appellant: answering every email, confirming attendance before every listing, disclosing what had to be disclosed even when it didn't help. She had sent copies of documents that, read closely, did more for the other side than for hers. That was the job, and she did it.

She found the Team Leader at their desk.

"You saw the 1 October package."

"I read the 9A against the response. Ten for ten." They passed her the page with their notes. "He's built it out of us. And the election asks for case management. Nothing else."

"He's asking for a conference."

"He's asking for a conference where the Commissioner asks us which facts we dispute. With that on the table." The Team Leader nodded at the 9A. "And we haven't particularised twenty-seven."

Matheson was quiet for a moment.

"He's asked us whether we consent to leave."

"We'll need a position on that. And it isn't really a position on leave, is it? If we consent, we're accepting a pleading built on our own admissions. If we oppose, we need prejudice, and you can't be prejudiced by your own admissions."

"So it's a position on the whole thing."

"It's a position on the whole thing," the Team Leader agreed. "Which isn't ours to take on our own."

They didn't say the rest. Neither of them needed to. Their own review decision had found the roster unreasonable and accepted injury and a significant contributing factor for three of the four factors (facts 260–262). Their own outlines contained no doctor. And the Chief Executive of the hospital and health service had written to the Commissioner, over her own signature, that fatigue risk management assessment at the Logan switchboard came after 30 June 2024. That letter was now an admitted fact.

"I'll draft a brief upward," the Team Leader said. "Prospects, exposure, options. Put the leave question in it. Put Tab 32 in it if he sends one."

"Tab 32?"

The Team Leader shrugged. "There's always a Tab 32."

---

## Six — Wednesday, 8:20 am

There was a Tab 32.

It came at 8:20 on the morning of Wednesday 7 October, in an email to Matheson alone. It was polite and referenced, and almost exactly as long as it needed to be.

It referred to Metro South Health's letter of 5 June to Commissioner Dwyer, and to the documents enclosed with it under Items 6 and 13. It attached two of them: *Role Description, Manager Switchboard Services, Logan Hospital (AO4)*, and the pandemic-leave sub-delegation instrument, already admitted at Tab 29. It asked one thing: could the Respondent admit, under rule 49, that the copy at Tab 32A was a true copy of the role description Metro South Health had enclosed. It gave a date: Friday 16 October, or before the conference if one was listed earlier. And it offered, courteously, to put the request as a formal notice to admit if the Respondent preferred.

Matheson read it and then opened the attachment. The index page first. Then the role description itself.

*Rostering and leave systems. Staff training. OHS. Works within timeframes. Understands boundaries and when to escalate.*

She didn't need anyone to explain it. Every heading in that document was a heading the appellant could ask a witness to read aloud, and then put an admitted fact under.

She looked at the four names on the Respondent's witness list. Then she looked at the role description again.

It was 9:10. She had a decision to make about what to say, and she knew what she couldn't say: the position wasn't hers to state, and it wasn't settled. She also knew what she owed him. A reply. She always replied.

She wrote:

> *I confirm receipt of your email of 7 October 2026 (below), as well as the email of 1 October 2026.*
>
> *The Regulator is looking over both emails and their contents and will have a response to you as soon as possible, noting your requested timeframe of Friday 16 October 2026.*

She paused over the next paragraph and decided to add it, because it was true and because it cost nothing:

> *I also wish to update you, that I am following up with MSH on the requested documents discussed in previous correspondence relating to the Notice to admit documents and the documents in issue.*

She read it through once, checked the date was right, and sent it at 9:14.

It said nothing about the Regulator's position. She noticed that as she sent it, the way you notice a step that isn't there on a familiar staircase. In February she had written *defending*. In July she had written *position remains*. In August, *unchanged*. Today she had written *looking over*.

She wasn't sure the appellant would notice.

She was fairly sure he would.

---

## Seven — The kitchen table

He noticed.

He read it on his phone, then opened it on the laptop so he could see it properly, then printed it, because some things need to be held.

*I confirm receipt… as well as the email of 1 October 2026.*

She had joined them. The narrow request and the whole package, answered as one.

*The Regulator is looking over…*

Not *I*. The Regulator.

*…both emails and their contents…*

Contents. The 9A. The election. The role description.

*…will have a response…*

One response.

*…noting your requested timeframe…*

Noting, not *by*. But she had kept the date.

And then, more loudly than any of the words, there was what wasn't there.

He put the printout down on the table, next to the pile that had been on that table, in one form or another, for two years. The roster that left seven hours between a closing shift and an opening one. The email that said a process was *effective from today*. The fatigue enquiry that went more than two weeks without a reply. The certificate from July 2024, and the Review Decision that had found the roster unreasonable and still left the claim rejected.

He'd been told once that the onus was his. He'd learned what that meant: not that he had to shout, but that he had to *prove*. So he had proved it, one admission at a time, until the other side's own words formed a line down a margin.

He didn't reply to the email. There was nothing to reply to. You don't answer a door that's being opened from the other side; you stand back from it.

Instead, he did what he had done every day for months. He opened the folder, checked the date on the calendar, and wrote one line in his notes:

> *7 Oct, 9:14: no position stated. Review at Regulator level. Response by ~16 Oct. Do nothing.*

## Eight — Upstairs

The brief went upstairs on Thursday the 8th. The Team Leader had kept it to two pages, because nobody reads the third.

**Prospects.** Injury: poor; our review accepted it. Significant contributing factor: poor; our review accepted it for Factors 2–4. Section 32(5)(a): poor to fair, and only on parts of the course of conduct. Paragraph 27 unparticularised.

**Exposure.** A defended hearing would call Metro South Health managers, to be cross-examined against admitted documents and their own role description. The Chief Executive's letter of 5 June would be relied on by the appellant as evidence. Our review officer's findings would be put against us. There would be a costs argument; the appellant has said he will be represented at the hearing.

**The appellant.** Composed at the August mention, and across the detail of the file. Asks only for case management. No offer currently open to accept or reject.

**Options.** (1) Resolve before the conference, on terms confined to the compensation claim. (2) Without-prejudice approach first. (3) Defend: particularise ¶27, call Ms Taylor, seek no expert.

**Recommendation.** Consent to leave. Admit Tab 32A, reserving the version. Seek instructions on (1), failing which (2).

The manager who read it was not a lawyer, but had been in the job long enough to know what a two-page brief with three options means when the first option is the recommendation. They underlined *on terms confined to the compensation claim*. They wrote *Crown? / advice on (1)* in the margin. They also wrote, smaller, a note to themselves that would never be typed anywhere: *Why are we defending a decision our own review partly reversed?*

They didn't send it back down with an answer. Not yet. Decisions like this travel slowly upward and arrive quickly downward. But they put it on the top of the pile, not the bottom.

---

## Nine — The Registry, again

On Friday the 9th the associate checked the file again. Nothing from the Respondent had reached the Registry. Whatever the parties had said to each other, they hadn't copied it to the Commission, so as far as the file showed, the Respondent hadn't yet been heard.

That was fine. The Commissioner had said *Await R*, and *Await R* was what the file was doing.

The associate had worked with this Commissioner long enough to know something about files he'd written two words on and files he'd written one word on, though they would never say so. *Await R* was two words. It meant: *there is something here, and we will let the other side find it for themselves.*

A case manager who's doing it well gives a party room to reach the sensible decision on its own. He doesn't push. He lists a conference when it's time, and a conference before a Commissioner who has read the file is a strong encouragement in itself.

They put the file back in the drawer marked for the week after next.

---

## Ten — Before the sixteenth

The chapter about what happens on the sixteenth of October doesn't exist yet. Nobody has written it, not the Team Leader, not Matheson, not the manager with the underlined margin, not the Commissioner with his two words.

What exists is what was on the record before anybody picked up a pen:

- Three hundred and three facts, two hundred and ninety-eight of them admitted.
- A Review Decision that found a seven-hour break unreasonable and accepted the injury.
- A Chief Executive's letter, now an admitted fact, saying fatigue assessment came after the date that mattered.
- Four witness outlines that don't withdraw an admission, and no doctor.
- A role description that every manager on the list will recognise.
- A pleading that is mostly an inventory of the other side's own words.
- An appellant who asked for nothing except that the Commission do its job.

And one email, sent at 9:14 on a Wednesday by an officer who always replied, which said everything it was allowed to say and left out the one thing it had always said before.

The appellant sat at his kitchen table that night with the printout and didn't write anything. That was the hardest part, and he'd learned to do it.

The file was out of his hands for now, which, for the first time in two years, was exactly where he wanted it.
