## Reading, with the literature

- Clark & Fox Tree 2002 (*Cognition*): *um* and *uh* are signals of an upcoming delay, *um* the
  longer. The opening and the limb-testing are *um*-dominated (23:8, 16:6); the closing
  directions flip to *uh*-dominated (5:13). Long planning early, short planning late.
- Smith & Clark 1993; Brennan & Williams 1995: fillers rise with low feeling-of-knowing and are
  read by listeners as lower confidence. 8 of the 22 limb-phase fillers front a question; the
  spoken form is *"tell me if I've got this wrong"* (16:37).
- Bortfeld, Leon, Bloom, Schober & Brennan 2001 (*Language and Speech*): rate rises with topic
  difficulty and unfamiliar interlocutors. Opening 18.7 → closing 11.2 is the unfamiliarity
  decaying across the hour.
- Brown & Levinson 1987; Pomerantz 1984: face-threatening and dispreferred turns are hedged and
  prefaced. Moving a self-represented party off an application under a duty of fairness is such
  a turn; the limb phase is where it was attempted.
- Shriberg 2001: disfluency tracks utterance planning. 29:17–32:02 carries 2 fillers in 471
  words because nothing in it was composed on the spot: the retreat restates the opening, the
  enforcement is one sentence, the offer was settled in the 5.74 s.
- Arnold, Hudson Kam & Tanenhaus 2007: listeners use *um* to anticipate new or difficult
  content. The appellant's single *um* at 29:18 precedes the densest content of the hour.
