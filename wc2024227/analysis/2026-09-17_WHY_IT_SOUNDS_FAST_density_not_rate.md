# WC/2024/227 — "YOU SPEAK TOO FAST"
## Measured: he does not. He speaks denser, quieter and flatter.

Same source as the rate/pitch analysis. All comparisons restricted to **sustained segments**
(≥ 1.5 s and ≥ 5 words) so that short interjections cannot inflate a rate ratio.

---

## 1. Rate — he is the slower of the two

| sustained segments | SHEPHERD | DWYER |
|---|---|---|
| n | 116 | 709 |
| **median rate** | **3.84 w/voiced-s** | **4.15 w/voiced-s** |
| p75 | 4.82 | 5.11 |
| p90 | 5.59 | 5.98 |
| % above 5 w/s | 18.1% | 27.5% |

Mann-Whitney **p = 0.019 — he is significantly slower.**

**And he is not more erratic.** Levene test on rate variance: **p = 0.40.** The higher coefficient
of variation in the unfiltered data (0.498 vs 0.320) was an artefact of one-word segments
(*"Sorry."* at 0.3 s scores 17.5 w/s and means nothing). On sustained speech the two are
indistinguishable in steadiness.

**He also pauses more.** Internal pause load is **16.6% of his own speaking span** against Dwyer's
10.9%; 3.2 pauses ≥ 0.5 s per 100 words against 2.3.

**Turn-taking is not it either.** He comes in a median 0.32 s after the Commissioner stops, with
0% overlap. Dwyer comes in at 0.36 s with 0% overlap. Symmetrical.

⇒ Slower, steadier, more paused, and never talks over anyone. By every rate measure the perception
is wrong.

---

## 2. What the perception is actually tracking

Three measured properties, all of which raise a listener's processing load without raising rate.

### (a) Lexical density — the big one

| | SHEPHERD | DWYER |
|---|---|---|
| words | 1,313 | 8,274 |
| **type–token ratio** | **0.286** | **0.156** |
| mean word length | 4.10 chars | 4.52 chars |

**He uses 1.8× the lexical variety per word**, in *shorter* words. Nearly every word is new
information. Dwyer's speech is long, redundant and self-restating — *"right?"*, *"you know"*,
*"if that makes sense"*, whole propositions delivered twice. That redundancy is what gives a
listener time to decode.

Shepherd has almost none of it. Combined with the register finding — **0 evaluative terms in 1,378
words** — there is no padding anywhere in his speech. Every second carries content.

### (b) Volume — 3.8 dB quieter

62.20 dB against 66.00 dB, d = 0.94. Reduced intensity degrades intelligibility directly. The
between-speaker figure is confounded by microphone position, but if the geometry that produces it
on the recording is the room's geometry, listeners in the room hear the same deficit.

### (c) Flat pitch — fewer chunking cues

f0 range 5.24 st against 7.61 st; within-segment sd 2.19 st against 2.82 st. Listeners use pitch
movement and boundary contours to segment continuous speech into units. A flat contour removes
those cues and transfers the parsing work to the listener.

---

## 3. Why that gets reported as "too fast"

Perceived tempo tracks **processing load**, not articulation rate. A listener who cannot keep up
does not experience "this is dense and quiet and unchunked" — they experience "this is too fast."
The complaint is real; the diagnosis attached to it is wrong.

The three properties above are the same three that make his delivery effective when the content is
the right content. **Flat, quiet, dense and unpadded is exactly what the 29:17.88 turn was** — and
that turn worked. The delivery that gets called too fast in ordinary conversation is the delivery
that made a Commissioner stop for 5.74 seconds.

---

## 4. One non-finding, recorded

Answering (first segment after a Commissioner turn) runs at 4.10 w/s against 3.62 w/s when
continuing mid-run. Direction is consistent with accelerating under question, but **p = 0.1186**.
Not significant; reported as a trend only, and not relied on.

---

## 5. Limits

Word-level timings are Whisper-derived and quantised, so the 2-second-window rates are coarse.
Type–token ratio is sensitive to sample size and Shepherd's sample is 6× smaller, which biases his
TTR **upward**; the direction of the finding is therefore not safe from that bias alone, though the
mean-word-length and redundancy observations are independent of it.
