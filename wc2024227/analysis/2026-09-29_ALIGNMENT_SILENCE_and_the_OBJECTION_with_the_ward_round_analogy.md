## 4. The analogy — the three-a.m. review
A senior clinician reviews a code that went wrong. The organisation's advance summary is comprehensive and explains why nothing can be known (logs not retained; questions irrelevant). The operator who took the call brings a heavy file the reviewer cannot read, then hands up two pages of times. The reviewer goes silent — a third of four minutes — reading the pages against the summary and the operator's own request, looking for one thing: did he ask for the manager's "effective immediately" messages. He cannot find it. He does not hold it against him; he names it as the one area and says the other side will have to produce them. Along the way the operator answered "unassessed" with "she wasn't in the room", "managers direct staff" with "patient safety environment" (→ "you can raise that"), and the wrong-ward call with cardiac arrest and respiratory distress (six seconds of silence; returned to forty minutes later as the one thing). The organisation's people stand at the end of the bed; the reviewer tells them once that a comprehensive summary is not engagement and that they will have to bring staff; no one answers. He gives the operator four weeks and the one question. The operator removes the need for the list: every timing becomes a numbered fact; admit or deny; 298 admitted; the CE's own summary disputed then confirmed from their own file; four staff, no doctor. On the day their doctor deadline passes he files the night again: no adjectives, every fact numbered, timings for the reader to add, the "effective immediately" messages first under the reviewer's own words, the misdirected call an admitted fact. The reviewer reads it in the same silence. He will not write the final report; he convenes the next meeting, and now has a question for the end of the bed he did not have in August: on this record, do you still want the review to go ahead.
