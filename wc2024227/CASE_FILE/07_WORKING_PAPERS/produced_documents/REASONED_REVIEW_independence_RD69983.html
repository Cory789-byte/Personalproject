Part B — The statutory framework

The two bodies

9.
WorkCover Queensland and the Workers' Compensation Regulator are different entities with different functions. On a review under Chapter 13 Part 2 they occupy opposite positions: WorkCover is the decision-maker whose decision is examined; the Regulator is the body that examines it.
BodyRole in this matter
WorkCover QueenslandThe insurer. Rejected the application for compensation on 13 September 2024.
Workers' Compensation Regulator (Workers' Compensation Regulatory Services, Office of Industrial Relations)The reviewer. Confirmed the rejection by review decision of 22 October 2024, reasons dated 24 October 2024.
Queensland Industrial Relations CommissionThe appeal body. Hears the appeal and may substitute its own decision.

The office of the Regulator

10.
Section 326 establishes the office and defines its character:
326 Establishment of office and appointment of Regulator
(1) The office of the Workers' Compensation Regulator is established.
(2) The Governor in Council may appoint a public service officer as the Workers' Compensation Regulator (the Regulator).
(3) The Regulator is appointed under the Public Sector Act 2022 and may hold that appointment in conjunction with another public service office.
(4) The Regulator must act independently when making a decision under this Act but otherwise is subject to direction in the person's capacity as a public service officer or an officer of the department.
11.
Three things follow, and they are the foundation of everything in Part L. First, the Regulator is not an independent statutory authority. It is a public service office, held by a public servant, who may concurrently hold another public service office. Second, s 326(4) is the only statutory duty of independence in the Act, and it is engaged here because a review decision under s 545 is a decision under the Act. Third, that duty is expressly confined: it attaches to the making of the decision, and everything else is expressly within the ordinary directable sphere.
12.
Section 329 governs delegation. The Regulator may delegate a function or power only to an appropriately qualified public service employee, an authorised person, or a person prescribed under a regulation. A private law firm is not a public service employee, and no relevant prescribing regulation has been identified. The decision is signed by a Senior Reviewing Officer of the Review Unit, who is a public service employee. The delegation is therefore proper on its face.

The review

13.
Section 539 states the object of the review part:
The object of this part is to provide a non-adversarial system for prompt resolution of disputes.
14.
The word "independent" does not appear in s 539, and does not appear anywhere in Chapter 13. This is a matter of some significance and it is addressed at paragraphs 30 to 33.
15.
Sections 543 and 544 allocate the parties' roles, and they allocate them unequally.
ProvisionThe applicant workerThe insurer
s 543
Right of appearance		 "The applicant may appear before the Regulator in person or be represented by another person at the applicant's expense… The applicant may also make representations to the Regulator by telephone or another form of communication." No corresponding provision. No right of appearance. No right to be represented.
s 544
Information		— An obligation only. The Regulator "may, by written notice, require the decision-maker to give the Regulator… all relevant information and documents", within 5 business days. "The decision-maker must comply with the notice." "The decision-maker must pay the cost."
16.
The asymmetry is deliberate and it is structural. Parliament conferred on the worker a right of appearance and representation, and conferred on the insurer no participatory role whatsoever — only a compellable duty to produce documents. The insurer is not a party to the review in any meaningful sense; it is the subject of it.
17.
Section 545(1) requires the Regulator, within 25 business days after receiving the application, to review the decision and decide to confirm, vary, set aside and substitute, or set aside and return the decision.
18.
Section 545(4) limits extension of that period. The Regulator may extend it only with the applicant's consent (to allow a s 543 appearance or to obtain s 544 information) or on the applicant's own written request. The Regulator has no unilateral power to extend the 25 business days.

The appeal

19.
Section 549(1): "A claimant, worker or employer aggrieved by the decision (the appellant) may appeal to an appeal body against the decision of the Regulator or the insurer (the respondent)."
20.
Section 550 prescribes procedure: 20 business days from receipt of notice of the review decision; commenced by filing a written notice of appeal.
21.
Section 558(1) confers the powers, and it is the source of the hearing's de novo character. The appeal body may: (a) confirm the decision; (b) vary it; (c) set aside the decision and substitute another decision; or (d) set aside and return the matter with directions.
22.
Section 558(2): where the appeal body varies or substitutes, "the decision is taken for this Act… to be the decision of the insurer". A substituted decision is therefore acceptance of the claim.
23.
Section 558(3) puts the costs of the hearing in the appeal body's discretion.

The structural consequence

24. Parliament conferred on the Commission the same substitution power it conferred on the Regulator. The scheme therefore assumes that reviews can be wrong and builds the remedy in. The statutory answer to a defective review is not to litigate the review. It is the appeal.

25. It follows that nothing in this document is capable of being a ground of appeal. On a hearing conducted afresh the Commission is not reviewing the review decision for error; it is replacing it. This is stated at the outset so that the purpose of the analysis is not misunderstood.

Part C — What "independent" means in this scheme

26.
The decision describes its own process, at page 2:
The review is an independent administrative process or a "review on the papers". This means that as the decision-maker, I have considered the material on WorkCover's claim file (S23LW142013) and any new information provided by you or another party during the review process.
27.
The published description of the function by the Office of Industrial Relations is to similar effect: an "independent, prompt, non-adversarial review of certain decisions made by insurers".
28.
The sense in which the process is independent is therefore independence of the insurer. That is the entire architectural purpose of Chapter 13 Part 2: that a body other than WorkCover examines WorkCover's decision. It is not, and does not claim to be, independence of government, of the department, or of the Minister — s 326(3) and s 326(4) place the office squarely inside the public service and expressly subject to direction except when deciding.
29.
Two textual matters must be recorded, both of which qualify the strength of any independence argument.
30.
First, Chapter 13 never uses the word. The object of the review part, s 539, is "a non-adversarial system for prompt resolution of disputes". It says nothing about independence. The phrase "independent administrative process" appears in the decision letter and in published material, not in the statute.
31.
Second, Parliament used the word elsewhere in the same Act, and used it deliberately. Section 490, the object of Chapter 11, provides for "an independent and non-adversarial system of medical review and assessment". Parliament therefore had the concept available, expressed it in Chapter 11, and did not express it in Chapter 13.
32.
The consequence is that the independence of a Chapter 13 review rests on two things and not on a third. It rests on s 326(4) — the duty to act independently when making a decision — and on the structural separation of the reviewing body from the insurer. It does not rest on any object clause, because there is none.
33.
Section 326(4) must be read whole. The duty is to act independently when making a decision, "but otherwise is subject to direction". Parliament drew the line at the decision itself. How the office is resourced, how a document is produced, and what assistance is obtained in producing it are, on the face of the provision, outside the protected sphere.
34.
It follows that s 326(4) is not a duty to prepare reasons in-house. It is a duty to decide independently. Obtaining assistance — research, advice, or a draft — is orthodox across government and does not of itself offend the provision, provided the decision-maker applies her own mind and adopts the reasons as her own.
35.
A further textual point tells against implying any restriction on who may prepare a review. Where Parliament wished to prescribe who must carry out a review, it did so expressly. Section 538(2), governing the insurer's internal review, provides that "the review must be made by a person who is in a more senior position than the person who proposes to make the decision". No equivalent provision governs the Regulator's review under Part 2. On ordinary principles of construction that silence is deliberate.
36.
Two general law doctrines remain available independently of the statute.
37.
Acting under dictation. A decision-maker must exercise independent judgment and must not simply adopt the will of another: Ansett Transport Industries (Operations) Pty Ltd v Commonwealth (1977) 139 CLR 54; R v Anderson; Ex parte Ipec-Air Pty Ltd (1965) 113 CLR 177. The vice is not receiving assistance; the vice is not deciding.
38.
Apprehended bias. The test is whether "a fair-minded lay observer might reasonably apprehend that the [decision-maker] might not bring an impartial mind to the resolution of the question": Ebner v Official Trustee in Bankruptcy (2000) 205 CLR 337. It applies to administrative decision-makers: Minister for Immigration and Multicultural Affairs v Jia Legeng (2001) 205 CLR 507. It requires the identification of what might lead to a departure from impartiality and the articulation of a logical connection between that matter and the feared departure. It does not require actual partiality, and it does not require proof that anyone did anything wrong. Where the concern is the participation of an interested person in preparing material for the decision-maker, the governing authority is Hot Holdings Pty Ltd v Creasy [2002] HCA 51; (2002) 210 CLR 438, in which the High Court held the decision unaffected on the facts but did not settle when such a person may be said to have played a significant role.

Part G — How the findings were reversed

60.
The reversal occurs at a single step. Having found factor 4 to be unreasonable management action, and having found employment to be a significant contributing factor, the decision aggregates:
I note that since two out of the three causative factors amounted to reasonable management action taken in a reasonable way, this means your psychological injury mainly arose out of such management action.
61.
The exclusion in s 32(5)(a) is then applied and the application is rejected.
62.
Two features of that step should be noted at once, and they are developed in Part H. It is arithmetical — it counts factors rather than evaluating them. And the word "mainly" imports a dominant-cause criterion, which is not the statutory test.

63. The structure of the decision is therefore that everything the appellant needed was found in his favour, and the rejection was produced by one paragraph of aggregation. That is the shape of the document and it is the reason the appellant relies on the decision rather than attacking it.

Part H — Defects in the reasoning

Defect 1 — the counting fallacy

64.
The aggregation passage quoted at paragraph 60 counts factors. The Industrial Court has rejected that approach. In Davis v Blackwood (commonly cited as Adams) [2015] ICQ 1 at [23], adopting Q-COMP v Hochen, there is no dominant cause test, and reasonable management action is not neutralised, nor unreasonable management action defeated, by being outnumbered.
65.
In Mahaffey v Workers' Compensation Regulator [2016] ICQ 10 at [51] and [54]–[57], a single unreasonable stressor bearing a sufficient causal connection to the injury is capable of removing the case from the s 32(5)(a) exclusion.
66.
The word "mainly" has no counterpart in s 32(5)(a). The subsection asks whether the disorder arose out of reasonable management action, not whether it mainly did.

Defect 2 — post-injury conduct used to characterise pre-injury action

67.
Section 32(5)(a) excludes a disorder "arising out of, or in the course of" reasonable management action taken in a reasonable way. The action must therefore be the action out of which the injury arose.
68.
The injury is dated 18 June 2024. On factor 2, the decision records at page 12:
In an undated email, Ms Reese scheduled a meeting with you at 10:00am on 12 July 2024 to discuss the outcome of your complaint.

In an email dated 12 July 2024, Ms Reese stated she rescheduled the meeting to 16 July 2024 since you were away on that day.
69.
The operative conclusion at page 18 is:
Likewise, I find Ms Reese contacted you twice in an attempt to schedule a meeting with you to discuss your concerns and allegations, and without your participation in this process, there was no other actions available for Ms Reese to take. Therefore, I am satisfied this factor amounted to reasonable management action taken in a reasonable way.
70.
The two meeting attempts post-date the injury by 24 and 28 days respectively. They also post-date the lodgement of the compensation application and the first work capacity certificate, both of 1 July 2024.
71.
Conduct occurring after an injury is not capable of being conduct out of which that injury arose. It could bear on an aggravation under s 32(3)(b), but no aggravation was in issue on the review. The word "Therefore" in the passage quoted at paragraph 69 is carried, in substantial part, by conduct legally incapable of supporting it.
72.
If the post-injury material is removed, the entire remaining basis for a finding of reasonable management action on factor 2 is: the complaint was acknowledged two days after lodgement; particulars were requested once, on 20 May 2024; and the matter was closed on 27 May 2024 — 11 to 14 days after lodgement, with no investigation, a fact the decision records the employer as having conceded ("The employer conceded this was true").

Defect 3 — an internally inconsistent excuse

73.
The decision excuses the Ethical Standards Unit on the footing that "due to your lack of communication of this information to the Unit, they could not have known this was the reason for your lack of participation".
74.
That reasoning is then extended to the July meetings by the word "Likewise". But the decision's own narrative records that the meeting was rescheduled "since you were away on that day". On the face of the decision, the position in July was known.

Defect 4 — "no other actions available" is asserted, not reasoned

75.
Alternatives plainly existed and none is considered: deferring the meeting until the appellant was fit; communicating the outcome in writing; seeking the treating practitioner's view on whether a meeting was appropriate; offering a support person. Bowers v WorkCover Queensland [2002] QIC 18, on which the decision relies, holds that management action "need not be perfect or above criticism" and "permits some failings, deficiencies and flaws". That is a proposition about whether action was reasonable. It is not a warrant for omitting to ask whether any alternative existed.

Defect 5 — the Delaney threshold recited but not tested

76.
The decision correctly states the principle in Delaney v Q-COMP Review Unit [2005] QIC 11 that actions joined by subject matter, time and personality may be evaluated globally. It then aggregates the three substantiated factors without asking whether they are so joined.
FactorSubject matterPeriodPerson
2Complaint handlingMay 2024 (and July)ESU / Ms Reese
3PayFebruary–May 2024Ms Taylor / payroll

77.
The three factors differ in subject matter, differ in period, and differ at least in part in the persons involved. Whether the Delaney precondition was satisfied is a live question that the decision does not address.

Defect 6 — factor 4 treated as an incident rather than a sequence

78.
The decision finds that the employer "only failed to maintain a 10-hour break… on one occasion" and substantiates the factor "only to the extent of the one occasion". The correspondence of 8 April, the escalation of 9 April, the follow-up of 24 April and the refusal of 1 May 2024 are recited elsewhere in the decision and are not carried into the factor 4 analysis. A 44-day sequence involving several decision-makers was assessed as a single shift.

Defect 7 — the instrument question expressly not decided

79.
The decision states that there was "uncertainty… regarding whether the 8-hour agreement continued to apply" but that "it is not necessary for me to consider whether the employer's stance on its application was reasonable". The question whether it was reasonable to apply a casual-era agreement to a later full-time roster is the manner question, and it was not answered.

Defect 8 — an element of the complaint that does not appear

80.
A full-text search of the 28 pages returns no occurrence of "patient", "safety" or "at risk". The only occurrence of "clinical" relates to the treating doctor's clinical findings.
81.
The appellant's email to WorkCover of 30 August 2024 is recorded in the decision as follows: "You emailed WorkCover on 30 August 2024 and in referring to the email of Ms Taylor dated 19 April 2024 regarding the new process for data entry, commented she failed to consult regarding this new process."
82.
The subject line of that email was "Failure to consult putting patients at risk". The clinical-risk element does not appear in the decision.
83.
The department concerned is a hospital switchboard, responsible for emergency call routing and after-hours on-call contact. A complaint touching clinical risk, closed within a fortnight without investigation, is a materially different matter from a workplace grievance closed within a fortnight. The decision does not engage with that difference because the element does not appear in it.
84.
No inference is drawn as to why. The omission is recorded as an omission.

Defect 9 — a material fact that was not before the reviewing officer

85.
The decision nowhere asks whether a fatigue risk management framework existed, whether any risk assessment was undertaken, or whether the employer's own policy control was applied. That is because the employer's concessions on those matters did not exist until 5 June 2026. This is not a criticism of the reviewing officer. It establishes that the decision was made on a materially incomplete record.

116.
Explanation A — engagement by the Regulator under the whole-of-government panel. Lawful, orthodox, procurement-approved, and consistent with the letterhead template having been supplied. Decision-makers across government commonly have reasons prepared with assistance, and s 326(4) is not offended provided the decision-maker applies her own mind.

120.
Two matters point away from the most adverse construction and are recorded because they should be.
121.

122.
Second, and more substantially, the document contains the concessions most damaging to the respondent's present case. The reasons record that employment was a significant contributing factor, that the treating doctor attributed the injury solely to work events, and that there was no pre-existing condition. The Regulator's Statement of Facts and Contentions in this appeal denies causation and pleads a pre-existing condition. A practitioner experienced in defending these claims would be aware that rejecting under s 32(5)(a) while expressly conceding s 32(1) surrenders the causation gate on appeal. 123.

125.
Two conclusions follow, and they point in different directions.

126. As to the statute: the independence a Chapter 13 review offers is narrower than the words used to describe it. It is a duty on the decision-maker to decide independently, and a structural separation of the reviewing body from the insurer. It is not an institutional independence, it is not stated as an object of the review part, and it carries no prescription about who may prepare a review decision.

127. As to this decision: the process it describes and the process the file records are not the same process. The decision presents itself as "an independent administrative process" conducted by the decision-maker on the papers. The file records a document built on the Regulator's letterhead but produced in the environment of a private firm that is a member of the insurer's own legal services panel, on a precedent containing no authority later than 2009 and omitting the leading authority, and finalised on the last day the statute allowed.

128. Those two things are capable of coexisting lawfully. Whether they did is not a question the file can answer. It is a question the Regulator can answer in a sentence.

  7. What is the Regulator's practice as to the use of external legal service providers in the preparation of review decisions under Chapter 13 Part 2, and what conflict-management arrangements apply to it?
