4. **Permanent appointment.** I was converted from temporary to permanent employment with effect from 3 March 2021 under the temporary-to-permanent conversion directive. Annexed and marked "CS-1" is the myHR Movement Details record.

5. **Full-time hours.** On 31 August 2023 I applied of my own initiative to increase my hours to full time on a rotational roster. My application was approved on 27 September 2023 and I commenced full-time hours on 16 October 2023. Annexed and marked "CS-2" is my application of 31 August 2023.

## PART B — MY ROLE (THE FULL PICTURE)
6. **The importance of the role.** The switchboard is the hospital's communications hub and a critical part of its patient-safety system. In an emergency (a Code Blue, a MET call, a security or clinical emergency) it is the switchboard that activates and pages the response teams, through the Spok messaging and paging system. Whether the right clinician is reached, and how quickly, depends on the switchboard and on the information in Spok being correct. The role is not peripheral administration; it is the point at which urgent help is summoned. An error or delay at the switchboard is an error or delay in the emergency response itself. The Respondent admits that "[m]aintaining accurate contact details for medical staff is a critical function of the Switchboard to ensure effective clinical handover and patient safety" (Admitted Fact: Form 24, Para 8).

7. **The systems I operate.** In my application of 31 August 2023 I recorded, in my own words, the systems I operate. I operate concurrently and in real time a number of separate systems, including the Alcatel switchboard console, the Spok messaging and paging system, Cerner, HBCIS, EDIS (the Emergency Department web database), Microsoft 365, and spreadsheets used for rostering and call records. I move between these systems continuously while answering calls.

8. **The first point of contact.** The switchboard is the first point of contact for Logan Hospital. The switchboard number is the principal published telephone number for the hospital (the first number listed for the public) and it is also the number that staff throughout the hospital use and know to call. Calls from the public and from staff alike come to me in the first instance.

9. **Volume.** I handle approximately 200 to 300 calls per shift, a substantial proportion of which come from people who are distressed, frightened, in conflict, or in crisis. I am frequently the first person a caller reaches in an urgent or emergency situation. Annexed and marked "CS-8" are the monthly statistics ("Individual monthly stats") which I maintain and author as part of my role. CS-8 is a tally of volume only — it records the number of calls I handled and emergency codes activated. It does not record which clinicians were paged, whether a page was received or acknowledged, or whether a page was misdirected; that system-level data is held only by MSH in the Spok system and I cannot produce it. Because CS-8 records emergency-code data referable to clinical events, it is provided subject to the non-publication order sought in the application; the aggregate figures stated in this affidavit may be received in open evidence.

10. **Triage.** A core part of my function is to triage and connect — directing calls ward-to-ward and from one point of urgency to another, identifying which calls cannot wait, and routing them to the correct clinician or team without delay, while continuing to answer the queue and operate the systems above.

11. **Locating emergency callers.** In an emergency, a caller is sometimes unable to tell me where they are calling from. In those circumstances I must identify the source and location of the call so that help can be directed to the right place. Any delay in doing so may delay the emergency response.

12. **Emergency codes and concurrent urgencies.** A core part of my function is the activation of emergency codes. Between 15 and 50 emergency codes are sent out through the Spok system by the switchboard over a 24-hour period, each paged simultaneously to approximately 10 clinicians who together form the responding team. These, and other urgent matters, do not arrive one at a time; during a single shift I manage a number of urgencies concurrently (code calls, emergency and security responses, and time-critical clinical calls) escalating each to the correct clinician, team or responder while continuing to manage the remaining calls in the queue.

13. **Direct relay of urgent clinical results.** A core clinical function of my role is the direct relay of urgent pathology and other clinical results between doctors (doctor-to-doctor, including to and from external doctors and facilities (for example, Hibiscus)) where any delay may directly affect a patient's treatment. I also connect and coordinate with external agencies, including the Queensland Ambulance Service and the Queensland Police Service.

14. **The criticality of that function, illustrated.** The importance of this relay function is illustrated by occasions on which a doctor holding urgent results could not be connected to the on-call clinician until I identified the difficulty at the switchboard and connected them directly. Where the on-call contact information on which the switchboard depends is not kept current, the resulting delay falls on urgent clinical communications, and it is at the switchboard (and on the operator) that the failure first appears and must be resolved in real time. I give this to illustrate the role; I do not rely on it as a stressor.

15. **The Direct Support Officer (DSO) function.** Each medical department has a Direct Support Officer ("DSO") — an administrative officer who provides direct support to that department, including in relation to its medical rostering and leave. When a department's DSO was not contactable, I performed that support function from the switchboard, in addition to my own duties (resolving conflicts in doctors' leave and rostering, escalating matters to executives, and managing intra-hospital disputes) while continuing to answer the call queue.

16. **Roster and leave administration.** My role includes administrative responsibilities carried out alongside the live switchboard work, including maintaining and updating doctor rosters and staffing numbers, and leave administration for doctors.

17. **The daily roster and the Spok dependency.** The switchboard runs the daily roster that is referred to and relied upon across the hospital. Spok pages clinicians by reference to the on-call roster, so the accuracy of that roster determines whether an emergency page reaches the right, available clinician.

18. **Weekend coverage.** On weekends, the management structure that operates during business hours is not present. During those periods the switchboard manages doctor leave, updates rosters, and carries out whatever else is required, in effect performing functions that would otherwise be performed by management.

19. **Complaints.** Complaints come directly to the switchboard (by telephone and to the switchboard email) and frequently take the form of accusations made directly to me. I am able to action and resolve a substantial proportion of them myself. Others fall outside the authority of my role and must await my manager or be routed to the dedicated complaints area, but in each case they first come to the switchboard and are dealt with by me. Where a complaint required my manager to action it, my ability to resolve it was limited by whether and when my manager was available.

20. **Complaints about my own manager.** My manager was frequently not contactable or reachable, and did not action the complaints or issues that were raised with her. Two consequences followed. First, complaints about my own manager (her unavailability, her responses, and her inaction) came directly to the switchboard, so that I was receiving and managing complaints concerning the very person to whom I would otherwise escalate them, and at times complaints that concerned the switchboard itself. Second, the matters she did not action generated further complaints, which then fell to me to absorb and manage. These arose because my manager was not present in the fast-paced environment in which the complaints were generated, and because there was no one else to whom they could be directed. By way of example, in my email of 15 May 2024 (Exhibit "CS-5") I recorded that clinicians and staff from other departments had sought updates on pager statuses and other requests which were, in the words I used at the time, "often left unresolved due to ambiguity around when [Ms Taylor] is available."

21. **My vantage point.** Because the switchboard is the central point to which complaints are directed, I am positioned to observe directly, and over time, both the volume and the subject-matter of the complaints the hospital receives.

22. **The increase over the period.** From that vantage point I observed that, over the period pleaded in the Amended 9A, the volume and the subject-matter of the unresolved complaints and requests coming to the switchboard (including those concerning my manager's availability and the currency of the on-call roster) increased. The records that would establish the full extent of that increase are held by MSH (Items 3(a) and 3(b) of the Form 29), and I cannot produce them.

23. **The baseline (the standing condition of the role).** The defining condition of my role is this: from the switchboard I have a vantage point over everything that goes wrong in the hospital, and I am accused of, and held responsible for, much of it (including matters properly the responsibility of others (the medical departments' Direct Support Officers, doctor availability, the updating of Spok, and decisions made at executive level)) regardless of where the fault actually lies. The volume is constant: approximately 200 to 300 calls per shift, a substantial proportion from people who are distressed, frightened, in conflict or in crisis, while I maintain the systems and the roster on which the hospital's emergency paging depends.

24. **The patient-safety function was performed at the Switchboard.** I was the operator through whom emergency codes were activated and urgent clinical and pathology results were relayed doctor-to-doctor. The patient-safety function of the hospital's emergency communications was performed by me at the Switchboard, and the conditions I describe in this Part applied to that function and to me as the person performing it.

25. That standing condition (bearing the patient-safety load of a hospital's communications, at constant volume, while absorbing the blame for failures across the whole system, and while the support above me was absent or unmaintained) is the condition in which I performed every duty described above.

## PART C — STRESSOR 1: COURSE OF MANAGEMENT CONDUCT (Amended 9A, Stressor 1)
26. **Erratic presence and unilateral directives without consultation [1(a)].** Throughout 2023 and 2024 my Line Manager, Ms Chloe Donovan-Taylor ("Ms Taylor"), maintained an erratic physical presence and imposed unilateral directives without consultation. Those directives were sent from her email account to the Switchboard's generic (shared) mailbox, and were frequently expressed to take effect "effective immediately". The objective record of her working presence is held in MSH's security-access system (Item 9 of the Form 29).

27. **The MASPER directive [1(a)].** The misrouted-call issues raised by the MASPER registrar and the medical department in May 2024 were, in substance, a problem of the roster not reflecting doctors' actual availability — a doctor, roster and coordination problem, not an error of the operators. Ms Taylor's own email of 9 May 2024 records that a doctor's hours were "not provided on the rosters" and that the contact number was "switched off." Rather than consult the switchboard (which could have identified the cause) or check the roster herself, she directed a change to the switchboard's call-handling process. Annexed and marked "CS-3" is that email chain.

28. **I raised the absence of consultation from the outset [1(a)].** On 16 June 2023 I wrote raising, among other workplace issues, "No consultation of the team members or the department when making major changes such as additional shift… commence/finish [times]." Annexed and marked "CS-4". I raised it again on 15 May 2024, and the Director, Ms Tammy Reese, asked me on 21 May 2024 to identify the directives about which I had consultation concerns. Annexed and marked "CS-5".

29. **Roster/Spok errors and resulting delay [1(a)].** When the on-call roster in Spok was not kept up to date, emergency and urgent pages were directed to the wrong or an unavailable clinician. Whether a page reached the correct clinician, was received or acknowledged, or was misdirected is recorded in MSH's Spok system; that record is held only by MSH and I cannot produce it — my own statistics record volume only. These errors accumulated at the switchboard and fell to me to identify and work around in real time. I raised them by complaint; to my knowledge and observation, no change to operating procedures, no investigation, and no remedial or safety action was taken in response, and the failure to address them resulted in direct delay, including to urgent clinical communications.

30. **The vocabulary of the complaints [1(a)].** The complaints about misdirected calls use a recognisable and recurring operational vocabulary — that a doctor or clinician was paged or directed to the "wrong ward", the "wrong location", the "wrong team" or the "wrong doctor"; that a page was "misdirected" or that there was a "pager error"; or that the "list", "directory" or "roster" was "out of date" and needed "updating". These are the actual words in which the complaints are made.

31. **Destruction of WHS records [1(b)].** On 6 June 2023 I recorded a critical operational instruction in the communication book regarding the need to verify and update doctors' on-call contact details. On that day my Line Manager, Ms Taylor, removed the page containing my handwritten entry from the Communication Book (Admitted Fact: Form 24, Para 6); the removed entry concerned the verifying and updating of doctors' contact details (Admitted Fact: Form 24, Para 7).

32. **Grievance and its handling [1(c)].** On 7 August 2023 I submitted a grievance regarding Ms Taylor's conduct, raising WHS concerns about unsafe rostering and fatigue. My grievance was not upheld and I was directed to continue reporting to Ms Taylor. In her email of 7 August 2023 Ms Reese acknowledged that "there was a rostering error that was accidentally made by Chloe with regards to night shifts" (Admitted Fact: Form 24, Para 5).

33. **COVID special pandemic leave obstruction [1(d)].** My application for COVID special pandemic leave (Process Reference 15480560) had to be submitted four times before it was approved — once by text message to my Line Manager's mobile and three times through myHR. On myHR the application was declined twice by my Line Manager, Ms Taylor, and was ultimately approved on the same supporting evidence I had already provided. The myHR records and audit trail recording those submissions and declines are the material the subject of Item 11 of the Form 29, disclosed by MSH with its letter of 5 June 2026 (see paragraph 51); I refer to that disclosure and do not annex it.

34. **Public Interest Disclosure [1(e)].** On 13 May 2024 I made a complaint concerning Ms Taylor and Ms Reese, which the Ethical Standards Unit determined on 24 December 2024 to constitute a Public Interest Disclosure (Admitted Fact: Form 24, Para 20); the determination bears reference PID 24-ESU-1130. I do not set out the content of that disclosure in this affidavit.

35. **Direction to retract within 48 hours [1(f)].** On 15 May 2024, within 48 hours of the matter referred to in the preceding paragraph, the Director, Ms Reese, directed me to retract an email I had sent inquiring about office hours (Exhibit "CS-5") (Admitted Fact: Form 24, Para 21). A substantively similar email sent by Ms Taylor on 9 May 2024 about another staff member's hours attracted no such direction (Admitted Fact: Form 24, Para 22).

36. **Suppression of union representation [1(g)].** I notified management of my intention to become the switchboard union delegate on 11 August 2023 (Admitted Fact: Form 24, Para 18). My intention was also recorded in my full-time application of 31 August 2023 and expressed directly to Ms Taylor. On 18 May 2024 I sent a request regarding union representation to the Together union (Ms Heather Hayes), and on 20 May 2024 Together confirmed it had "passed [my] correspondence on to [my] Health team." Annexed and marked "CS-6". The Union Encouragement Policy (QH-POL-248) requires managers to take a "positive, supportive role" (Admitted Fact: Form 24, Para 17); management failed to facilitate the delegate appointment for a period exceeding 9 months (Admitted Fact: Form 24, Para 19), leaving the department without delegate representation while I was disputing unsafe rostering and fatigue practices.

## PART D — STRESSOR 2: REMUNERATION (Amended 9A, Stressor 2)
37. **Underpayment and the AVAC delay.** Between February and April 2024 I was not paid my correct entitlements. On 3 May 2024 Queensland Health Payroll (Payroll Officer Elaine Grant) instructed Ms Taylor to "submit an AVAC to correct these shifts" (Admitted Fact: Form 24, Para 40); the AVAC was not submitted until 28 May 2024, a 25-day delay (Admitted Fact: Form 24, Para 41).

## PART E — STRESSOR 3: STATUTORY FATIGUE BREACH (Amended 9A, Stressor 3)
38. **The 7-hour break.** On 17–18 March 2024 I was rostered to work consecutive shifts ending at 23:00 and recommencing at 06:00 — a 7-hour break (Admitted Fact: Form 24, Para 1), against the 10-hour minimum (or 8 hours by written agreement, of which there was none) mandated by the Award and the FRMS (Admitted Fact: Form 24, Paras 3–4). Factoring my established commute, the rest opportunity available to me was substantially less than that 7-hour gap.

39. **Coordinating emergency codes under exhaustion.** Under that exhaustion I was required to manage life-safety emergency codes and doctor pages through the Spok system — the very function described in paragraph 6 above. Being required to control emergency clinical coordination in that state, after I had previously raised fatigue concerns, is the conduct pleaded at Stressor 3.

40. **Fatigue-leave refusal.** When I required recovery time, Ms Taylor relied on a 2020 agreement to deny paid fatigue leave, requiring me to exhaust my own personal leave on 19 March 2024.

41. **The Review Officer's findings.** In the Review Decision dated 24 October 2024 the Regulator's Review Officer found that rostering me with the 7-hour break "amounted to unreasonable management action", and recorded that the Regulator was "satisfied [my] employment was a significant contributing factor to the psychological injury". The contents of that Review Decision are admitted (Admitted Fact: Form 24, Para 34).
