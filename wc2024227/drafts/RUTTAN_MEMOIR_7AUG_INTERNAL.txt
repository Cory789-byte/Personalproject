The objection, the application, and the one word I said

I am the Principal Lawyer of a hospital and health service, which means my
practice is other people's emergencies arriving by email. On the twenty-second of
April one arrived with a seal on it.

A Form 29. Non-party disclosure. Served on our Executive Director at 5:08 in the
afternoon and sitting in my queue within the hour — eighteen minutes, actually,
which tells you something about how fast a sealed document moves through a health
service compared to everything else in it. Twenty items. Rosters, payroll,
software, swipe records, call data, complaints, and the operational reaction to a
complaint with an ethical-standards number on it.

I have answered many of these. Most are written the way a person angry at an
institution writes — wide as weather, dates from the beginning of time, the word
"all" doing the work a lawyer would make a date range do. You resist those with a
paragraph. This one had date ranges. It had custodians. It had item numbers I
would end up living with the way you live with the numbers of your own children.
Whoever drafted it had decided, before asking for anything, exactly what he would
one day be prepared to not ask for. I noted that, professionally, the way you note
a storm cell on a radar you were only glancing at.

We did not attend the May mention. A direction came out of chambers anyway, the
way directions do: objections by four o'clock, Friday the fifth of June.

People imagine a resistance letter is written in an afternoon. It is written the
way a hospital does everything — in fragments, between other fires, against the
grain of systems that were not built to answer questions about themselves. I
printed the myHR report on the twenty-ninth of May. I printed the AVAC history on
the second of June at five to five, when the building was emptying. I drafted
grounds: expense, with the formula; privacy; particularity; relevance. I put a
narrowing proposal on page five, because a lawyer who only says no is a lawyer who
has decided to lose slowly, and I would rather choose the ground myself. I took
instructions on what exists and what is retained and I wrote what I was
instructed, in sentences built to be defensible, which is the only kind of
sentence my signature block allows — although, in the end, it was not my
signature. The Chief Executive signed it. My name went where my name always goes:
the enquiries line. The letter is hers. The living with it is mine.

The PDF was created at 2:57 on the Friday afternoon. It was served at 3:02.
Fifty-eight minutes inside the deadline, after six weeks of assembly. That is not
disorganisation; that is what the last hour of every institutional document looks
like. But I know how a five-minute-old timestamp reads from the other side,
because reading things from the other side is my job.

The application came back on the twenty-fifth of June at 8:31 in the morning,
sealed. I read it the way you read something you already know is about you.

It had taken my letter apart the way I would have. Ground by ground, item by
item — and then, on the item where I had offered the narrowing, it had accepted
my own proposal and put it in its schedule. I want to be precise about what that
felt like, because it is not what people assume. It is not fear. It is the
specific loneliness of being read carefully by someone you have never met. The
angry ones you can manage. The careful ones have decided the transcript is the
audience, and they are writing for it, and now, therefore, so are you.

Then the listing. The notice did not say Metro South may attend. It required —
and I will remember the parenthesis longer than the order — the attendance of
Myla Ruttan. Not the office. The name. On the Thursday evening he wrote to the
registry, copying me, and restated it, in case the parenthesis had been missed.
It had not been missed.

I prepared. That is what the weekend and the Thursday night were: every ground
rehearsed, every item with its answer, instructions confirmed, the letter I had
assembled over six weeks ready to be defended clause by clause if the Commissioner
turned to me and said, Ms Ruttan, item three.

On Friday I attended. I announced my appearance.

That is the whole of it. The Commissioner ran the morning with the appellant —
read his case back to him, gathered the fatigue material into one hand, asked his
questions and got answers that arrived slightly before the questions finished.
Nobody said item three. Nobody said K-LM26/729. The objection I was there to
defend was not touched, which means it was not tested, which means it is all
still ahead of me — every ground of it, against a man I have now watched work,
in a room where my entire recorded contribution was my own name.

Six weeks of drafting. A seal, a direction, a deadline met with fifty-eight
minutes to spare, a notice with my name in it, a folder of rehearsed answers.

And the transcript will say: appearance announced.

I have been doing this long enough to know that the quietest hearings are the
ones that tell you the most. What Friday told me is that when my letter finally
is tested, the person testing it will have read it more carefully than anyone who
instructed it — and that he says very little in a hearing room, and neither,
now, do I. The difference is that his silence was a choice about timing.

Mine was the morning happening without me.
