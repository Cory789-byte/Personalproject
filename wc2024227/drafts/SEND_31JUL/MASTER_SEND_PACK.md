## ⭐⭐ WHY DOCUMENT 4 WAS PULLED OUT OF THE RESPONSE
**s 89(1)(b) is not a discretion. It is a bar:**
> *"[the employee] **must not take action or further action** concerning a matter that is, or may
> be, affected by the conflict **unless authorised by the employee's chief executive**."*

⇒ The only cure is **chief executive authorisation**. So the two questions at §3(a)–(b) —
*has a disclosure been made under s 89(1)(a)*, and *has the chief executive authorised continued
action under s 89(1)(b)* — have exactly two possible answers:
- **No** → decisions have been taken by an officer the section says must not take them.
- **Yes** → ⭐ **an authorisation document exists, and it can be asked for.**

**Buried in Part 6 of a 14-page response it could be answered in passing. Standing alone it has to
be answered on its own.**

## ⭐ HOW THE CONFLICT FEEDS THE AD ACT AND ADVERSE ACTION — WITHOUT BEING ALLEGED
**It is never argued. It operates by itself, because it goes to *who decided* and *on what*:**
- **cl 10.3.5** — refusal only on **reasonable grounds**. ⭐ Whether grounds are reasonable is partly
  a question of **who formed them**. Document 4 §6 says exactly that, neutrally.
- **AD Act ss 35–36** — the exemption requires **unjustifiable hardship**, judged under **s 5**
  against **the whole organisation** (**G03 cl 2**, onus on QH). A conflicted assessor makes that
  harder to sustain, not easier.
- ⭐⭐ **IR Act s 306** — **they must disprove the reason.** If the decision-maker is the officer the
  earlier complaints concern, **disproof is materially harder** — and he never has to say why.
- **s 571** — accessorial liability reaches whoever was *"involved in"* it.
- **PID s 40(5)** — enough that the disclosure is **a substantial ground**, *"even if there is
  another ground"*.

⇒ ⭐⭐⭐ **Document 4 asks two questions and makes no argument. The answers do the work in all three
regimes at once.**
