diagnosed with an illness and needs managerial support and reasonable adjustment.

Clause 1.2 of the Code of Conduct for the Queensland Public Service records that having a
conflict of interest is not of itself wrongdoing, and that what matters is whether it is
disclosed and managed. I proceed on that basis, and I ask these questions in that spirit.

2A. WHAT THE "UNRESOLVED WORKPLACE MATTERS" ARE, AND WHY THEY ARE WORKPLACE MATTERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The letter of 7 July 2026 records that the Health Service does not have sufficient information
to understand the nature of the unresolved matters involving line management, or to assess
whether they present psychosocial hazards. I take that at face value, and I would rather close
the gap than argue about it.

The matters concern how the work is organised and decided, not the character of any officer.
They are, in substance:

  (a)  how patient safety concerns raised at the switchboard are escalated and answered;

  (b)  how changes to the work, the roster and the on-call arrangements have been introduced,
       and whether affected staff were consulted before rather than told afterwards;

  (c)  fatigue and recovery between shifts, and how rostering is managed against it; and

  (d)  the ability of staff to have genuine input into decisions that affect the work — which
       the Managing the risk of psychosocial hazards at work Code of Practice 2022 treats as a
       matter of work design and organisational justice, and therefore as a psychosocial hazard
       in its own right where it is absent.

Each of those is a workplace matter. None of them can be answered by a medical practitioner
writing about me. Sections 47 to 49 of the Work Health and Safety Act 2011 require the Health
Service to consult workers who are directly affected when identifying hazards, when deciding how
to control them, when proposing changes affecting health or safety, and when deciding the
procedures for monitoring the health of workers. That consultation has not occurred at any point
since 26 June 2026.

I record, without argument, that the information gap the Health Service identifies is one only
the Health Service can close, and that it is being used in the meantime to maintain my exclusion.

2B. MANAGERIAL SUPPORT AND ADJUSTMENT WHILE I AM UNWELL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When an employee is diagnosed with an illness and is certified fit with restrictions, the Health
Service's own policies contemplate managerial support and reasonable adjustment so the person can
continue or return to work safely.

  (a)  HR Policy G03 provides at clause 1.1 for temporary adjustments so that an employee may
       "continue work during their recovery", and at clause 1 that managers are responsible for
       ensuring that work environments and work "do not unreasonably prevent employment for
       people". Clause 1.1.1 extends workplace rehabilitation support to employees with "no claim
       or entitlement to compensation (health management case)".

  (b)  The Workplace rehabilitation standard QH-IMP-401-5 requires that, where alternate duties
       are required, meaningful suitable duties be identified and actively sought and "made
       available wherever reasonably practicable"; that the line manager "facilitate the injured
       workers on-going connection with the workplace"; and that the worker be informed of the
       appropriate dispute resolution process. It applies to a worker requiring rehabilitation
       for "either a work or non-work-related injury/illness".

I obtained the checklist the Health Service required in order to facilitate my return. It
certified me fit with restrictions. What followed was exclusion rather than adjustment. Interim
duties were declined in any capacity on 15 July 2026, and no adjustment plan has been identified
to me at any time.

I do not say that the Health Service may not seek medical information. It may. But a medical
process clarifies functional limits; it does not displace the duty to consider adjustments in the
meantime. I ask that interim adjustments be identified and implemented under the restrictions
already certified, while the balance of the process is completed.

2C. DUE PROCESS — HOW THE DECISIONS ARE MADE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Support and adjustment are the substance. Due process is the way the decisions about them are
made. In this setting, put plainly, it means:

  (a)  that I am told what power is being used, and who is deciding;

  (b)  that I have a fair opportunity to respond before a serious employment consequence —
       exclusion from the workplace, a decision about my capacity, or the stopping of my pay;

  (c)  that the decision is made by an officer independent enough of the matters in issue;

  (d)  that I am consulted on the workplace and psychosocial matters said to bear on my return,
       as sections 47 to 49 of the Work Health and Safety Act 2011 require; and

  (e)  that deadlines and consequences are not one-sided.

On the last point I record only this. On 7 July 2026 and again on 31 July 2026 I was given seven
calendar days, with a consequence attached. On 15 July 2026 the Health Service advised that it
was unable to commit to a timeframe of its own, and on 30 July 2026 that it was unable to
guarantee my return. I have asked several times for a date and have not been given one.

The Managing the risk of psychosocial hazards at work Code of Practice 2022 calls the same idea
organisational justice, and treats its absence — including a lack of procedural fairness — as a
psychosocial hazard in its own right. HR Policy G03 clause 1 requires that proper consideration
be given to that Code in providing reasonable adjustments.

I do not say that any tribunal has found a denial of fair process, and I make no such claim in
this letter. I say that the process described above has not been applied to me, and I ask that it
be applied now — which is what the separation proposed at Part 4 is for.

3.  THE QUESTIONS I ASK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Section 89(1) of the Public Sector Act 2022 provides that if a public sector employee has an
interest that conflicts or may conflict with the discharge of the employee's duties, the
employee:

     "(a) must disclose the nature of the interest and conflict to the employee's chief executive
     as soon as practicable after the relevant facts come to the employee's knowledge; and
     (b) must not take action or further action concerning a matter that is, or may be, affected
     by the conflict unless authorised by the employee's chief executive."

I ask the Health Service to confirm:

  (a)  whether any disclosure has been made under section 89(1)(a) in relation to the decisions
       concerning my attendance, my pay and my return to duty;

  (b)  whether any authorisation has been given under section 89(1)(b) for an officer to take, or
       to continue to take, action concerning those matters;

  (c)  who holds the delegation for those decisions, and the source and instrument of that
       delegation;

  (d)  whether the advice of 2 July 2026 and the decision of 3 July 2026 were made with the
       knowledge or approval of the officer now determining my return to duty; and

  (e)  whether any conflict has been identified and recorded, and if so, how it is being managed.

I ask these questions in writing because I have asked them before, in my email of 28 July 2026,
and have not received an answer.

If no authorisation has been given under section 89(1)(b), I ask that you consider the direction
contemplated by section 89(2), and that in the meantime the decisions described at Part 2 be
allocated to an officer outside Corporate Services.

I am conscious that this is a request that you exercise a function under the Act, and not a
request that anything be found about anyone. If the answer to each of (a) to (e) is that the
matter has been dealt with, that answer will resolve it entirely and I will take it no further.

4.  THE SEPARATION I PROPOSE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

I propose the ordinary separation of functions. It requires no restructuring, removes no one from
the management of my employment, and requires no finding to be made about anyone.

  (a)  THE CLINICAL MATERIAL — held exclusively by the Injury Management team, Human Resources,
       on a medical-in-confidence basis. Corporate Services would not hold, and would not have
       access to, the clinical material. This is what the Employee Capabilities Checklist itself
       recommends at Additional Comments 4(c): "return-to-work coordination and correspondence
       through the Injury Management team, Human Resources."

  (b)  THE DECISION — any decision concerning my capacity or my return to duty made by an officer
       outside Corporate Services.

  (c)  IMPLEMENTATION — Corporate Services retains the operational role: rostering me, putting in
       place whatever adjustments are decided, and the ordinary day-to-day management of my work.
       My line manager would receive the adjustments to be implemented, not the clinical
       information behind them.

The officer steps out of the decision, not out of the workplace.

Metro South Health is a large organisation and I do not anticipate that this would present any
difficulty.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

I raise one further matter, neutrally.

A decision on my request for a change in the way I work may only be made, or refused, on
reasonable grounds. Whether grounds are reasonable is not only a question of what was decided; it
is also a question of who decided it and on what material. If the decision is made by an officer
within the arrangements the decision concerns, that is a matter which can be raised later, by
either of us, and I would rather it were dealt with now.

I would also note that the Managing the risk of psychosocial hazards at work Code of Practice
2022 identifies poor organisational justice — including a lack of procedural fairness — as a
psychosocial hazard in its own right, and that HR Policy G03 requires proper consideration to be
given to that Code.

I therefore ask that this be resolved before the decisions are made, rather than after.
