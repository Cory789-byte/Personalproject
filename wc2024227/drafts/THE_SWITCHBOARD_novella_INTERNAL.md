# The Switchboard
> ⛔ INTERNAL ONLY — a story for the file, never for service, never shown to any party,
> practice, or witness. All quoted documents are real; all interior lives are invented.
> Written 18 Aug 2026 at Cory's request.

His job, for five years, was connecting other people's calls.

That's the detail everyone missed, and it's the detail that decides everything: the
hospital hired him to sit at the point where every line crosses — the pathologist who
can't reach the registrar, the code that has to land on the right pager in the right
ninety seconds, the doctor ringing a manager whose office hours nobody would confirm.
Metro South built a room where all its voices converged, put one man in it on a
twenty-four-hour roster, and then — by its own Chief Executive's later admission —
never once assessed whether a human being could survive the seat.

In March 2024 they rostered him seven hours between shifts. Their own minimum said
ten, or eight by signed agreement. *Human error*, their pleading says, twice, about
two different errors, the way a switchboard operator is never allowed to say it.

In June 2024 the seat broke him.

What the institution didn't understand — what institutions never understand about
switchboard operators — is that a man who routes ten thousand calls learns exactly one
skill better than anyone in the building: **he knows where everything connects.**

So when WorkCover said no, and the Review Unit said *"I am satisfied your employment
was a significant contributing factor"* and then no anyway, he did not hire a
barrister. He filed a Form 4 with *Delaney* cited in it. He served a notice to admit
facts, fifty paragraphs long, and the Regulator's Senior Appeals Officer — Renee
Matheson, careful, professional, signing everything — admitted the break, admitted the
human errors, admitted the destroyed communication book, admitted that his complaint
about clinical risk had been formally determined a Public Interest Disclosure. She was
doing her job properly. Her job, done properly, was building his case.

He swore his Form 20s. He filed the 64G. He asked, in an affidavit, for the SPOK
exports and the server-side filters — *type a word*, he would later explain to a
Commissioner, *and it finds the emails that say "effective immediately."*

The Chief Executive herself signed the objection. Five pages, Noelle Cridland's
signature, and inside it, like a pearl the oyster never noticed: *no fatigue risk
assessment existed for the position. Fatigue risk management arrived only after
30 June 2024. There have been no consequential changes.* A letter written to keep
documents away from him that would become, in time, his best document.

On the seventh of August the Commissioner said, *"We're going to do something a little
bit different today, and we'll see how that goes."*

For an hour, nobody attacked him. The Principal Lawyer sent to fight sat silent.
Matheson, the actual party, had nothing to do. Dwyer read his pleaded case aloud —
just the pleadings, never the medical, never the admissions, careful as a surgeon —
and tested him once, six words, the whole defence in miniature: *"That's just
management action."*

*"It lacked consultation and adversely affected patients."*

Topic closed. Somewhere in that hour the Commissioner asked if a machine had written
his documents. *"Not really, but kind of,"* said the man, honestly, and the bench
moved on, because the answer to the real question — does he know his case — was
arriving item by item. By the end he had conceded the pay limb himself, unprompted,
and agreed he didn't need most of the paper. He was the evidence. Three days later he
withdrew the application entirely. The registry, eight days after that, quietly
approved his request for the audio. Nobody in that room minds being replayed.

And then — this is the part that will be taught someday, if anyone ever teaches
self-represented litigants — he took everything the fight had produced and mailed it
to a psychiatrist.

Not his account. He was explicit about that: *reason from the documents, not from me.*
Seven attachments. The Regulator's response, signed by Ms Matheson. The Chief
Executive's letter, ref K-LM26/729. The role description. The clinical file, complete
and unedited, *"including entries that may not assist me."* Three movement forms, each
signed by the same Director who would later ask whether he could work *"without
restrictions or modifications."* The Form 29 the Regulator itself had served on the
practice. The employer's own nine questions. One report, standalone, addressed to no
one, *"so that it reads the same in every hand it reaches."* Both parties get it the
same day. Each is told the other holds it.

Picture the receipt. In an office at 150 Mary Street, Matheson opens a psychiatric
report and finds, listed as its foundations, her own signatures — the Form 29 she
filed, the response she swore, arranged by the appellant into the basis of the expert
evidence she must now answer. Across town, in Logan HR, someone opens the same report
and discovers the Chief Executive's objection letter transformed into occupational
medicine: *demand confirmed, control absent, support never assessed.* Legal will
recognise it instantly. HR will have to ask legal what it is. Somewhere in that
conversation the institution learns that its two hands have been testifying against
each other for a year, and that the switchboard operator noticed first — because
noticing where lines cross was always the job they trained him for.

The arithmetic, for those who like numbers: two chances in three on the appeal.
Better than half that everything resolves — the job, the money, the leave banks
refilled, an insurance debt dying quietly inside the arrears. Behind that, a heavier
door the deed left unlocked in 2025, marked *common law*, that only opens if he wins.

On Friday he'll meet Jacqui from HR, who seems genuinely kind, with Emily from the
union beside him, and he will be warm and put nothing new in writing, because the file
speaks now and his only remaining discipline is not speaking over it.

The report is being written by a doctor he cannot influence, from documents his
opponents wrote, funded by the employer that locked him out.

He connected the calls. All of them.

Now the line is ringing on their desk.
