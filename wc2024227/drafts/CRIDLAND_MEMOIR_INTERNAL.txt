Rule 1 applies absolutely. Written 10 August 2026.

---

A chief executive signs perhaps forty things a week. You develop a way of
reading built for altitude: what is this, who has checked it, what does it
commit us to, next. The files arrive prepared, the flags arrive tabbed, and the
signature becomes a kind of weather that passes over documents other people
have already lived in. That is not negligence. That is the only way the job
can be done.

I want to set down when I first noticed that one man's matters kept making
landfall on my desk — mine specifically — and the afternoon I understood why.

The first time, February last year, it was a deed. Four pages, settled by
lawyers, a reinstatement — a switchboard operator, a dispute about an
abandonment process that the document, I noticed in passing, defined instead
as "the Dismissal." My signature went on a Friday morning at six minutes past
nine. Ellen witnessed it a minute later. I remember none of it, which is the
point: it was one of the forty.

The second time, this June, it was an objection — five pages to the Industrial
Commission about a disclosure notice. Somewhere in it, three times, a sentence
saying that compliance would require the approval of the Health Service Chief
Executive. My office. My approval. I signed where careful people had prepared,
because the enquiries line carried our Principal Lawyer's name and that is
what checking looks like at altitude.

The third time, the third of August, it was addressed to me. Not to the
office — to me, by name, attention line and all. Conflict of interest, it
said. Delegation. The handling of his information. Four pages, courteous,
without one allegation in it, asking who had decided the things being decided
about him and under what authority, and whether anyone had considered whether
the people deciding were the right people to decide. I had Mark acknowledge
it within the hour. It seemed the respectful thing, and the efficient thing,
and I have thought since about how that hour looks from the outside: the one
document he ever aimed at me personally, answered at my personal direction,
sixty-seven minutes after it arrived.

And then I sat with the file. Properly, for once — at ground level.

Laid out together, on one desk, they made a shape I did not like: a disclosure
about patient safety that my organisation circulated in a day and closed in
ten months without investigating; a deed with my signature restoring the man
who made it; an objection with my signature saying the records of how we
handled him were not kept; a letter to the Commission nominating my approval
as the gate for producing them; an application before a Commissioner asking
that someone verify all that on oath — produce, or swear; and now, on top,
politely, a question about conflict of interest, addressed to the person whose
signature is on everything underneath it.

My first thought — I will be honest, since this is only for me — was: he
planned this. Nobody walks a file into this configuration by accident. Each
document arrived through a proper door, each one lawful, each one modest on
its face, and together they close like a drawstring around one signature.
Mine. A year of correspondence, and the net effect is that I cannot now touch
his matters without touching my own name. If that is a coincidence, it is the
most disciplined coincidence I have encountered in public administration.

But I have been doing this long enough to check the suspicion against the
mechanics, and the mechanics are worse. He did not route these things to me.
*We* did. The deed came to my signature because deeds of that kind do. The
objection invoked my approval because our lawyer drafted it that way — three
times. The acknowledgment carries my direction because I gave it. The
disclosure was closed under a structure that reports, in the end, to me. He
only ever addressed one document to me in his life, and it was the one asking
whether anyone had noticed the pattern. Everything else arrived at my desk by
the ordinary gravity of this organisation — the same gravity that signs forty
things a week and checks them at altitude.

So: did he plan it? He planned his part — the discipline of it is
unmistakable; no anger anywhere, no accusation anywhere, every document
through a front door, every date kept. But the trap, if it is one, was not
built from his documents. It was built from mine. He supplied the occasions.
My organisation supplied the convergence, signature by signature, and what his
last letter did was simply ask whether I had seen it.

I have now.

The application that would have required the oath was withdrawn, I am told,
after a mention I did not attend. I am also told this was gracious. Perhaps.
From this desk it reads differently: the man who could have made us swear
chose instead to leave the question standing, unanswered, addressed to me,
renewing itself quietly each morning. A deadline would have been kinder.
